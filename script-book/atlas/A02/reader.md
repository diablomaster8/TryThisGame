# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A2: The Monkey and the Monk

## A2-1. Born from Stone

- `a02-01-s1`: Step into the painted page and climb to the stone egg at the top. · _Touch the page, then follow the smell of peaches uphill._
- `a02-01-s2`: Watch the stone egg hatch. · _Stay close to the egg. Your Nubbin knows what hatching sounds like._
- `a02-01-s3`: Look down from the clouds with Heaven’s court. · _Follow the gold light up to the Hall of Divine Mists._

#### `reader:a02-01-page` · [main](main.md#a02-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page painted in ink and wash: a mountain of peach trees, a waterfall, and clouds drawn as curls._  
_In the margin Nova has pencilled: “A very long walk. Take snacks.”_  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): a story that is hundreds of years old  
> _This page comes from Journey to the West, a Chinese novel written about 400 years ago. People still tell it, act it and draw it today._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard that the story comes from a Chinese novel written long ago._

_The ink goes wet under your fingers, then warm. You and your Nubbin step in and stand on warm rock, high above the clouds._  
_This is the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. Everything on it smells of peaches, even the stones._  
_At the very top sits an egg made of stone, taller than you are. Your Nubbin walks round it twice, very slowly._  
_Suppose each trip round the egg is seven metres. Your Nubbin has gone round twice, listening with its cheek on the stone._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiply: 2 trips of 7 metres  
> _Two trips round the egg, seven metres each. How far has your Nubbin walked?_  
> - ✅ 14 metres → Yes. Two groups of seven make fourteen.
> - 9 metres → That is 2 + 7. The trips are equal groups, so multiply. 2 × 7 = 14 metres.
> - 72 metres → That puts the 7 and the 2 side by side. Two trips of seven metres is 2 × 7 = 14 metres.
> _For grown-ups: Your child solved an equal-groups multiplication story (2 × 7)._

_Then it looks up at you, solemn as a doctor._  

**Choices**

- **Knock on the egg.**
    - Something inside knocks back. Your Nubbin knocks again, delighted, and it goes on like that for some time.
- **Sit down beside it and wait.**
    - You sit, and your Nubbin sits. The wind comes and sits too, and the egg seems to like the company.

#### `reader:a02-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a02-01-page-done)

_A wind gets up from nowhere and leans on the egg, the way you lean on a stuck door._  
_The stone is smooth and pitted, as if wind and rain have been polishing it for ages._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-ESS2-1, notice): weathering shapes rock slowly  
> _Wind, rain and ice slowly wear rock away, and that is called weathering. Over thousands of years it can smooth a stone._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child learned that weathering slowly wears rocks smooth._


#### `reader:a02-01-hatch` · [main](main.md#a02-01-hatch)

_The egg cracks with a sound like a dropped plate. A stone ball rolls out, and the ball unfolds into a monkey._  
_He is the grey of the rock, all knees and elbows. He bows to the north, the south, the east and the west._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, ask): turning on a compass: north to east  
> _Monkey faces north, then turns a quarter turn to his right, the way a clock hand moves. Which way is he facing now?_  
> - ✅ East → Yes. North, then a quarter turn clockwise, is east.
> - West → West is a quarter turn the other way, to the left. Clockwise from north comes east, then south, then west.
> - South → South is a half turn, two quarter turns, from north. A single quarter turn gives east.
> _For grown-ups: Your child used clockwise quarter turns to find a compass direction._

_Then he opens his eyes. Two beams of gold shoot up through the clouds, as if he were checking the sky for holes._  
**Monkey**: Ha! Who are you? Are you a rock as well? You’re very round for a rock.  
_Your Nubbin, who also came out of an egg, decides on the spot that this is family._  
**Monkey**: A cousin! I knew there’d be others. Everything up here is mine, I think. You can have some of the view.  
**Monkey**: Not the peaches. Well. One peach.  

#### `reader:a02-01-hatch-done` · [main](main.md#a02-01-hatch-done)

_Monkey’s eyes throw two beams of gold light, straight as rods, up through the clouds._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-2, notice): light travels in straight lines  
> _Light travels in straight lines. That is why a torch makes a beam, and why shadows have sharp edges._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard that light travels in straight lines._

_Far above, in a hall in the sky, somebody very important has to put his tea down._  

#### `reader:a02-01-heaven` · [main](main.md#a02-01-heaven)

_The page turns up one corner of itself, and you are on a cloud above a hall of jade and gold._  
**The Jade Emperor**: Two beams of light from a mountain in the east. Who is making a fuss down there?  
_A herald bows very low and stays down there, to be safe._  
**A heavenly herald**: Only a monkey, Majesty. Born from a stone this morning. He has eaten something, and the light has gone out.  
**The Jade Emperor**: Then let him be. Heaven and earth make strange things all the time. Let him play.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): weighing a decision (a think question)  
> **The Jade Emperor**: I decided to let him play. Was that wise?  
> _There is no single right answer. Think about it, then choose._  
> - Yes, give him room → That is a fair answer. Jade Emperor would say: curiosity first, rules later. We will see if the room is used well.
> - No, watch him closely → That is a fair answer. Jade Emperor would say: small things grow, and so do problems. He did write it down, after all.
> - It depends on Monkey → That is a fair answer, and a careful one. A decision can be wise or unwise, depending on what happens next.
> _For grown-ups: Your child weighed up whether it was wise to let a powerful newcomer play freely._

_Your Nubbin leans over the edge of the cloud and waves at a small grey dot. The dot waves back._  
**The Jade Emperor**: Still. Write it down somewhere. Small things grow.  

#### `reader:a02-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a02-01-complete)

_By evening the stone monkey has learned to walk, climb, eat peaches and argue with other monkeys, in that order._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): the twelve zodiac animals; Monkey is ninth  
> _In Chinese tradition, twelve animals name the years, in a repeating cycle: Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog, Pig._  
> _Count along, and you will find Monkey ninth. Remember this list, because the pilgrims will meet more of its animals._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child learned the order of the twelve zodiac animals and where Monkey sits in it._

**Monkey**: Cousin! You came back down. Tomorrow I’m going to do something enormous. I haven’t decided what.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): big plans and care (a think question)  
> _“Something enormous” could mean a lovely thing or a terrible thing. How would you plan it?_  
> - Ask a friend what they think first → That is a fair answer. Monkey could use a friend like that.
> - Try it and see → That is a fair answer. Trying teaches a lot, though, as we will see, it also gets Monkey into trouble.
> _For grown-ups: Your child thought about how to plan something ambitious._

_He gives your Nubbin a peach that is mostly bite marks. Your Nubbin holds it like treasure._  
_Nova has drawn a tiny peach in the margin. Now a tiny ink monkey sits beside it, eating it._  
_Far above, someone important is still wondering what, exactly, he has just seen._  
_The page rustles softly, as if it too would like to know what happens next._  

### Glossary

- **weathering**: The slow wearing away of rock by wind, rain and ice.
- **herald**: A messenger who brings important news.

## A2-2. The Water-Curtain Cave

- `a02-02-s1`: Follow the stream up to its waterfall. · _Walk up beside the stream with the monkeys._
- `a02-02-s2`: Leap through the waterfall and look behind it. · _Dive after Monkey. Water is not a wall._
- `a02-02-s3`: Sit at the feast in the cave. · _Find a seat near Monkey in the cave._

#### `reader:a02-02-stream` · [main](main.md#a02-02-stream)

_A hot afternoon. The monkeys splash in the stream until somebody asks where all the water comes from._  
_Nobody knows, so everybody goes to look. You and your Nubbin go too, because everybody means everybody._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): the water cycle feeds streams  
> _The monkeys ask where the stream’s water comes from. What is the best answer?_  
> - ✅ Rain falls on the mountain, runs downhill, and later rises again as clouds → Yes. Rain feeds streams, streams reach the sea, the sun lifts water into clouds, and the cycle goes round.
> - A spring that never stops, with no rain at all → Springs are real, but they too are fed by rain that soaked into the ground. The whole cycle is rain, runoff, sea, vapour and cloud.
> _For grown-ups: Your child described where stream water comes from and how it cycles._

_The stream ends at a waterfall that hangs down the cliff like a white curtain. Behind it, nobody can see anything._  
**An old gibbon**: Whoever goes through that curtain and comes back with all their fur on, we make them king.  
_Every monkey looks at its own feet. A few look at their neighbours’ feet, which is a sort of suggestion._  
**Monkey**: I’ll go! I was born from a rock. Rocks don’t mind water.  
_Your Nubbin steps up beside him, quite serious about it. It has always liked a waterfall._  

**Choices**

- **Tell them both to be careful.**
    - **Monkey**: Careful is for monkeys who aren’t going to be king.
- **Offer to count them in.**
    - **Monkey**: Yes! Count. Kings should start on a number.
    - You count to three. Monkey goes on two.

#### `reader:a02-02-stream-done` · [main](main.md#a02-02-stream-done)

_He crouches, shuts his eyes tight, and leaps._  
_Just before he goes, the curtain of water swings in the wind._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): multiple-meaning words: curtain  
> _A curtain usually hangs at a window. What does “curtain” mean for a waterfall?_  
> - ✅ Water hanging down in a sheet that hides what is behind → Yes. The word is borrowed because the water looks like fabric.
> - A piece of cloth sewn onto the cliff → It is not real cloth; it is a comparison. The water hangs down like a curtain.
> _For grown-ups: Your child worked out a second meaning of "curtain" from context._


#### `reader:a02-02-leap` · [main](main.md#a02-02-leap)

_Your Nubbin dives after him, and you follow your Nubbin. The water thunders on your head, and then it stops._  
_Behind the curtain it is dry. An iron bridge crosses to a cave with stone beds, stone pots and stone bowls._  
_Over the doorway someone has carved the cave a name, long ago, in tidy characters._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, notice): Chinese writing uses characters  
> _Chinese is written in characters. Each one stands for a word or a part of a word, and a name can be carved in just a few of them._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard that Chinese is written with characters._

**Monkey**: Nobody lives here! It’s got beds. It’s got pots. It’s got a front door made of waterfall!  
_Suppose there are four rows of stone beds, with six beds in each row. Your Nubbin tries them all in turn._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiply: 4 rows of 6  
> _Four rows of stone beds, six in each row. How many beds does your Nubbin have to try?_  
> - ✅ 24 → Yes. Four groups of six: 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24.
> - 10 → That adds 4 + 6. We need four equal groups of six. 4 × 6 = 24.
> _For grown-ups: Your child used equal groups to multiply (4 × 6)._

_It finds every one of them exactly right._  
**Monkey**: Wait here. If I come back alone they’ll say I made it up.  

#### `reader:a02-02-leap-done` · [main](main.md#a02-02-leap-done)

_He shoots back out through the curtain, shouting. A moment later, a lot of wet monkeys shoot in._  
_The roar of the waterfall shakes the cave walls so that you can feel it in your teeth._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): multiple-meaning words: shoot  
> _Monkey “shoots” out, and the wet monkeys “shoot” in. What does shoot mean here?_  
> - ✅ To move very fast → Yes. Here shoot means to move very fast, like a shooting star.
> - A new green stem on a plant → That is another meaning: a plant’s new shoot. Here the monkeys are moving very fast, so shoot means to rush.
> _For grown-ups: Your child used context to choose the right meaning of "shoot"._


#### `reader:a02-02-king` · [main](main.md#a02-02-king)

_They make him king, as promised. He takes the name Handsome Monkey King, and nobody dares laugh._  
_The page turns a few years, as easily as a page. The feasts go on and on, and then, one night, the king cries._  
**Monkey**: One day I’ll be old. One day I’ll stop. All this, and one day it won’t be mine.  
_The cave goes very quiet. Your Nubbin climbs into his lap, which is all it knows to do._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, notice): every living thing has a life cycle  
> _Every living thing has a life cycle: it begins, grows, may have young, grows old, and ends._  
> _Trees, monkeys and people all do. It is a hard thing to feel, and it is allowed to make you sad._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child learned that all living things have life cycles, and that sadness about it is allowed._

**An old gibbon**: There are some who never grow old, they say. Buddhas, immortals and sages. They live far off, among people.  
**Monkey**: Then I’ll go and find them. I’ll learn it. I’ll learn not to stop.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): fear of growing old (a think question)  
> _Monkey is afraid of growing old and stopping. Is that fear helpful, or does it just hurt?_  
> _There is no single right answer. Think about it, then choose._  
> - It can push us to live well → That is a fair answer. Many people feel that a short time makes each day matter.
> - It mostly just hurts → That is a fair answer. Fear can crowd out the present, and a friend in your lap helps.
> - Both, depending on the day → That is a fair answer. A feeling can be useful and heavy at the same time.
> _For grown-ups: Your child reflected on a hard feeling about time and ageing._


**Choices**

- **Tell him growing old isn’t the worst thing.**
    - **Monkey**: You say that because you aren’t a king.
    - He keeps your Nubbin on his lap, though.
- **Tell him to go and find out.**
    - **Monkey**: Yes. That’s the right answer. Cousin’s keeper gives the right answers.

#### `reader:a02-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a02-02-complete)

_In the morning the monkeys build him a raft and pile it with fruit. They wave until he is a dot._  
_You and your Nubbin are on the raft too. It seems rude to wave from the shore._  
**Monkey**: I’ll be back. When I come back, I won’t ever have to stop.  
_Suppose the monkeys loaded five baskets with twelve fruits in each. Your Nubbin counts them twice, to be sure._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiply: 5 baskets of 12  
> _Five baskets with twelve fruits in each. How many pieces of fruit are on the raft?_  
> - ✅ 60 → Yes. 5 × 12 = 60. Five tens and five twos: 50 + 10.
> - 17 → That adds 5 + 12. Five equal baskets means we multiply. 5 × 12 = 60.
> _For grown-ups: Your child multiplied 5 × 12._

_Years go by on the water, as fast as you can turn a page. He learns to wear clothes and to walk like a person._  
_Behind them, the waterfall swings shut like a curtain, and the cave goes back to being quiet._  

### Glossary

- **immortal**: Someone who never dies or never grows old, in stories.

## A2-3. Lessons in Shape-Changing

- `a02-03-s1`: Visit Monkey’s teacher in his cave. · _Climb the quiet mountain with Monkey._
- `a02-03-s2`: Wait on the back step through the night. · _Sit outside the back door and keep still._
- `a02-03-s3`: Watch Monkey show off under the pines. · _Stand in the yard and watch._

#### `reader:a02-03-lesson` · [main](main.md#a02-03-lesson)

_High on a quiet mountain there is a cave, and in the cave a teacher with a great many students._  
**Master Subhuti**: A monkey. Then you shall have a name: Sun Wukong, Awake to Emptiness. It suits you. You are mostly noise.  
_The page turns seven years of sweeping floors. Then the teacher offers Monkey a choice of lessons._  
**Master Subhuti**: I could teach you to see fortunes. To sit still. To make medicines. To read the old books.  
**Monkey**: Will any of them make me live for ever?  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): choosing a goal (a think question)  
> _Monkey turns down every lesson except the one that might make him live for ever. Was that a wise way to choose?_  
> _There is no single right answer. Think about it, then choose._  
> - Yes, he knew what he wanted → That is a fair answer. A clear goal can stop you wasting years.
> - No, the other lessons had value → That is a fair answer. Patience and medicine might have helped him more than he knew.
> - Both are true → That is a fair answer. A single goal gives focus, and it can also make you miss other good things.
> _For grown-ups: Your child thought about choosing a goal and what it might cost._

**Master Subhuti**: No.  
**Monkey**: Then no, thank you.  
_The teacher climbs down from his seat, taps Monkey three times on the head with his ruler, and walks out with his hands behind his back._  
_The other students are furious. Monkey is grinning from ear to ear._  

**Choices**

- **Ask Monkey why he’s grinning.**
    - **Monkey**: Three taps: come at the third watch of the night. Hands behind his back: use the back door.
    - **Monkey**: He’s going to teach me in secret. Don’t tell the others. Don’t tell Cousin, it can’t keep a secret.
- **Work it out for yourself.**
    - Three taps, hands behind the back. You catch Monkey’s eye, and he winks.

#### `reader:a02-03-night` · [main](main.md#a02-03-night)

_At the third watch the back door is open a crack, and Monkey slips through it. You sit outside on the step._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals: the five watches of the night  
> _In old China the night, from about 7 in the evening to 5 in the morning, was split into five equal watches. How long is one watch?_  
> - ✅ 2 hours → Yes. Ten hours shared into five equal parts is two hours each. The third watch is the middle of the night.
> - 5 hours → Five is the number of watches. Share the ten hours into five equal parts instead. 10 ÷ 5 = 2 hours.
> _For grown-ups: Your child divided a time span into equal parts (10 hours ÷ 5)._

_Your Nubbin falls asleep against the door almost at once. It snores in a small, polite way._  
_Inside, the teacher talks for a long time in a low voice. You don’t hear the words, and you aren’t meant to._  
_The page turns three more years of nights. Then one evening Monkey comes out walking a foot above the ground._  
**Monkey**: Seventy-two changes! I can be anything. And look, look at this.  
_He does a somersault on a cloud, and is gone. A moment later he is back, a little out of breath._  
**Monkey**: A hundred and eight thousand li in one go! Cousin, did you see?  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, 4.MD.A.1, notice): the li and a big journey  
> _A li is an old Chinese unit of distance. Today it is taken as half a kilometre (in the old days it varied)._  
> _So 108,000 li is 54,000 kilometres, and the Earth is only about 40,000 kilometres round._  
> _Monkey claims one somersault goes further than all the way round the world._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child converted an old unit of distance and compared it with the size of the Earth._


#### `reader:a02-03-pine` · [main](main.md#a02-03-pine)

_The other students have heard rumours. They gather round Monkey under the pines, all asking at once._  
**Monkey**: A pine tree? Easy.  
_There is a pop, and in Monkey’s place stands a pine tree, very proud of itself. The students cheer._  
_Your Nubbin stands beside it with both arms up, very still. It is being a small pine tree._  

> **Lesson** (art, notice): shadow puppets: light, a screen, and shadows  
> _Monkey turning into a pine is a favourite scene in Chinese shadow-puppet plays._  
> _Flat figures are held between a lamp and a white screen, and the audience sees only their shadows._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard how shadow-puppet theatre works._


> **Lesson** (science, 5-ESS1-2, ask): shadow size depends on distance from the lamp  
> _A puppeteer wants a bigger shadow on the screen. What should she do with the puppet?_  
> - ✅ Move it closer to the lamp, away from the screen → Yes. The closer a puppet is to the light, the more of the light it blocks, so the bigger its shadow.
> - Move it closer to the screen → That makes the shadow sharper and smaller. To make it bigger, move it towards the lamp.
> _For grown-ups: Your child predicted how moving a puppet changes the size of its shadow._

**Master Subhuti**: Who is shouting in my yard?  
_The pine tree turns back into a monkey, quite quickly._  
**Master Subhuti**: You show off. A gift you show off makes other people want it, and then they come for it. And for you.  
**Master Subhuti**: Go home. And never tell anyone who taught you. Not one word.  

**Choices**

- **Tell the teacher Monkey meant no harm.**
    - **Master Subhuti**: I know. That is the trouble with him. He never means any.
- **Stay out of it.**
    - You stay out of it. Your Nubbin, still being a pine tree, stays out of it too.

#### `reader:a02-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a02-03-complete)

_Monkey bows to his teacher, properly, all the way to the floor. For once he doesn’t say anything clever._  
**Monkey**: Hold on to my tail, Cousin’s keeper. I know a quick way home.  
_One somersault, and the world rolls under you like a rug being shaken. Then there are peaches again._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): similes and metaphors  
> _“The world rolls under you like a rug being shaken.” Is that a simile or a metaphor?_  
> - ✅ A simile, because it uses “like” → Yes. A simile compares two things using “like” or “as”.
> - A metaphor → A metaphor says one thing is another. This one says it is like another. So it is a simile.
> _For grown-ups: Your child told a simile from a metaphor._

_Your Nubbin tumbles off the cloud and lies on the grass, beaming. It would like to do that again, please._  
_Far behind you, a teacher in a cave sighs, smiles, and goes back to his books._  
_He says nothing to the others, and nobody asks._  

### Glossary

- **watch**: In old China, one of the five parts into which the night was divided.

## A2-4. Havoc in Heaven

- `a02-04-s1`: Visit the heavenly stables. · _Go up to Heaven with Monkey._
- `a02-04-s2`: Look at the peach trees in the garden. · _Walk slowly. Your Nubbin is watching the peaches._
- `a02-04-s3`: Watch what happens at the banquet. · _Follow Monkey to the Jade Pool._

#### `reader:a02-04-stables` · [main](main.md#a02-04-stables)

_Since the teacher, Monkey has been busy. He has an iron staff from the sea-dragons, and he has rubbed his name out of the Book of Life and Death._  
_Heaven decides it would be simpler to give him a job. So up you all go._  
**Monkey**: Keeper of the Heavenly Horses! A grand title. They must think a lot of me.  
_He is very good with the horses. They grow fat and shiny, and your Nubbin rides one round the paddock._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): a horse’s eyes and senses  
> _A horse’s eyes sit on the sides of its head, so it can see almost all the way round without turning._  
> _That is a useful sense for an animal that is hunted._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child learned how a horse’s eyes help it survive._

**Monkey**: Wait. What rank is Keeper of the Horses, exactly?  
_A stable hand tells him: it is the lowest job in Heaven. Monkey throws down his hat and goes straight home._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): does a title matter? (a think question)  
> _Monkey storms off because the job’s rank is low, even though he was good at it. Does a title matter more than the work?_  
> _There is no single right answer. Think about it, then choose._  
> - The work matters more → That is a fair answer. The horses were happy, and he was doing it well.
> - Respect matters too → That is a fair answer. Feeling valued helps people do their best.
> - A bit of both → That is a fair answer. A good job and fair respect both count.
> _For grown-ups: Your child weighed rank against the value of doing good work._

_Heaven sighs, and tries again: Great Sage Equal to Heaven. A big title with no work at all._  

#### `reader:a02-04-stables-done` · [main](main.md#a02-04-stables-done)

**The Jade Emperor**: Give him the Peach Garden to look after. It will keep him busy and out of trouble.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): a character’s motive  
> _Why does the Jade Emperor choose the Peach Garden for Monkey?_  
> - ✅ He hopes a job will keep Monkey busy and out of trouble → Yes. That is his motive. The ministers, who know about peaches and monkeys, are not so sure.
> - He wants Monkey to eat the peaches → No. He means it as a safe task. He does not see the problem yet. The ministers do.
> _For grown-ups: Your child read a character’s motive from what he says._

_Several ministers look at each other and say nothing, which says a great deal._  

#### `reader:a02-04-garden` · [main](main.md#a02-04-garden)

**The garden spirit**: Three thousand six hundred trees, Great Sage. These ripen every three thousand years. These, every six thousand.  
**The garden spirit**: And these at the back, every nine thousand. Eat one, and you live as long as heaven and earth.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): divide: 3,600 trees in three equal groups  
> _The trees are in three groups of equal size, each ripening on a different cycle. If there are 3,600 trees in all, how many are in each group?_  
> - ✅ 1,200 → Yes. 3,600 ÷ 3 = 1,200. Think 36 hundreds shared by three: 12 hundreds.
> - 1,800 → That is half of 3,600. We need a third. 36 ÷ 3 = 12, so 3,600 ÷ 3 = 1,200.
> _For grown-ups: Your child divided 3,600 by 3._

_Your Nubbin has gone very still. It is looking at the peaches the way it looks at you at teatime._  
**Monkey**: Thank you. You may go. I shall inspect the trees alone. Very carefully.  
_The spirit goes. Monkey shrinks to the size of your thumb, climbs the oldest tree, and eats the best peach on it._  
_Then the next best. Then he falls asleep in a fork of the branches, round as a plum._  
_A ripe peach drops into the grass at your Nubbin’s feet. It glows faintly, like a lamp turned low._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-LS2-2, notice): fruit helps seeds travel  
> _A peach has one large stone, with a seed inside._  
> _Fruit is tasty so animals will carry the stone away and drop it somewhere new. That is one way plants spread._

**The Buddha**: You have come a long way. Rest a little, and then take what you came for.  
_Disciples bring out scrolls, bundle after bundle. You load them onto the horse and the pole with Sandy, carefully._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): translating and copying texts  
> _The real Xuanzang spent the rest of his life translating the Buddhist texts he brought home into Chinese._  
> _Before printing was common, texts were copied by hand, one careful scroll at a time._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard how the real monk’s texts were translated and copied._

_Halfway down the mountain a wind tears open a bundle. The pages inside are blank, every one._  
**Tang Sanzang**: Blank? After all this? Did we do something wrong?  
_You go back up. The Buddha is not cross, and not surprised._  
**The Buddha**: The blank ones are the truest. But in the East, people need words. So: words.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.2, ask): words and silence (a think question)  
> _The Buddha says the blank scrolls are truest, but that people need words. What can words give that silence cannot, and what can silence give that words cannot?_  
> _There is no single right answer. Think about it, then choose._  
> - Words let ideas travel across distance and time → That is a fair answer. A written page can reach someone far away, long after it was written.
> - Silence lets us notice what words cannot say → That is a fair answer. Some things are felt before they can be named.
> - We need both → That is a fair answer. The Buddha seems to say just that.
> _For grown-ups: Your child reflected on what words and silence each offer._

_As thanks, the monk gives the golden bowl the emperor gave him, which is all he has. The new scrolls are full of writing._  

#### `reader:a02-18-complete` · [main](main.md#a02-18-complete)

_Five thousand and forty-eight rolls of scripture, tied up in neat bundles. You and Sandy take the pole between you._  
**Tang Sanzang**: We have them. We actually have them.  
**Monkey**: Told you. India by lunch.  
**Pigsy**: It was fourteen years.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): compare: rolls and days  
> _Fourteen years is about 5,110 days, and the pilgrims brought back 5,048 rolls. Are there more rolls than days, or fewer?_  
> - ✅ Fewer: 5,048 is less than 5,110 → Yes. That is nearly one roll for every day of the trip.
> - More: 5,048 is bigger → Compare digit by digit: 5,0 is below 5,1. 5,048 is less than 5,110.
> _For grown-ups: Your child compared two four-digit numbers._

**Monkey**: A very long lunch.  
_The mountain behind you glows in the late sun, and the scrolls hang from the pole like a great, patient bird._

## A2-19. The Eighty-First Hardship

- `a02-19-s2`: Lay the scrolls on the rock to dry. · _Set them out in the sun, one by one._

#### `reader:a02-19-turtle` · [main](main.md#a02-19-turtle)

_Far away, Guanyin is reading a list of every hardship the pilgrims met. There were eighty, and there ought to be eighty-one._  
**Guanyin**: One more, then. A gentle one.  
_At the River of Heaven, the old white turtle who carried you all across on the way out is waiting, smiling._  
_The page skipped that crossing years ago, between one chapter and the next. The turtle has not skipped anything._  
**The old white turtle**: Welcome back. Did you ask the Buddha my question? When I shall stop being a turtle, and become a person?  
**Tang Sanzang**: Oh. Oh, no. I forgot. I am so sorry. I forgot.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): what to do when you forget something that matters  
> _The monk forgot the turtle’s question. What is the best thing to do when you forget something that matters to someone?_  
> - ✅ Say sorry, and then do what you can to put it right → Yes. An honest sorry, followed by an action, is a strong mix.
> - Hope they won’t mind → The turtle did mind, quietly. An honest apology and a plan work better.
> _For grown-ups: Your child thought about how to apologise for forgetting._

_The turtle doesn’t say anything. He just sinks, very slowly and quite sadly, into the river._  
_Everyone goes into the water: monk, Monkey, Pigsy, Sandy, horse, scrolls, you. Your Nubbin is delighted._  

#### `reader:a02-19-turtle-done` · [main](main.md#a02-19-turtle-done)

_Your Nubbin swims round and round the turtle, then sits on his shell and pats it, as if to say it was a fair point._  
**The old white turtle**: No harm done. I only wanted to be remembered.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): oral history: stories keep people remembered  
> _Before most people could read, families kept the past alive by telling it, from grandparent to grandchild._  
> _Monkey’s adventures were told aloud in teahouses and acted on stages for centuries, before and after the novel was written down._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard how telling stories aloud keeps people and the past remembered._


#### `reader:a02-19-rock` · [main](main.md#a02-19-rock)

_You all haul the scrolls out of the river and lay them on a big flat rock to dry in the sun._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-4, notice): drying in sun and wind  
> _Wet paper dries when the water in it turns to vapour and drifts away._  
> _Sun warms it, and wind carries the vapour off, which is why a sunny, breezy rock is a good drying place._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child learned why sun and wind dry wet things._

_When you lift them, a few of the last pages stick fast to the stone. They tear, and the ends stay behind._  
**Tang Sanzang**: No. No! We came all this way. We crossed the whole world. And now they’re not whole.  
_Monkey looks at the torn edges for a long time. He doesn’t make a joke._  
**Monkey**: Heaven and earth aren’t whole either, master. Nothing is. These are just honest about it.  
**Monkey**: Maybe they’re meant to be not quite finished. So there’s room for whoever reads them next.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): is something less valuable if it is unfinished? (a think question)  
> _Monkey says the scrolls may be meant to be not quite finished. Is something less valuable if it is incomplete?_  
> _There is no single right answer. Think about it, then choose._  
> - No: gaps leave room for others → That is a fair answer. An unfinished thing can invite the next person in.
> - Yes: complete is better → That is a fair answer. Whole things are easier to use, though the monk learns to live with this one.
> - It depends what is missing → That is a fair answer. A missing last page and a missing foundation are not the same.
> _For grown-ups: Your child reflected on the value of unfinished things._


**Choices**

- **Try to peel the last pages free.**
    - You try, very carefully, but they stay where they are. In the end you leave them, and the rock keeps a few words for itself.
- **Leave them on the rock.**
    - You leave them. Your Nubbin pats the torn edges, as if they were a pet that had done its best.

#### `reader:a02-19-home` · [main](main.md#a02-19-home)

_The city of Chang’an turns out to meet you. The pines by the monastery gate, people say, all turned to point east the morning he came home._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, notice): Chang’an is Xi’an  
> _Chang’an was a real capital city, the eastern end of the Silk Road. Today it is called Xi’an._  
> _A tall pagoda built there to keep the scrolls, the Great Wild Goose Pagoda, still stands._  
> _For grown-ups: Your child heard that Chang’an is today’s Xi’an and where the scrolls were kept._


> **Lesson** (science, 5-ESS1-2, ask): shadows at sunrise  
> _The pines point east, towards the rising sun. At sunrise, which way do the pines’ shadows point?_  
> - ✅ West, away from the sun → Yes. A shadow points away from its light, so sunrise shadows are long and fall to the west.
> - East, towards the sun → A shadow always falls on the side away from the light. The sun is in the east, so shadows fall west.
> _For grown-ups: Your child worked out the direction of a sunrise shadow._

_The emperor himself walks out to meet the monk, and the scrolls are carried in with music._  
_Then, before anyone has finished cheering, the pilgrims are lifted back to Vulture Peak on a soft wind._  
_The Buddha gives each of them a new name. The monk becomes a buddha, and so does Monkey._  
**Monkey**: A buddha! Me! Then take this band off, master. A buddha in a headband looks silly.  
**Tang Sanzang**: Touch your head, Wukong.  
_Monkey touches his head, and the band is gone. Nobody saw it go._  

#### `reader:a02-19-complete` · [main](main.md#a02-19-complete)

**Monkey**: Gone. It went when I stopped needing it. Of course it did.  
_He turns to you. His eyes are still little suns, but kinder now, the way the sun is kinder in the evening._  
**Monkey**: You believed me once, when nobody else did. On the ridge. I never forgot.  
**Tang Sanzang**: And you were right. I was afraid, and I called it being careful. I am glad he came back.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): admitting you were afraid  
> _The monk says, “I was afraid, and I called it being careful.” Why is that a brave thing to say?_  
> - ✅ It means looking honestly at your own feelings → Yes. Naming a fear for what it is takes courage, and often helps mend things.
> - It is not brave, it is just an excuse → An excuse hides the truth. The monk is doing the opposite. Naming the fear honestly is brave.
> _For grown-ups: Your child thought about why admitting fear is brave._
