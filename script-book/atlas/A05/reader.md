# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A5: The Lion of Mali

## A5-1. The Hunters’ Prophecy

- `a05-01-s1`: Step into the red-earth page and find the young griot. · _Touch the page, then follow the sound of a balafon to the town gate._
- `a05-01-s2`: Stand at the back of the king’s court and listen to the hunter. · _Stay beside Balla under the silk-cotton tree, and listen closely._

#### `reader:a05-01-page` · [main](main.md#a05-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page the colour of red earth, patterned black and white like cloth dyed with mud._  
_In the corner, in Nova’s hand: “Griots have told this for eight hundred years, and no two tell it alike.”_  
_Under that: “Listen to more than one.”_  
_The earth warms under your palm. Then it is dust, and round thatched houses, and the smell of millet smoke._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, notice): Manding and the Mali Empire on the map: West Africa, and how old this story is  
> _This page is set in Manding, in West Africa, south of the Sahara desert. Today its lands lie in Mali and Guinea._  
> _The story tells how the Mali Empire began, about 800 years ago. Today’s Mali is named after it, and griots across the region still tell it._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the story is set in Manding, in West Africa, where the Mali Empire began about 800 years ago, and that griots still tell it._

**Balla Fasséké**: You came out of a picture. My father said someone would, one day. He says a lot of things.  
**Balla Fasséké**: I’m Balla Fasséké, son of Doua, who is griot to the king. One day I’ll be a griot too. Today I carry his things.  
**Balla Fasséké**: You could carry some. A griot needs an apprentice, and I haven’t got one yet.  
_Your Nubbin is already sitting on his balafon, a row of wooden keys with gourds hung underneath._  

**Choices**

- **Ask what a griot does.**
    - **Balla Fasséké**: We remember. Who married whom, who fought where, who was brave and who only said so.
    - **Balla Fasséké**: A king without a griot is a king nobody will remember. It makes kings polite to us.
- **Offer to carry the balafon.**
    - **Balla Fasséké**: Gently. It’s older than my father, and it knows it.

#### `reader:a05-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a05-01-page-done)

**Balla Fasséké**: Come. The king sits under the silk-cotton tree in the mornings. Something is going to happen there today.  
_You don’t know how he knows. By the look of him, he doesn’t either._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): a think question: what do you predict, and why?  
> _Balla says something will happen today, and can’t explain how he knows. What do you think is going on?_  
> - He has a griot’s feeling for stories → A good guess. A griot learns to sense when a story is about to start.
> - He has heard a rumour → Could be. News travels fast in a king’s town.
> - He is just guessing → That’s a fair answer. To be honest, I’m not sure myself.
> _For grown-ups: Made a prediction from a clue in the text and gave a reason for it (no single right answer)._


#### `reader:a05-01-court` · [main](main.md#a05-01-court)

_King Naré Maghan sits in the shade of a great silk-cotton tree, with his court around him._  
_Beside him stands Doua, who seems to be listening to everything at once._  
_A stranger comes in off the road: a hunter, with a bow on his back and a bag of cowrie shells at his belt._  
**The Hunter of Sangaran**: I’m a hunter of Sangaran, king. I’ve come because the shells told me to.  
**King Naré Maghan**: Then sit, hunter. A king should hear what the shells say, even when he doesn’t like it.  
_The hunter throws the cowries on a goatskin and looks at them for a long time._  
**The Hunter of Sangaran**: Two hunters will come to your gate with a woman. She is strong. Marry her.  
**The Hunter of Sangaran**: She will give you a son, and that son will make the name of Manding last as long as the sun does.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): oral history: stories carried by memory and the word “prophecy”  
> _A prophecy is a statement about what will happen in the future._  
> _Stories like this one were passed down aloud for centuries._  
> _That is called oral history: the past kept alive by spoken words instead of books. Griots train for years to remember it._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a prophecy is a statement about the future, and what oral history is._


**Choices**

- **Watch the king’s face.**
    - The king says nothing. He turns a kola nut over in his fingers, slowly, like a man deciding to remember.
- **Watch Balla.**
    - Balla’s lips are moving. He is saying the hunter’s words over to himself, so that he will have them.

#### `reader:a05-01-court-done` · [main](main.md#a05-01-court-done)

**Doua**: Hunter, the king thanks you. Eat before you go, and eat well.  
**Balla Fasséké**: Did you get all that? Say it back to me tonight. Then we’ll both have it.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, CASEL: Self-management, ask): ways to remember something heard once  
> _You have to remember the hunter’s words by tonight. Which helps most?_  
> - ✅ Say it aloud in order, again and again → Yes. Repeating in order is how griots keep a long telling safe.
> - Hope you remember it → Hope is nice, but practice works better. Say it aloud, in order.
> - Think about something else → Your mind will wander off. Say it aloud, again and again.
> _For grown-ups: Chose repetition aloud, in order, as a way to remember something._


#### `reader:a05-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a05-01-complete)

_That night, by the fire, you say the hunter’s words back to Balla. You get most of them, and he fills in the rest._  
**Balla Fasséké**: That’s the first thing a griot learns. Hear it once, keep it always.  
**Balla Fasséké**: Some tellers leave the hunter out, and start with the two brothers and the buffalo. My father starts here.  
**Balla Fasséké**: He says a story should begin with a promise. Then everybody listens to find out if it’s kept.  
_Balla taps one key of the balafon with his thumb. It makes a low, round sound, like water moving in a gourd._  
_Your Nubbin taps a shorter key. The sound comes out higher and flatter, and much more pleased with itself._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-1, ask): vibration and pitch: longer bars make lower notes  
> _Balla’s key sounds low and round, and your Nubbin’s sounds higher. Which key is longer?_  
> - ✅ Balla’s key, the lower one → Yes. Longer bars vibrate more slowly, and slower vibrations make lower notes.
> - Your Nubbin’s key → It’s the other way round: longer keys shake slowly, giving low notes. Short keys give high ones.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that vibrations make sound, and that longer balafon keys make lower notes._

**Balla Fasséké**: We’ll work on it.  

### Glossary

- **griot**: A West African storyteller, singer and keeper of history, who remembers things for a whole people.
- **prophecy**: A statement about something that will happen in the future.
- **oral history**: The past kept alive by spoken words and memory instead of written books.

## A5-2. Sogolon

- `a05-02-s1`: Sit by the fire and hear Balla tell the tale of the buffalo of Do. · _Find Balla at the fire behind Doua’s house and sit down._
- `a05-02-s2`: Walk with Sogolon from the gate to the king’s court. · _Meet the two hunters at the gate and walk beside the woman with them._

#### `reader:a05-02-fire` · [main](main.md#a05-02-fire)

**Balla Fasséké**: Before the strangers reach the gate, you should know where they’ve been. Sit. This is a fire story.  
**Balla Fasséké**: In the land of Do there was a buffalo nobody could hunt. It trampled the fields, and the best hunters came home with nothing.  
**Balla Fasséké**: Two brothers went after it. On the road they met an old woman, hungry and alone, and they shared their food with her.  
**Balla Fasséké**: She told them the truth: she was the buffalo. Because they’d been kind, she told them how to win.  
**Balla Fasséké**: She asked one thing. The king of Do would offer them any girl they liked. They were to choose the plainest. Her name was Sogolon.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): kindness is repaid  
> _What made the old woman help the two brothers?_  
> - ✅ They shared their food with her → Yes. They were kind when they didn’t need to be.
> - They begged her for help → They didn’t beg. They shared their food, and she chose to help.
> _For grown-ups: Noticed that the brothers’ kindness is why the old woman helped them._

_Your Nubbin has crept so close to the fire that its feet are warm. It doesn’t move them._  
**Balla Fasséké**: Some tellers say the old woman was Sogolon’s aunt. Some say she was Sogolon’s own spirit, walking about without her.  
**Balla Fasséké**: My father says it doesn’t matter which. What matters is that she was strong, and that it came to the girl.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): a think question: why do tellings differ?  
> _Balla tells two versions of who the old woman was, and won’t say which is true. Why might a griot do that?_  
> - Each version teaches something → A fair answer. Each version shows a different thing about Sogolon.
> - Nobody knows for sure → That could be it. Stories passed by mouth change a little in every telling.
> - So listeners can choose → A good thought. A griot trusts the listener to think too.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why a storyteller might share more than one version (no single right answer)._


**Choices**

- **Ask which one Balla believes.**
    - **Balla Fasséké**: I believe whichever one I’m telling. That’s the job.
- **Say the brothers won by being kind.**
    - **Balla Fasséké**: Yes. Write that one down in your head. It comes back.

#### `reader:a05-02-fire-done` · [main](main.md#a05-02-fire-done)

_Out on the road a dog barks, and then another. Balla is on his feet before the third._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): animals’ senses: dogs hear more than people  
> _Dogs can hear much softer and higher sounds than people can._  
> _That’s why the village dogs notice travellers long before anyone else. Their ears are an early warning._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that dogs hear better than people, which is why they bark at faraway visitors._

**Balla Fasséké**: That’s them. Come on.  

#### `reader:a05-02-gate` · [main](main.md#a05-02-gate)

_Two dusty hunters come through the gate. Between them walks a young woman, stooped, with a hump on her back and very bright eyes._  
**The Elder Hunter**: We bring her to the king of Manding. We were told he would know why.  
_People come out of their doorways to look. Someone whispers, and someone else doesn’t bother to whisper._  
**Sogolon**: I’ve been looked at before. It doesn’t wear me out the way they hope.  
**The Elder Hunter**: She walked from Do on her own feet. We offered her a donkey. She said the donkey looked tired.  
**Sogolon**: It did look tired.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): what helps someone who is being stared at  
> _People stare at Sogolon as she walks. What would help her most?_  
> - ✅ Walk beside her → A stranger from a picture, and a small round thing. Good company.
> - Stare to see what she looks like → Staring is exactly what hurts. Walking beside her says “you are not alone”.
> - Whisper to a neighbour → Whispering is another way of staring. Walking beside her is kinder.
> _For grown-ups: Chose to walk beside someone who is being stared at, rather than join in staring._


**Choices**

- **Walk beside her.**
    - You fall in beside her. She glances at you, and something in her shoulders lets go a little.
    - **Sogolon**: A stranger from a picture, and a small round thing. Good. I like the company of odd things.
- **Offer to carry her bundle.**
    - **Sogolon**: No. It’s mine, and it’s light. But thank you for asking instead of taking it.
    - Your Nubbin takes hold of one corner anyway, and walks the rest of the way like that, helping.

#### `reader:a05-02-gate-done` · [main](main.md#a05-02-gate-done)

_Under the silk-cotton tree, the court laughs out loud when it sees her. The king doesn’t._  
**King Naré Maghan**: Doua. The hunter of Sangaran. Do you remember what he said?  
**Doua**: Every word, my king.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): what a character’s silence tells us  
> _The court laughs, but the king doesn’t. What does that tell you about him?_  
> - ✅ He takes the prophecy seriously → Yes. He’s thinking about the hunter’s words, not about how she looks.
> - He is bored → A bored king wouldn’t be asking Doua to recall the prophecy.
> - He is angry at Sogolon → He asks about the prophecy, not about her. He’s listening, not judging.
> _For grown-ups: Read a character’s motive from his actions: the king takes the prophecy seriously._


#### `reader:a05-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a05-02-complete)

**King Naré Maghan**: Then I know why you brought her to me. Sogolon of Do, will you be my wife?  
_The laughing stops all at once, like a pot taken off the fire._  
**Sogolon**: I will. And I’ll thank your court to stop staring. I haven’t grown a second head.  
_Doua is the first to laugh, warmly this time. Then the king laughs too, and the rest of the court has to._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): how a character handles being laughed at  
> _Sogolon turns laughing-at into laughing-with. What gave her that dignity?_  
> - ✅ She spoke up, honestly and with humour → Yes. Dignity means keeping your self-respect, even when others don’t.
> - She ignored everybody → She didn’t ignore them. She answered, calmly and with a joke.
> - She got angry and shouted → Her words were sharp, but she stayed calm. That calm is dignity.
> _For grown-ups: Described how Sogolon kept her dignity: speaking up honestly, calmly and with humour._

**Balla Fasséké**: Remember that. She made them laugh with her, not at her, in one breath. My father has been trying to do that for twenty years.  

### Glossary

- **dignity**: Keeping your self-respect and calm, even when others are unkind.

## A5-3. The Boy Who Couldn’t Walk

- `a05-03-s1`: Visit Sogolon’s house and keep her son company. · _Sogolon’s house is the small one at the back of the royal yard._
- `a05-03-s2`: Go with Sogolon to ask Queen Sassouma for baobab leaves. · _Walk with Sogolon across the yard to the queen mother’s house._

#### `reader:a05-03-house` · [main](main.md#a05-03-house)

_The atlas page turns a little, the way a griot skips ahead. A wedding, a birth and seven rainy seasons pass in a breath._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-ESS2-1, notice): wet and dry seasons in West Africa  
> _In much of West Africa the year is split into a rainy season and a dry season, rather than summer and winter._  
> _Seven rainy seasons means seven years. The rains grow the crops, which are harvested as the dry season begins._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that much of West Africa has rainy and dry seasons, and that seven rainy seasons means seven years._

_Sogolon’s son is seven years old. He sits in the dust outside her door, broad and solemn, and he crawls wherever he goes._  
**Balla Fasséké**: His name is Mari Djata. One day everyone will call him Sundiata. He doesn’t talk much. He eats like three boys and he watches everything.  
_Your Nubbin toddles over and sits down in front of him. The boy looks at it for a long time._  
_Then he puts out one hand, very slowly, and pats it on the head._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): reading a character’s trust from what he does  
> _Sundiata doesn’t pat many people. How can you tell he trusts your Nubbin?_  
> - ✅ He chose to touch it, slowly and gently → Yes. Actions can show feelings that words don’t.
> - He said so out loud → He didn’t say a thing. What he did tells us.
> _For grown-ups: Inferred a character’s feeling from his actions rather than his words._

**Sogolon**: He doesn’t do that for many people. Your little round friend is honoured.  
**Balla Fasséké**: The old king died in the dry season, with my father beside him. Dankaran Touman is king now, and his mother rules.  
**Balla Fasséké**: Some people say the hunter was wrong. They say it where Sogolon can hear.  

**Choices**

- **Sit down in the dust with the boy.**
    - You sit, and nobody says anything. The boy goes on watching the yard, and you watch it with him.
    - After a while you notice he isn’t waiting for anything. He’s just looking, very hard, at all of it.
- **Ask Sogolon if he’ll walk one day.**
    - **Sogolon**: He will walk when he walks. I don’t pull up seedlings to make them grow faster.
    - **Sogolon**: And until then, he is my son, and that is already a great deal.

#### `reader:a05-03-house-done` · [main](main.md#a05-03-house-done)

**Balla Fasséké**: Mark this, apprentice. He isn’t waiting to become someone. He already is someone. He’s just not finished.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): a think question: is slow the same as behind?  
> _Balla says Sundiata is already someone, only not finished. Is going slowly the same as being behind?_  
> - No. Everyone grows at their own pace → A thoughtful answer. A seed isn’t behind a tree. It’s a seed.
> - Sometimes, but it can still be fine → That’s honest. Being slower can feel like being behind, even when it isn’t.
> - I’m not sure → That’s fair. It’s the kind of question griots think about for years.
> _For grown-ups: Reflected on whether going slowly means being behind (no single right answer)._


#### `reader:a05-03-leaves` · [main](main.md#a05-03-leaves)

**Sogolon**: I need a few baobab leaves for the sauce. Ours are gone. Sassouma has plenty.  
_Queen Sassouma sits in her own yard with her women, under a baobab heavy with leaves. She sees Sogolon coming a long way off._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): baobabs drop their leaves in the dry season to save water  
> _A baobab is deciduous: it loses all its leaves in the dry season, and grows them again when the rains come._  
> _Dropping leaves saves water. In many parts of West Africa, fresh baobab leaves are cooked in sauces, which is why a tree full of them is worth asking for._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that baobabs lose their leaves in the dry season to save water, and that the leaves are eaten._

**Sogolon**: Sister. A handful of leaves, for my son’s supper.  
**Sassouma Bérété**: Take them, of course. My son picked them for me himself, this morning. He climbed up to fetch them. On his own two legs.  
**Sassouma Bérété**: Your son, I hear, still fetches things the way a lizard does.  
_The women laugh. Sogolon takes the leaves, and her hand doesn’t shake, quite._  

**Choices**

- **Say nothing, and walk out beside Sogolon.**
    - You walk out beside her. She keeps her back straight all the way to her own door, which is a long way, for her.
- **Look Sassouma in the eye.**
    - You look at Sassouma. She looks back, cool and pleasant, as if you were a cloud that might rain.
    - **Balla Fasséké**: Good. She’ll remember your face. Mind you, that may not be good.

#### `reader:a05-03-leaves-done` · [main](main.md#a05-03-leaves-done)

_At her own door, Sogolon sits down on the step and cries, quietly, with her face in the leaves._  
_Her son crawls over to her. Your Nubbin sits on her other side and leans._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): how to comfort someone who is crying  
> _Sogolon is crying. Which is the best way to comfort her?_  
> - ✅ Sit close and stay → Yes. Being there is the comfort; you don’t have to fix anything.
> - Tell her to stop crying → Crying is a way of letting sadness out. Sit close and let her.
> - Change the subject → Changing the subject can make a sad person feel unheard. Stay close instead.
> _For grown-ups: Chose to sit close and stay with someone who is crying._


#### `reader:a05-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a05-03-complete)

**Sundiata**: Mother. Why are you crying?  
_Sogolon looks up. So does Balla, and so, it seems, does the whole yard._  
**Sogolon**: Because of leaves. Because of a woman with a sharp tongue. Because of you, my son.  
**Sundiata**: Then dry your eyes, Mother. Today I stand up.  
**Sundiata**: Send to the forges. Ask the smiths for a rod of iron, so heavy it takes all of them to carry it.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiply to change years into months  
> _Sundiata crawled for seven years, and there are 12 months in a year. How many months is that?_  
> - ✅ 84 months → Yes: 7 × 12 = 84. Eighty-four months of crawling, and he never stopped trying.
> - 19 months → That’s 7 + 12. We need 7 groups of 12: 7 × 12 = 84.
> - 70 months → Close, but 7 × 10 is 70. A year has 12 months: 7 × 12 = 84.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied 7 × 12 to find the number of months in seven years._

_Balla’s mouth is open. He shuts it, and you see him say the words over to himself, quickly, so he will have them._  

### Glossary

- **deciduous**: Describes a plant that loses all its leaves for part of the year.

## A5-4. The Iron Rod

- `a05-04-s1`: Run to the smiths with Sogolon’s message. · _The forges are down by the river, where the smoke is blackest._
- `a05-04-s2`: Watch the boy stand. · _Stand with Balla at the edge of the crowd outside Sogolon’s door._
- `a05-04-s3`: Gather the baobab’s leaves for Sogolon. · _Pick up the leaves around the baobab now standing at Sogolon’s door._

#### `reader:a05-04-forge` · [main](main.md#a05-04-forge)

_The smiths’ quarter smells of charcoal and hot water. Hammers ring on every side, each on its own beat._  
_The master smith is a big, quiet man with burn marks up both forearms. He listens to the whole message without stopping work._  
**The Master Smith**: The heaviest rod we’ve ever made. For the crawling boy.  
**The Master Smith**: My father forged a rod years ago, for a day like this. He said someone would come asking. He kept it under the bellows.  
_It takes six of his apprentices to drag it out. It is as long as two of you and dark as a wet night._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-4, notice): heating makes iron soft; cooling makes it hard again  
> **The Master Smith**: A forge is a hearth, hot enough to make iron glow. Bellows pump air in to make the charcoal burn even hotter.  
> **The Master Smith**: Hot iron goes soft, so I can bend and hammer it. When it cools, it goes hard again. That change can be done again and again.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that heating softens iron and cooling hardens it, and that bellows feed the fire with air._

**The Master Smith**: Smiths keep secrets, apprentice. That’s half of what we are. The other half is patience.  

**Choices**

- **Take hold of the rod with the apprentices.**
    - You take hold, and it is so heavy your arms forget they are yours. Your Nubbin pushes from behind, which helps exactly nothing.
- **Ask if a boy can really lift that.**
    - **The Master Smith**: No. A boy can’t. That’s the point of asking for it.

#### `reader:a05-04-forge-done` · [main](main.md#a05-04-forge-done)

_The whole quarter downs hammers and follows the rod up the hill. So do half the children in Niani._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiply to find a total  
> _Six apprentices drag the rod, and say each one could drag 15 kilograms alone. How much can they drag together?_  
> - ✅ 90 kilograms → Yes: 6 × 15 = 90. And still it took all six to move it.
> - 21 kilograms → That’s 6 + 15. Six groups of 15 is 6 × 15 = 90.
> - 60 kilograms → Try again: 6 × 10 = 60, and 6 × 5 = 30. Together, 90.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied 6 × 15 to find the combined pull of six apprentices (an in-story sum)._

**Balla Fasséké**: Everyone wants to see it. Half of them want to laugh. Stay close to me and keep your ears open.  

#### `reader:a05-04-stand` · [main](main.md#a05-04-stand)

_The smiths lay the rod down in front of Sogolon’s door. The crowd goes quiet in a ring around it._  
_The boy crawls to it. He takes it in both hands, and sets one end in the dust._  
_He pulls, and his arms shake. Sweat runs down his face, and still he pulls, slowly, hand over hand, up the rod._  
_He is on his knees, then on one foot. Then he is on both feet, with the iron rod bent in his hands like a hunter’s bow._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): balanced and unbalanced forces  
> _Gravity pulls Sundiata down towards the ground. For him to pull himself up the rod, what must be true?_  
> - ✅ His pull must be bigger than gravity’s pull on him → Yes. When forces are unbalanced, things move, and his muscles finally won.
> - Gravity must switch off → Gravity never switches off. His pull has to be stronger than the pull down.
> - The rod must pull him up → The rod is just a handle. It’s Sundiata’s pull that wins over gravity.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that an object moves when one force is bigger than the other (unbalanced forces)._

**Balla Fasséké**: He’s standing.  
_Then Balla is singing. It is a praise song, his own, made on the spot, and the whole hill takes it up._  
**Sogolon**: My son is standing.  

**Choices**

- **Cheer.**
    - You cheer. Your Nubbin cheers, too, which is a high round squeak, and runs three times round his feet.
- **Look at Sogolon.**
    - Sogolon isn’t cheering; she has her hands over her mouth. She has waited seven years, and she is still waiting, one breath more.

#### `reader:a05-04-stand-done` · [main](main.md#a05-04-stand-done)

**Balla Fasséké**: Some tellers say the first rods snapped in his hands, and he leaned on his mother’s stick in the end. Some say it was a baobab branch.  
**Balla Fasséké**: I was there. It was iron. It bent. But I’ll tell you the stick version, too, because that one’s also true.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): a think question: can two tellings both be true?  
> _Balla says the stick version is also true. How can two different tellings both be true?_  
> - Each one holds a true part of the story → A fair answer. The truth can have more than one side.
> - They can’t both be true → That’s a fair answer too. Historians also ask which telling is most likely.
> - It depends what “true” means → A sharp thought. Is “true” about facts, or about what the story means?
> _For grown-ups: Reflected on how differing tellings can both hold truth, and compared a teller’s view with their own (no single right answer)._


#### `reader:a05-04-baobab` · [main](main.md#a05-04-baobab)

**Sundiata**: Mother. You wanted baobab leaves.

### Glossary

- **charter**: A set of rules for how people live together.
- **intangible cultural heritage**: Traditions that live in people, such as stories, music and skills, rather than in buildings or objects.
- **jeli**: The Mande word for a griot. The plural is jeliw.
- **manuscript**: A book or document written by hand.
