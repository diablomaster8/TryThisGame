# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A8: The Twelve Tasks

## A8-1. Caesar’s Challenge

- `a08-01-s1`: Step into the small green dot on the map of Gaul. · _Everything else is Roman red. Look by the sea, top left._
- `a08-01-s2`: Go to the village and hear what the Romans want. · _Follow Obelix and the boars. Everyone is in the village square._

#### `reader:a08-01-map` · [main](main.md#a08-01-map)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a map of Gaul, around 50 BC. It is painted Roman red from the mountains to the sea._  
_Not quite all of it. Up by the sea, one small green dot sits untouched, with the red curving carefully round it._  
_Four tiny Roman camps ring the dot like cats round a hedgehog, unsure where to start._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, notice): where Gaul was, and when Caesar’s wars happened  
> _Gaul was the Roman name for a big region, roughly where France is today, plus parts of Belgium and nearby lands._  
> _Julius Caesar’s army fought the peoples of Gaul in the Gallic Wars, from about 58 to 50 BC. “BC” counts years backwards to the year 1._  
> _So on this map, nearly all of Gaul is Roman red. The map’s green dot is the story’s joke._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Gaul was roughly modern France, that Caesar’s Gallic Wars ran about 58 to 50 BC, and read a map key by colour._

_You touch the green dot, and it is warm and smells faintly of roast boar. Then you are standing in a beech wood._  
_Something small and white shoots out of the bracken, stops nose to nose with your Nubbin, and wags so hard it nearly falls over._  
_It is a very small dog with a black-tipped ear. He and your Nubbin look at each other, very serious, for a long moment._  

**Choices**

- **Let them work it out.**
    - They work it out in two seconds: some circling, one sneeze, and then they are best friends.
- **Crouch down and hold out a hand.**
    - The dog licks your hand once, politely, then goes straight back to your Nubbin. You know your place.

#### `reader:a08-01-map-done` · [main](main.md#a08-01-map-done)

_Two men come crashing through the trees after the dog: one small, with a winged helmet, and one enormous, with a boar under each arm._  
**Obelix**: Dogmatix! Oh. He has found a friend. Look, Asterix, a little round friend.  
_For someone big enough to carry two boars at once, Obelix crouches very slowly, so as not to frighten your Nubbin._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): being gentle with someone smaller  
> _Obelix is huge and strong, but he crouches slowly beside your Nubbin. Why might a big person move gently near someone small?_  
> - ✅ So the small one feels safe → Yes. Being big means taking care. Moving slowly tells a small creature, “I will not hurt you.”
> - Because he is afraid of it → He is careful, not afraid. He does it so he will not frighten the Nubbin, which is kind.
> - It makes no difference → It does to the Nubbin. A sudden, big movement can be scary, even from a friend.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how a bigger, stronger person can help a smaller one feel safe._


#### `reader:a08-01-square` · [main](main.md#a08-01-square)

_The village is round, fenced and loud. A chief stands on a shield held up by two men who are clearly tired of holding it._  
**Caius Pupus**: Salve! That is Latin for hello, and I am Caius Pupus. I bring the words of Julius Caesar.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): Latin: salve, and where English gets its words  
> **Caius Pupus**: The Romans spoke Latin. “Salve” means hello, and there is “vale” for goodbye. You will hear both on this journey.  
> **Caius Pupus**: English is full of Latin words. “Vocabulary” and “audience” both come from Latin.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the Latin greeting “salve” and that English borrows many words from Latin._

**Caius Pupus**: Caesar hears that you Gauls are gods. He does not believe it. He proposes twelve tasks, like the labours of Hercules.  
**Caius Pupus**: Pass them all, and you are gods. Fail one, and you will kindly become Roman, like everybody else.  
**Vitalstatistix**: Twelve tasks! Ha! We will do thirteen, fourteen if there is lunch.  
**Asterix**: Who decides if we pass? Not a Roman. You would fail us for breathing.  
**Caius Pupus**: And not a Gaul. You would pass yourselves for sneezing.  
_Every head slowly turns to the one person who is neither: you, at the back, with a Nubbin and a dog._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): fairness: who should referee, and why  
> _The village wants a referee who is neither Roman nor Gaul. Why does that matter? Is a stranger always fair?_  
> - A referee must not favour either side → That is a good reason. Being on neither side makes it easier to be fair. Whether it is enough is a good thing to keep thinking about.
> - A stranger might still have a bias → A fair answer. Even a stranger can have hidden favourites. The best referees notice their own bias, and say so.
> - Rules matter more than the referee → A fair answer. Clear rules help any referee. But somebody still has to apply them fairly, and that is your job.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what makes a referee fair, and whether being outside the sides is enough._


**Choices**

- **“All right. I’ll referee.”**
    - **Vitalstatistix**: Splendid! A referee from the land of books. Nobody can say fairer than that.
- **“Me? I only came in through a map.”**
    - **Asterix**: Exactly. You do not owe anybody anything. That is what makes you fair.
    - You find you cannot argue with that.

#### `reader:a08-01-square-done` · [main](main.md#a08-01-square-done)

**Caius Pupus**: Here is your wax tablet. A tablet is a flat board with wax on it, and you scratch letters in the wax with a stick.  
_Down its side, in neat Roman numerals, it counts from I to XII. Every line is blank._  
_The Romans wrote numbers with letters: I is one, V is five and X is ten. You make other numbers by adding them up._  

> **Lesson** (maths, notice): Roman numerals: I, V, X and adding them  
> **Caius Pupus**: Look: II is one and one, so two. III is three. VI is five and one, so six. XII is ten and one and one, so twelve.  
> **Caius Pupus**: You will tick them off one at a time. I to XII, one for every task.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Roman numerals use letters I, V and X, and that the numbers are made by adding them._


#### `reader:a08-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a08-01-complete)

_Getafix the druid, white-bearded and unhurried, stirs a cauldron by his hut. He ladles a flask full for Asterix._  
**Getafix**: The potion, for the tasks. Strength for a while, and not one drop for Obelix.  
**Obelix**: I only want a little bit. A taste.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): saying no kindly, with a reason  
> _Getafix says no to Obelix. How could he say no in a way that still shows he cares?_  
> - ✅ Explain why, and say it gently → Yes. A reason, a kind voice and a little humour help a no feel like care.
> - Say no and walk away → That is quick, but it can leave a friend feeling shut out. A short reason helps.
> - Give in, to avoid a fuss → It is tempting, but if a no matters for someone’s sake, kindness means keeping it, gently.
> _For grown-ups: Practised saying no kindly with a reason._

**Getafix**: You fell in when you were a baby. It is permanent. You are the reason I keep a lid on it.  
_Obelix sulks for exactly as long as it takes to remember the boars. Dogmatix and your Nubbin are asleep in a heap by the fire._  
**Caius Pupus**: We leave at dawn for task one. I should warn you, I have a very good eye for cheating.  

### Glossary

- **tablet**: A flat board covered in wax, which people scratched writing into.
- **numeral**: A symbol that stands for a number.

## A8-2. Asbestos

- `a08-02-s1`: Pace out the course and meet the runner. · _Walk from the start stone to the finish post, then talk to Asbestos._
- `a08-02-s2`: Start the race and see who reaches the post. · _Drop your hand to start. Then watch the road, not Asterix._

#### `reader:a08-02-track` · [main](main.md#a08-02-track)

_Task one is a race along a straight dirt road, from a stone to a post far off. You pace it out, and stop counting._  
_At the start, a young man in a short white tunic is stretching. He stretches parts of himself you did not know stretched._  
**Asbestos**: Asbestos, of Greece. I have won at Olympia, where the Games began. Gods and people came to watch us run.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.3-5, notice): the Olympic Games began in Greece  
> **Asbestos**: The ancient Games were held at Olympia, in Greece. Tradition says the first was in 776 BC. After that they came every four years.  
> **Asbestos**: Athletes ran, wrestled and threw. Today’s Olympic Games were started again in Athens in 1896, and they still come every four years.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Olympic Games began in ancient Greece, at Olympia, every four years, and were revived in 1896._

**Asbestos**: You will not see me after the start. That is not boasting. It is a description.  
**Caius Pupus**: The referee will start the race. Please be impartial. I mean, please be quick.  
_Your Nubbin and Dogmatix crouch at the start stone with their bottoms in the air, very serious._  

**Choices**

- **Let them race too.**
    - **Caius Pupus**: Animals are not in the rules.
    - You point out that animals are not out of the rules either. Pupus writes that down, crossly.
- **Scoop them up out of the way.**
    - You lift your Nubbin. Dogmatix sits down on your foot, as if that settles it.

#### `reader:a08-02-track-done` · [main](main.md#a08-02-track-done)

_You lift your hand. Asbestos goes still as a statue, and Asterix yawns._  
_The road is one stade long: the length of a Greek running track, nearly two football pitches end to end._  
_The Greeks called the track a “stadion”, and the Romans said “stadium”. That is where our word comes from._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, notice): units of length: the stade and the metre  
> _The stade at Olympia was about 192 metres. Five stades would be 960 metres, nearly a kilometre._  
> _Old units like the stade changed from place to place. Today’s races use metres, so any two tracks can be compared._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a stade was about 192 metres, and compared it with metres and kilometres._


#### `reader:a08-02-race` · [main](main.md#a08-02-race)

_Your hand drops. Asbestos is a white blur, then a cloud of dust, then a dot._  
_Asterix ambles off the line with his hands behind his back, like a man going to look at the weather._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): similes  
> _“Like a man going to look at the weather” compares Asterix’s walk to something. What is it called when you compare with “like” or “as”?_  
> - ✅ A simile → Yes. A simile compares two things using “like” or “as”. It paints a picture quickly.
> - A metaphor → Close. A metaphor says one thing is another. A simile says it is like another thing, using “like” or “as”.
> - A rhyme → Rhymes repeat sounds. Here the words compare two things with “like”, which is a simile.
> _For grown-ups: Identified a simile (a comparison using “like” or “as”)._

**Caius Pupus**: Is he trying?  
_Halfway along the road he has one sip from his flask. Then the road is empty where he was._  
_Down at the far end two dots become one, and that dot is Asterix, sitting on the finish post, waving, with an apple._  
_Asbestos reaches the post at a speed nobody has ever reached anything, sees Asterix, and keeps going out of pure surprise._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiplying to find a distance  
> _Say Asbestos runs 8 metres every second. How far has he run after 5 seconds?_  
> - ✅ 40 metres → Yes. 8 metres a second for 5 seconds is 5 times 8, which is 40 metres.
> - 13 metres → That adds 8 and 5. Each second he runs 8 metres, so it is 5 groups of 8: 5 times 8 is 40 metres.
> - 85 metres → Not quite. Five groups of eight is five times eight, which is 40 metres.
> _For grown-ups: Used multiplication to find distance: 5 seconds at 8 metres a second._


#### `reader:a08-02-race-done` · [main](main.md#a08-02-race-done)

_Far away, over the hills, a small white figure is still running. Obelix shades his eyes and watches him go, a little enviously._  
**Obelix**: Is he all right? He looked a bit surprised.  
**Asterix**: He will be fine. He just needs somebody to tell him he ran well, because he did.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): losing well and being kind to someone who lost  
> _Asbestos is the fastest runner in the world, and he has just lost. What could help him feel better?_  
> - Telling him honestly that he ran well → A fair answer. Honest, specific praise helps, and it is true. He did run well.
> - Pretending he won → A kind wish. But he would know it was not true, and honest praise lasts longer.
> - Leaving him alone → A fair answer. Some people want space after losing. A kind word can still help later.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how to be kind and honest to someone who lost._


#### `reader:a08-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a08-02-complete)

_You scratch a mark beside I on the wax tablet. Pupus looks at it, sighs, and initials it._  
**Obelix**: I could have done that, if there had been a boar at the end.  

> **Lesson** (maths, notice): I is one, and the numerals read left to right  
> **Caius Pupus**: I is a single stroke, like one finger held up. It is the smallest Roman numeral.  
> **Caius Pupus**: Notice that the Romans had no symbol for zero. Counting began at I.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Roman numeral I is one, and that Roman numerals had no zero._

**Asterix**: He was very fast. He just was not first.  
**Caius Pupus**: One task. Eleven to go. I have brought a great many spare tablets.  

### Glossary

- **impartial**: Fair to everyone, and not taking anyone’s side.
- **stade**: An ancient Greek measure of length, the length of a running track.

## A8-3. Verses

- `a08-03-s1`: Carry the javelins out to the throwing field. · _There are three javelins on the cart. Carry them one at a time._
- `a08-03-s2`: Watch both throws and measure them. · _Stand by the far marker. Count your steps from the line to where each javelin lands._

#### `reader:a08-03-field` · [main](main.md#a08-03-field)

_Task two is a wide field of short grass with a white line at one end. A tall man in an embroidered coat waits beside it._  
**Verses**: Verses, of Persia, at your service. Forgive me if I do not shake hands. These hands are working today.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.3-5, notice): ancient Persia, and throwing as an ancient sport  
> **Verses**: Persia was a great empire, in the lands that today include Iran. It was famous for its long roads, fast messengers and fine gardens.  
> **Verses**: Javelins began as hunting spears. Throwing them for distance became a sport in the ancient Olympics.  
> _For grown-ups: Heard that Persia was a great empire in the region of modern Iran, and that javelin throwing began as hunting and became a sport._

_He has three javelins on a cart, each wrapped in silk. He unwraps them like a man introducing his children._  
**Verses**: This one is Swift. This one is Patient. And this one I have never thrown. I am saving her for an occasion.  
**Asterix**: Is this an occasion?  
**Verses**: It is beginning to feel like one.  

**Choices**

- **Carry them very carefully, like Verses would.**
    - **Verses**: You carry them like friends. Thank you.
- **Ask Obelix to help.**
    - Obelix carries all three in one hand and the cart in the other, and asks where you would like the cart.

#### `reader:a08-03-field-done` · [main](main.md#a08-03-field-done)

_You lay the javelins on the grass by the line. Your Nubbin sits beside the one called Patient, which seems right._  
_You decide to measure the throws. You could count your paces, but your Nubbin’s paces are very small._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): why we use standard units  
> _Your paces are longer than your Nubbin’s. Why is a measuring rope, knotted every Roman foot, better than counting paces?_  
> - ✅ A Roman foot is the same for everyone → Yes. A standard unit means everyone gets the same answer, however long their legs are.
> - Paces are always shorter → Not always. The point is that paces change from person to person, and a Roman foot never does.
> - It makes no difference → It does. If you measure in paces and your Nubbin measures in paces, you will get different numbers for the same throw.
> _For grown-ups: Understood why standard units of length matter: paces vary, metres do not._


#### `reader:a08-03-throws` · [main](main.md#a08-03-throws)

_Verses takes Swift, steps up, and throws. It goes up like a bird and comes down at the end of the field, quivering._  
**Caius Pupus**: A record, surely. Referee, measure it.  
_You measure it. It takes a long time, and you start again twice, because Dogmatix keeps bringing you sticks._  
_Asterix takes a sip from his flask and borrows a plain javelin. He throws it underarm, almost lazily._  
_It goes over Verses’ javelin, over the trees and over the hill. The whistle of it fades away._  
**Verses**: Where is it going?  
**Asterix**: I could not tell you. I just threw it.  
**Verses**: And what is she called?  
**Asterix**: Er. Stick.  
_Dogmatix, who has brought you eleven sticks today, looks up sharply._  
**Verses**: Stick. A modest name. I like her already.  

> **Lesson** (sel, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): fairness when a result cannot be measured  
> _Verses says you cannot measure a throw that has not finished. Is it fair to give Asterix the win without a number? What would you do?_  
> - Give him the win, because it clearly went further → A fair answer. When the evidence is clear, a number may not be needed. Pupus might want it in writing, though.
> - Wait until it lands, then measure → A fair answer. Fairness sometimes means being patient and waiting for the facts, even when it takes a week.
> - Say neither wins, and try again → A fair answer. Starting again with equal rules is one way to be sure. It might also be a long afternoon.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about fairness when a result cannot be measured._


**Choices**

- **Start walking after it with your tablet.**
    - **Caius Pupus**: Come back. Come back! We will be here for a week.
    - You come back. You give it the win on what you are calling “visible evidence of leaving”.
- **Call it: the javelin is still going.**
    - **Verses**: A fair ruling. One cannot measure a throw that has not finished.

#### `reader:a08-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a08-03-complete)

_You scratch two strokes on the wax tablet and Pupus initials them. Verses wraps Swift in silk again and bows to Asterix._  
**Verses**: A good throw is a good throw, whoever makes it. I am going to find out where yours lands.  

> **Lesson** (maths, notice): II is two, and what the numerals add up to  
> **Caius Pupus**: Two strokes: I and I. The Romans wrote II for two, and III for three. Up to three, you just keep adding strokes.  
> **Caius Pupus**: After that it gets tidier. Four is IV, as you will see.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Roman II and III are made by repeating the stroke I, and that four is written differently._

_He sets off over the hill with the cart, and the javelin he was saving still in its silk._  
**Obelix**: He is nice. Are all the champions going to be nice?  
**Caius Pupus**: No.  

### Glossary

- **javelin**: A light spear that is thrown, often as a sport.
- **embroidered**: Decorated with patterns sewn in thread.

## A8-4. Cilindric

- `a08-04-s1`: Read Cilindric’s rulebook before the bout. · _You are the referee: the rules are your job. Open the book on the bench._
- `a08-04-s2`: Referee the bout. · _Stand by the rope. Asterix has an idea; let it play out._

#### `reader:a08-04-rules` · [main](main.md#a08-04-rules)

_Task three is a ring of rope in a meadow. Inside it a short, square man in a white wrap is folding a towel into perfect quarters._  
**Cilindric**: Cilindric. Good morning. Before we begin, the referee will please read the rules.  
_He hands you a book as thick as a loaf, every page ruled into neat columns._  
**Cilindric**: My art is about using your opponent’s strength. But also about doing it correctly. In the right order. With the right bow.  
_Rules one to forty are about bowing. Rule forty-one says what to do if the bow is returned too low._  
**Asterix**: Is there a rule for what happens if the other man does not want to wrestle?  
**Cilindric**: Rule three hundred and twelve. It is very firm.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): what makes a rule a good rule  
> _Cilindric has more than three hundred rules, and forty of them are about bowing. Which of these is the best kind of rule?_  
> - ✅ A short rule that keeps people safe and is the same for everyone → Yes. Good rules are clear, fair and have a reason. Rules about safety are the first you want.
> - A rule nobody can remember, but which is very detailed → A rule nobody can remember cannot help anyone. Good rules are simple enough to follow.
> - A rule that only helps the person who wrote it → That is not fair. A good rule should be fair to everyone it covers, not just its author.
> _For grown-ups: Compared good and bad rules: clear, fair and for a reason._


**Choices**

- **Read every rule, properly.**
    - **Cilindric**: Ah. A referee who reads. You have my respect.
    - It takes until lunch. Pupus brings sandwiches, and seems to understand completely.
- **Skim it and nod wisely.**
    - **Cilindric**: Hm. You have skipped the section on nodding.

#### `reader:a08-04-rules-done` · [main](main.md#a08-04-rules-done)

_You close the book. Cilindric bows to it, then to you, then to Asterix, then, after a moment’s thought, to the meadow._  
**Caius Pupus**: Vale, book. That is Latin for goodbye. It comes from a word meaning “be well”.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): everyday Latin: salve, vale, gratias  
> **Caius Pupus**: Three words to keep: salve for hello, vale for goodbye and gratias for thank you.  
> **Caius Pupus**: “Vale” began as “be well”, like our “farewell”. Many words begin as a wish for someone.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the Latin words salve, vale and gratias, and that “vale” meant “be well”._

_Pupus looks pleased with himself. He has been waiting all day for someone to ask about Latin._  

#### `reader:a08-04-bout` · [main](main.md#a08-04-bout)

_They face each other and bow. Cilindric moves like water, and suddenly Asterix is upside down and then the right way up._  
**Asterix**: Wonderful. Could you show me that again, slowly? I want to be sure I have the rules right.  
**Cilindric**: Of course. Rule eighty. First the hand, here. Then the foot, here. Then the turn, so.  
**Cilindric**: Rule eighty-one follows rule eighty, with the elbow under, so, and then the knee comes through, so…  
_Cilindric, demonstrating on nobody but himself, turns, tucks, turns again, and ties himself into a tidy, polite knot._  
**Cilindric**: I appear to have won, against myself. Which, under rule three hundred and twelve, means I have lost.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): when rules get in your own way  
> _Cilindric was undone by his own rules. Is that his fault? Can there be too many rules?_  
> - Yes. Too many rules can trip you up → A fair answer. Rules should help us, and a rulebook as thick as a loaf is hard to follow even for the person who wrote it.
> - No. He just made a mistake → A fair answer. Even with good rules, people slip. What matters is how kindly he and everyone else handle it.
> - The rules are fine; the knot was bad luck → A fair answer. Sometimes things go wrong without anyone to blame. A kind reaction still helps.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether having too many rules can trip people up._


**Choices**

- **Help untie him.**
    - It takes you, Obelix and both animals. Cilindric thanks each of you by name, including the dog.
- **Look up rule three hundred and twelve to be sure.**
    - **Cilindric**: Correct. A referee who checks. I would wrestle under you again any time.

#### `reader:a08-04-complete` · [main](main.md#a08-04-complete)

_You scratch a third stroke on the wax tablet. Cilindric folds his towel again, perfectly._  
**Cilindric**: A clean result. Unusual, but clean. Please keep the book. I have three more at home.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, do): III is three; counting the tablet  
> _(Count the strokes on your tablet: I, II and now III.)_  
> **Caius Pupus**: Three strokes for three tasks. Count what is left: nine more.  
> Check: `edu:count:tablet-marks:3`
> _For grown-ups: Counted three Roman strokes on the tablet and worked out nine remain._

**Obelix**: He was nice too.  
**Caius Pupus**: They are all nice. That is the trouble. Nobody nice ever wins anything for Rome.  

### Glossary

- **demonstrating**: Showing how something is done.

## A8-5. The Isle of Pleasure

- `a08-05-s1`: Row across to the island. · _Take the oars. Do not let Obelix row alone, or you will go in circles._
- `a08-05-s2`: Get everybody back to the boat. · _Find your Nubbin and Dogmatix first. Obelix will take care of Asterix._

#### `reader:a08-05-boat` · [main](main.md#a08-05-boat)

_Task four is an island in a blue lake, with soft music coming over the water and a smell of honey cakes._  
**Caius Pupus**: The task is simple. Cross the island and leave it. Nobody ever has. They all stay.  
**Asterix**: Why?  
**Caius Pupus**: Because it is lovely. That is the whole difficulty.  
_Obelix rows with one oar and you row with the other. The boat goes forward, mostly, and sideways the rest of the time._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, notice): why boats float: balanced forces  
> _The boat floats because water pushes up on it. Gravity pulls down, and the water’s push balances it._  
> _A boat is shaped to push aside a lot of water, which gives a big upward push._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a floating boat is held up by water’s upward push, balanced against gravity._

_Dogmatix stands at the front like a figurehead. Your Nubbin leans beside him into a wind it is not really getting._  

#### `reader:a08-05-boat-done` · [main](main.md#a08-05-boat-done)

_The boat bumps onto white sand. There are cushions on the beach, left there on purpose._  
_A ripple laps at the boat. “Aqua” is the Latin for water, Pupus says, and it is why you have an aquarium._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): English words from the Latin “aqua”  
> _“Aqua” is Latin for water. Which of these English words has “aqua” in it?_  
> - ✅ Aquarium → Yes. An aquarium is a place for water animals. “Aquatic” also comes from “aqua”.
> - Aeroplane → “Aero” means air, not water. “Aquarium” is the one that starts with “aqua”.

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): reading and adding a Roman numeral  
> _The last mark is XII. Adding its parts, which number does it stand for?_  
> - ✅ Twelve: 10 + 1 + 1 → Yes. X is ten, then two I strokes add two. Ten, one and one make twelve.
> - Eleven: 10 + 1 → That is XI. XII has two strokes after the X, so ten plus one plus one is twelve.

> _The form says, “Attach a completed copy of this form.” Each copy needs another copy attached. Why can it never be finished?_  
> - ✅ Every copy needs another copy first → Yes. Each form waits for another form, which waits for another, so there is never a first one finished. That is a loop with no way out.
