# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A10: The Heart of the Island

## A10-1. The Chosen One

- `a10-01-s1`: Step onto the island and find the voice telling a story. · _Touch the page, then follow the children’s laughter to the open house._
- `a10-01-s2`: Hear the rest of the story, the frightening bit included. · _Stay in the shade with the children until the story is done._
- `a10-01-s3`: Watch over the baby turtle on the beach. · _Go down to the waterline where the toddler has escaped to._

#### `reader:a10-01-page` · [main](main.md#a10-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page washed in sea-blue. There is an island in the middle of it, very green, ringed with white._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, 2-ESS2-2, notice): the Pacific is the largest ocean; maps show land and water  
> _Nova’s page is a map of the Pacific Ocean. The Pacific is the largest ocean on Earth, bigger than all the world’s land put together._  
> _Thousands of islands are scattered across it. Motunui is one tiny green dot among them._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Pacific is the largest ocean and holds thousands of islands._

_The paint goes warm under your hand, then wet. Then it is sand, and you and your Nubbin are standing in it._  
_Behind the palms there is a house with no walls, only a roof on posts. A crowd of small children sits inside it._  
_An old woman is telling them a story with her hands. A painted cloth hangs behind her: an island shaped like a sleeping woman._  
**Gramma Tala**: Before the palms, before the fish, there was the sea and nothing else. Then the mother island came up out of it.  
**Gramma Tala**: Her heart could make things grow. She gave that away to every island she could reach. Green, and more green, and more.  
_She spots you at the edge of the shade, and doesn’t miss a beat. She just shuffles up to make room._  
**Gramma Tala**: Sit, sit. Somebody from past the reef. We don’t get many. Your little one can sit on the end.  
_Your Nubbin sits on the end, beside a toddler with a great deal of hair. The toddler pats it, firmly, like a drum._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): what a simile compares  
> _Your Nubbin is patted “firmly, like a drum”. What does that simile tell us about the way the toddler pats?_  
> - ✅ Firmly, with a steady beat, as if she were drumming → Yes. A drum is struck firmly and in rhythm, so the simile gives us the sound and the feel of her pats.
> - Very gently, like stroking a cat → A drum is hit, not stroked. The simile means firm, steady pats.
> - She is not patting it at all → She is patting it, and the simile compares her pats to drumming.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out what a simile tells us about how a character acts._


#### `reader:a10-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a10-01-page-done)

**Gramma Tala**: Now. Where was I? The heart. Everyone wanted it. And one of them came to take it.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): thinking about why people take what is not theirs  
> **Gramma Tala**: Everyone wanted the heart. And one of them came to take it.  
> _Why might someone take something that was never theirs to take? Think it over._  
> - They were greedy → Greed is one reason. Keep that in mind: later, the story gives a different one.
> - They wanted to be loved or admired → That is a deep answer. People sometimes do wrong things to feel they matter.
> - They did not think anyone would mind → That happens too. Not asking is easier than finding out what others think.
> _For grown-ups: Gave a reason someone might take what is not theirs._


#### `reader:a10-01-story` · [main](main.md#a10-01-story)

**Gramma Tala**: A trickster. A shapeshifter, with a great fish hook the gods gave him. Maui.  
**Gramma Tala**: He took the heart, and the mother island began to crumble. As he fled, a demon of earth and fire rose up after him.  
_Most of the children squeal and hide behind each other. The toddler leans forward, delighted._  
**Gramma Tala**: Maui was struck from the sky, and the heart and his hook fell into the sea. Nobody has found them since.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): oral history: knowledge kept by telling  
> _Gramma Tala tells this story by heart. For centuries, Pacific peoples kept stories, family histories and star paths by learning them and telling them aloud._  
> _Each generation had to listen carefully, because the knowledge lived only in people’s memories._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Pacific peoples kept their history by telling it aloud, generation to generation._

**Gramma Tala**: And the darkness is still spreading, island by island. One day it will reach us.  
_A tall man ducks in under the roof, with the face of a father who has heard this story before._  
**Chief Tui**: Mother. That’s enough. They won’t sleep for a week.  
**Chief Tui**: There is no darkness, and nobody here needs to cross the reef. We have fish, we have coconuts, we have each other. That’s plenty.  
_The children scatter. The toddler is carried off under his arm, upside down, still waving at your Nubbin._  
**Gramma Tala**: He’s my son, and he’s a good chief. He’s also wrong. Both at once. You’ll find that’s quite common.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): being good and wrong at once  
> _Gramma Tala says her son is a good chief and also wrong, both at once. Can that be true of a person?_  
> - Yes. Good people can be wrong sometimes → That is a kind way to see it. A mistake does not cancel a person’s goodness.
> - No. A good person is never wrong → That is a strong view. Think of a time a good person you know made a mistake.
> - It depends what they are wrong about → A fair point. Some mistakes are small, and some cost a great deal.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether a good person can be wrong._


#### `reader:a10-01-story-done` · [main](main.md#a10-01-story-done)

_Gramma Tala picks up a stick and walks off towards the shore, humming. She doesn’t look back to see if you follow, and you do._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): what actions show about a character  
> _Gramma Tala walks off without checking that you are following. What does that tell us about her?_  
> - ✅ She trusts you and expects you to come along → Yes. She does not look back because she is sure you will follow, and actions can show trust.
> - She has forgotten you are there → She noticed you at once, earlier. Not looking back is a sign of trust, not forgetting.
> - She wants to be left alone → Then she would not have taken you in. She expects you to follow.
> _For grown-ups: Read a character’s action to work out what she trusts._


#### `reader:a10-01-turtle` · [main](main.md#a10-01-turtle)

_The toddler has got loose again. She is down on the wet sand, frowning at something small and flat._  
_It is a baby turtle, trying to reach the water. Seabirds circle above it, with ideas._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, notice): a sea turtle’s life cycle  
> _Sea turtles lay eggs in nests dug in warm sand. When the babies hatch, they crawl to the sea by themselves, usually at night._  
> _Only a few survive to be adults. Birds and crabs eat many on the way, which is why the toddler’s leaf-roof matters._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how sea turtles begin life: eggs in sand, then a dangerous crawl to the sea._

_The toddler pulls a big leaf off a bush and holds it over the turtle like a roof. She walks beside it, step by step._  

**Choices**

- **Send your Nubbin to walk on the other side.**
    - Your Nubbin waddles along the other side of the turtle, holding up its arms. Together they make a very small, very slow parade.
- **Wave the birds away.**
    - You flap your arms at the birds, and your Nubbin flaps its tiny wings. The birds decide, on balance, to go and bother someone else.

#### `reader:a10-01-turtle-done` · [main](main.md#a10-01-turtle-done)

_The turtle reaches the foam and is gone. The toddler claps, and so does your Nubbin, mostly missing._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, notice): caring for wildlife responsibly  
> _In the story the toddler helps. In real life, hatchlings need to crawl to the sea on their own, and people should not pick them up._  
> _What does help is keeping beach lights off at night, because bright lights can lead hatchlings away from the water._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how people really help baby sea turtles: leave them be and keep lights off._


#### `reader:a10-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a10-01-complete)

_Then the sea pulls back from the toddler’s feet, and keeps pulling, and stands up in a wall of clear water._  
_Fish hang in it like flecks in glass. The wall leans down to look at her, the way a dog leans down to a puppy._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): what a simile tells us  
> _The water “leans down to look at her, the way a dog leans down to a puppy.” What does this tell you about the ocean?_  
> - ✅ It is huge and gentle, and curious about her → Yes. A big dog leaning to a puppy is careful and interested, so the ocean seems the same.
> - It is about to attack her → A dog leaning down to a puppy is friendly. The simile shows the ocean is gentle.
> - The ocean is really a dog → A simile compares two different things. The sea is like a big, kind dog, but it is still the sea.
> _For grown-ups: Used a simile to understand a character: the ocean._

_It sets a shell in her hands, then another. Then a small green stone with a spiral cut into it._  
**Chief Tui**: Moana! Moana, where are you?  
_The water falls back to being just the sea. The toddler runs to her father’s voice, and the green stone drops in the sand._  
_Gramma Tala bends, slowly, and picks it up. She tucks it into the shell at her neck._  
**Gramma Tala**: The sea picks who it picks, and never explains itself. Grandchildren are the same.  
_The page ripples. When it settles, the palms are taller, and some years have gone by._  

### Glossary

- **trickster**: A character in stories who plays tricks and breaks rules, sometimes to cause trouble and sometimes to help.
- **shapeshifter**: Someone in a story who can change into a different creature or shape.

## A10-2. The Reef

- `a10-02-s1`: Walk the village with the chief’s daughter, and see the coconut trees. · _Meet Moana by the chief’s house and go up to the grove._
- `a10-02-s2`: Go down to the lagoon, where the nets have come in empty. · _Find the fishermen at the boat shelter._
- `a10-02-s3`: Go out to the breakers with Moana, and bring her back in. · _Wade out to the reef edge and be ready to pull._

#### `reader:a10-02-coconuts` · [main](main.md#a10-02-coconuts)

_Moana is sixteen now, and is being taught to be chief, which mostly means people bringing her problems._  
**Moana**: You’re Gramma’s guest! She said you’d come back. She says that about everyone, but she was right this time.  
**Moana**: Come on. The coconut farmers want me. They always want me before breakfast.  
**Coconut farmer**: Look at this. Cracked one open this morning.  
_He holds out a coconut split in half. Inside, where it should be white, it is black and soft and smells of nothing good._  
**Coconut farmer**: Whole trees of them. Not just old trees. Young ones too.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-4, notice): plant disease spreads, and coconuts travel by sea  
> _Plant diseases can spread from tree to tree, which is why farmers clear sick trees and plant new ones on clean ground._  
> _Coconuts float, and sea currents carried them between islands. That is one way coconut palms reached so many shores._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that plant diseases spread, and that floating coconuts can cross the sea._

**Moana**: Then we clear those trees and plant a new grove over there. It’s good ground. We just haven’t used it.  
_The farmer nods slowly, which on Motunui means yes, and also means we’ll see._  

**Choices**

- **Offer to dig the first hole for the new grove.**
    - **Coconut farmer**: A guest who digs. Now I’ve seen everything.
    - You dig. Your Nubbin helps by sitting in the hole, to check it is the right size.
- **Ask how long the trees have been sick.**
    - **Coconut farmer**: Since the rains. It started on the far side and walked this way, a tree at a time.
    - Moana looks towards the far side of the island, and then past it, at the sea. She doesn’t say anything.

#### `reader:a10-02-coconuts-done` · [main](main.md#a10-02-coconuts-done)

**Moana**: That’s one. Next it’ll be the roofs, or the fishing. It’s always the fishing.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): noticing how someone else feels  
> _Moana is learning to be chief, and every problem lands on her. How might she be feeling, do you think?_  
> - Overwhelmed → A good guess. Trouble after trouble before breakfast is a lot to carry.
> - Proud to be trusted → That could be true too. People can feel proud and tired together.
> - Lonely, because nobody else can fix it → That is thoughtful. Being the one people turn to can feel lonely.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how a young leader might feel under pressure._


#### `reader:a10-02-lagoon` · [main](main.md#a10-02-lagoon)

_The fishing canoes are drawn up on the sand, their nets draped over them, dry. Nothing in them but weed._  
**Old fisherman**: Every day, fewer. Today, none. The lagoon’s been fished out, or the fish have gone somewhere.  
**Moana**: Different nets? The far side of the island?  
**Old fisherman**: Tried both. Nothing.  
_Moana looks past the lagoon, to where the waves break white on the reef. Past that, the sea is a darker blue._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-LS4-1, notice): what a reef and a lagoon are  
> _A reef is a ridge of rock or coral just under the sea’s surface. Waves break on it, so the water behind it, the lagoon, stays calm._  
> _That calm water is a safe nursery for young fish, and a good place to fish from a canoe._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how a reef shelters a calm lagoon._

**Moana**: Then we go out past the reef. The fish must be out there somewhere.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Eco.1.K-2, ask): scarcity means choosing  
> _When there are fewer fish, the village has to make choices. Which of these is a choice caused by scarcity?_  
> - ✅ Sharing the few fish fairly, or looking for new fishing grounds → Yes. When something runs short, people must decide how to share it or where to find more.
> - Choosing what colour to paint a canoe → A paint colour is a choice, but the fish running short does not cause it.
> - Pretending there is no problem → Ignoring it is not a plan. Scarcity means people must choose how to cope.
> _For grown-ups: Linked fewer fish to the choices a village must make._

_The fishermen go very quiet. One of them looks over your shoulder, where the chief has just walked up._  
**Chief Tui**: We don’t cross the reef.  
**Chief Tui**: We have one rule, and it keeps us safe. You know it. You’ll be chief. You’ll keep it too.  
_Moana opens her mouth, and closes it again. Your Nubbin, sensing a mood, sits on her foot._  

#### `reader:a10-02-lagoon-done` · [main](main.md#a10-02-lagoon-done)

_The chief walks off. Moana watches the reef for a long time, chewing the inside of her cheek._  
**Moana**: He didn’t say why. He never says why.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.OA.C.5, ask): a rule that halves each time  
> _Say the lagoon’s catch fell over four days like this: 48 fish, 24, 12, 6. What is the rule, and what comes next?_  
> - ✅ Halve it each day, so 3 fish next → Yes. 48 ÷ 2 = 24, 24 ÷ 2 = 12, 12 ÷ 2 = 6, so 6 ÷ 2 = 3.
> - Take away 6 each day, so 0 fish next → Check it: 48 − 6 is 42, not 24. The amount lost shrinks each day, so the rule is halving.
> - Take away 12 each day → 48 − 12 is 36, not 24. Halving matches every step: the next number is 3.
> _For grown-ups: Found a halving rule in a number pattern and extended it._


#### `reader:a10-02-breakers` · [main](main.md#a10-02-breakers)

_An hour later, Moana is pushing a small canoe off the sand, with a spotted pig sitting in the front of it._  
**Moana**: That’s Pua. He’s coming for luck. Don’t tell anyone. I’m only going to look.  
_She paddles well in the lagoon. At the reef, the waves come in bigger and closer, one after another, like a crowd at a door._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): coral reefs are built by tiny animals  
> _Coral looks like rock or a plant, but a reef is built by animals. Each coral polyp is tiny, with a soft body and tentacles._  
> _A polyp makes a hard limestone skeleton around itself. Over thousands of years, billions of polyps build a reef big enough to stop ocean waves._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that coral reefs are built by tiny animals called polyps._

_The third wave lifts the canoe, turns it over, and throws it back towards the beach like a leaf._  
**You**: Moana!  
_You wade in up to your chest. Your Nubbin paddles past you, much faster, its tiny wings going like a fan._  
_Between you, you get hold of her arm, and then Pua, who is very much not enjoying this._  

#### `reader:a10-02-breakers-done` · [main](main.md#a10-02-breakers-done)

_Moana sits on the sand with a cut on her foot and the canoe in two pieces beside her. Pua stands well up the beach, sulking._  
**Moana**: Fine. Fine. Point taken.  
_Your Nubbin comes out of the shallows last. In its mouth is a small white shell with a spiral on it, like a tiny staircase._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): what a simile compares  
> _The shell has a spiral “like a tiny staircase.” What do a spiral shell and a staircase have in common?_  
> - ✅ Both wind round and round, step by step, to a centre → Yes. A spiral turns round a middle point, as a spiral staircase does.
> - Both are made of wood → Not wood. The simile is about the shape: winding round and round.
> - Both are cold → Temperature is not the point. They share a winding shape.
> _For grown-ups: Explained what a shell and a staircase have in common in a simile._


#### `reader:a10-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a10-02-complete)

**Moana**: He’s right. I nearly drowned the pig. Maybe I’m meant to stay, and be a good chief, and keep the rule.  
_She looks at the cut on her foot, and not at the reef. It takes some effort._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): what helps after a failure  
> _Moana failed, and now she wonders whether she should give up. What helps most after a failure?_  
> - ✅ Rest, work out what went wrong, and try again with help → Yes. Failing teaches, and trying again with a plan and a helper is how people get better.
> - Never try again → Giving up keeps you safe, but it also stops you learning.
> - Blame someone else → Blaming feels easier, but it does not show you what to change.
> _For grown-ups: Talked about learning from a failure and trying again._

_Your Nubbin drops the spiral shell in your hand, very pleased with itself. You put it somewhere safe._  
_Down the beach, out of sight round the point, somebody is laughing on her own, and splashing._  
**Moana**: That’ll be Gramma. She dances with the sea. Everybody thinks she’s odd. She says that’s their business.  

### Glossary

- **grove**: A small group of trees growing together.
- **lagoon**: A calm stretch of shallow sea water, often sheltered by a reef or a ring of coral.
- **reef**: A ridge of rock or coral near the surface of the sea, where waves break.
- **polyp**: A tiny coral animal with a soft body and tentacles that builds a hard skeleton around itself.

## A10-3. Gramma Tala

- `a10-03-s1`: Find Gramma Tala dancing at the water’s edge. · _Walk round the point, to where the waves come in long and low._
- `a10-03-s2`: Let Gramma Tala teach your Nubbin one step. · _Stand in the shallows with your Nubbin and choose a step._
- `a10-03-s3`: Sit on the sand with Gramma and Moana while the tide turns. · _Sit down beside Gramma Tala on the dry sand._

#### `reader:a10-03-point` · [main](main.md#a10-03-point)

_Round the point, the waves come in long and slow. Gramma Tala is dancing in the edge of them, in a skirt that is soaked to the knee._  
_She moves the way the water moves. Forward when it comes in, back when it goes, as if the two of them had rehearsed._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, notice): waves move energy, not water  
> _Wind pushing on the sea makes waves. A wave carries energy across the water, but the water itself mostly just moves up and down in small circles._  
> _That is why a floating coconut bobs on a wave and does not rush ashore with it._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that waves carry energy while the water mostly bobs in place._

_On her back, from shoulder to shoulder, is a tattoo of a manta ray. It is old, and the lines have softened, and it is beautiful._  
**Gramma Tala**: Don’t stand there like a post. The sea doesn’t bite. Not today.  
**Moana**: Gramma, you shouldn’t be out here on your own.  
**Gramma Tala**: I’m not on my own. I’ve got the sea, and a guest, and a round little thing with wings. That’s a crowd.  
**Gramma Tala**: Mantas are my favourites. When I’m finished here, that’s what I’ll be. I’ve told everyone. They don’t have to go to meetings.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): what humour shows about a character  
> _Gramma says that when she is a manta, she will not have to go to meetings. What does this show about her?_  
> - ✅ She is playful and not afraid of growing old → Yes. She can joke about the future, which shows she is at peace with it.
> - She is cross with everyone → Her tone is warm and teasing, not cross.
> - She wants to leave the island → She is joking about what she will be, not about leaving.
> _For grown-ups: Used a character’s joke to understand her outlook._

**Moana**: Gramma!  

#### `reader:a10-03-point-done` · [main](main.md#a10-03-point-done)

_Your Nubbin has crept down into the foam and is trying to copy her. It mostly falls over, very sincerely._  
**Gramma Tala**: Oh, now. That one has the makings.  

> **Lesson** (art, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): dance carries stories  
> _Across the Pacific, dance is more than fun. It is a way of telling stories, remembering people and showing respect for the land and sea._  
> _Gramma’s dance with the waves is part of that long tradition, in the story’s own invented way._  
> _For grown-ups: Heard that dance can tell stories and keep memories alive across Pacific cultures._


#### `reader:a10-03-step` · [main](main.md#a10-03-step)

**Gramma Tala**: Come here, small one. Feet in the water. Feel it pull? That’s the sea saying hello.  
_Your Nubbin stands in the foam, rocking a little each time it goes out._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, do): balance: forces and leaning  
> _(Stand with your feet apart. Lean slowly to one side until you feel you might wobble, then come back to centre.)_  
> _You stay up while your weight is over your feet. Lean too far, and the forces are unbalanced, so you fall._  
> Check: `edu:balance:lean:hold`
> _For grown-ups: Felt the balance point when leaning, and learned why leaning too far makes you fall._

**Gramma Tala**: I’ve got two steps for a beginner. One for the manta, one for the wave. You only get one today. I’m old, my knees are older.  
**Gramma Tala**: Which shall it be?  

**Choices**

- **The stingray glide.**
    - **Gramma Tala**: Arms out wide, and soft. Lean, and let the sea carry you sideways. Don’t flap. Mantas never flap. They fly in the water.
    - Your Nubbin spreads its arms, leans, and slides along the foam on one foot, perfectly level, for very nearly a second.
- **The wave step.**
    - **Gramma Tala**: Up on your toes when it comes in. Down and back when it goes. Roll your arms like the water rolls over itself.
    - Your Nubbin rises, sinks, rises, rolling its little arms, a beat behind the sea and then, suddenly, right on it.

#### `reader:a10-03-step-done` · [main](main.md#a10-03-step-done)

**Gramma Tala**: There. That’s yours now. A step’s like a name. Once somebody’s taught it to you, it doesn’t come off.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): what a simile means  
> _Gramma says, “A step’s like a name. Once somebody’s taught it to you, it doesn’t come off.” What does she mean?_  
> - ✅ What you are given with love belongs to you for good → Yes. A name, or a step, is yours once someone gives it to you.
> - Names are written in ink → She does not mean ink. “Doesn’t come off” means it cannot be taken away.
> - You must never learn another step → She is describing how teaching lasts, not forbidding new things.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the meaning of a simile about learning._


#### `reader:a10-03-sand` · [main](main.md#a10-03-sand)

_Gramma sits down on the dry sand with a noise like a sack of shells. Moana sits beside her, and you on the other side._  
**Moana**: I tried to go past the reef this morning. I nearly drowned Pua.  
**Gramma Tala**: I know. I saw. You went out on the wrong side, in the wrong boat, at the wrong tide. Otherwise, very good.  
**Moana**: Dad says the reef keeps us safe.  
**Gramma Tala**: It does. Safe is a good thing. It isn’t the only thing.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): is safe the same as well?  
> _Gramma says safe is a good thing, but not the only thing. Is being safe the same as being well?_  
> - Safe is what matters most → Staying safe is very important. Think about what you would miss if you stayed safe all the time.
> - Safe can mean missing things that make life good → That is what Gramma seems to mean: a very small life is safe, but may not be a full one.
> - Both matter, and it depends on the risk → A wise answer. Good decisions weigh the danger against what is gained.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether being safe is the same as being well._

**Gramma Tala**: Your father once had a reason for his rule. You should ask him for it, one day. Gently.  
_She looks at Moana for a long moment, as if she is weighing something in her hand._  
**Gramma Tala**: You’ve got a question in you the size of a canoe. You always have. Ask it.  

#### `reader:a10-03-sand-done` · [main](main.md#a10-03-sand-done)

**Moana**: Who we are. Where we came from. Why we never leave.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): genealogies: knowing who we come from  
> _Moana asks who they are and where they came from. Pacific peoples answered with genealogy: a spoken record of ancestors, back to the first._  
> _Reciting a genealogy told you where you belonged, and who had come before you._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Pacific peoples used spoken genealogies to say who they are and where they come from._

**Gramma Tala**: Good. Get your guest. Bring a torch. It’s a walk.  

#### `reader:a10-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a10-03-complete)

_Gramma gets up, which takes three tries and a hand from you. She does not mention the hand._  
_Your Nubbin walks behind her up the beach, still doing its step, a little crooked, every few paces._  
**Gramma Tala**: It’ll come. Everything worth having comes slow. Mantas, grandchildren, steps.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiply to find a total  
> _Gramma says everything worth having comes slowly. If your Nubbin practises its step for 5 minutes a day for 12 days, how many minutes is that?_  
> - ✅ 60 minutes, which is one hour → Yes: 12 × 5 = 60, and 60 minutes make one hour. Slow practice adds up.
> - 17 minutes → 17 is 12 + 5. Each of the 12 days has 5 minutes, so multiply: 12 × 5 = 60.
> - 50 minutes → Close: 10 days would be 50. With 12 days it is 12 × 5 = 60.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied equal daily amounts of practice time and converted to hours._

> **Lesson** (science, 5-ESS1-2, notice): the star compass: stars rise and set at fixed points  
> _This skill is called wayfinding. A navigator pictures the horizon as a ring of “houses”, and the Hawaiian star compass has 32 of them._  
> _Each star rises in its own house and sets in another, in the same places every night. Learn the houses and you can tell direction._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that stars rise and set at fixed points on the horizon, which wayfinders use as a compass._

> - About 13 degrees → 13 is 3 + 10. Each fist is 10 degrees, so three fists are 3 × 10 = 30.
