# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A11: The Enchanted Place

## A11-1. A Bear on the Stairs

- `a11-01-s1`: Step into the pencil Forest and meet the boy at its edge. · _Touch the page, then walk up the path to the boy with the bear._
- `a11-01-s2`: Hold the balloon string while Pooh floats up to the bees. · _Stand under the tall oak and keep both hands on the string._
- `a11-01-s3`: Help get Pooh down again, without laughing. · _Keep the string steady while Christopher Robin aims his popgun._

#### `reader:a11-01-page` · [main](main.md#a11-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page drawn in soft pencil: a stream, a bridge, and a hill with a ring of trees on top._  
_Across the top there is writing, in slow, careful print._  
_It is the start of a story about a bear who comes downstairs in a very bumpy way._  
_The pencil goes soft under your fingers, then softer. Then it is pine needles, and you and your Nubbin are standing in them._  
_A boy is coming up the path with a bear under his arm, upside down. Neither of them seems to mind._  
**Christopher Robin**: Hallo. You’re not from the Forest. I know everybody in the Forest.  
**Christopher Robin**: This is Pooh. He came down the stairs on his head, so he may be a bit muddled. He usually is anyway.  
**Pooh**: How do you do. Is that a Heffalump?  
**Christopher Robin**: No, Pooh.  
**Pooh**: A very small one, I expect. They start small. Everything does.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): saying “I don’t know” instead of guessing  
> _Pooh is sure your Nubbin is a very small Heffalump, though nobody knows what a Heffalump is. Is it better to say “I don’t know”, or to guess?_  
> - Say “I don’t know” → That’s honest, and it leaves room to find out. Pooh’s sort of guess is more fun, but yours is safer.
> - Make a good guess → A guess can be a fine start, if you say it is a guess. Pooh says his like a fact, and that is where it gets tricky.
> - It depends on the question → That is a thoughtful answer. Some questions need care, and some are only a game.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about when it is better to say “I don’t know” than to guess._

_Your Nubbin looks behind itself, in case there is a Heffalump there._  

**Choices**

- **Tell Pooh it’s a Nubbin.**
    - **Pooh**: A Nubbin. Yes. That’s just what a very small Heffalump would say.
- **Let your Nubbin decide.**
    - Your Nubbin sits on Pooh’s foot. Pooh decides that is a friendly thing for a Heffalump to do, and lets it.
- **Ask what a Heffalump is.**
    - **Christopher Robin**: Nobody knows exactly. That’s the best sort of animal.

#### `reader:a11-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a11-01-page-done)

**Pooh**: I was just going to see about some honey. You can come, if you like.  
**Pooh**: Honey always goes better with somebody watching.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, notice): a character’s motive is the reason they act  
> **Christopher Robin**: A motive is the reason a character does something. Pooh’s motive for going out today is honey.  
> **Christopher Robin**: Knowing a character’s motive helps you guess what they will do next. Try it on the bees.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a motive is the reason a character does something._


#### `reader:a11-01-balloon` · [main](main.md#a11-01-balloon)

_High in an oak there is a buzzing. Pooh stops so suddenly that your Nubbin walks into the back of him._  
**Pooh**: That is a honey sort of buzz. I’d know it anywhere.  
**Pooh**: The trouble with honey is that it’s always at the top of things. Christopher Robin, have you got such a thing as a balloon?  
**Christopher Robin**: I’ve got two. A green one and a blue one. What do you want a balloon for?  
**Pooh**: Honey.  
**Pooh**: The blue one, please. Bees won’t mind a bit of blue sky. And I shall roll in the mud, so I look like a small black cloud.  
_He does, very thoroughly. Your Nubbin rolls in the mud too, in case it’s a rule._  
**Christopher Robin**: You hold the string. Don’t let go unless he says. And don’t laugh. It hurts his feelings.  
_Up goes Pooh, past the branches, round and black and very hopeful. He stops at exactly the height of the nest._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): balanced forces: a balloon pulling up, gravity pulling down  
> _Pooh hangs in the air, neither rising nor falling. The balloon pulls him up and gravity pulls him down. What do we call it when the pulls are equal?_  
> - ✅ Balanced forces → Yes. Equal pulls in opposite directions cancel out, so Pooh stays put.
> - Unbalanced forces → Not quite. With unbalanced forces he would be rising or falling. He is still, so the pulls are equal.
> - No forces at all → Forces are still there. Gravity never switches off. They are just equal, so he does not move.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that equal forces pulling in opposite directions are balanced and cause no movement._


#### `reader:a11-01-balloon-done` · [main](main.md#a11-01-balloon-done)

_The string tugs in your hands like a kite that has been told about honey._  
**Pooh**: Christopher Robin? These bees are looking at me in a very suspicious sort of way.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.2.4, notice): using context to work out a new word: suspicious  
> **Christopher Robin**: Suspicious means not trusting someone, and thinking they may be up to something. The bees are quite right to be.  
> **Christopher Robin**: You can often work out a new word from what is around it. A muddy “cloud” has come for their honey: no wonder they look at it oddly.  
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the word suspicious from context._


#### `reader:a11-01-bees` · [main](main.md#a11-01-bees)

**Pooh**: Would you walk up and down with your umbrella, Christopher Robin, and say it looks like rain? Then they’ll believe I’m a cloud.  
_Christopher Robin walks up and down under his umbrella, saying it looks like rain. It doesn’t, and the bees know._  
**Pooh**: I think these are the wrong sort of bees. They would make the wrong sort of honey. I shall come down now.  
**Christopher Robin**: How?  
_Pooh hadn’t thought about that. He holds on, with the face of a bear doing a hard sum._  
**Pooh**: If you were to shoot the balloon with your popgun, I should come down. Gently. I hope.  
**Christopher Robin**: I might miss.  
**Pooh**: You might. Try not to.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): what to do when a laugh hurts someone  
> _Christopher Robin says laughing hurts Pooh’s feelings, then admits he laughed too. What is the best thing to do if you laugh and someone is hurt?_  
> - ✅ Say sorry, and mean it → Yes. Funny things can still sting. A real sorry mends it faster than anything.
> - Pretend you did not laugh → That tends to make it worse. The hurt is still there, and now it is a secret too.
> - Say it was only a joke → “Only a joke” does not take the sting away. Saying sorry does.
> _For grown-ups: Chose a real apology after a laugh hurt someone’s feelings._


**Choices**

- **Bite your lip and hold steady.**
    - You bite your lip, and the string stays steady. Pooh looks down at you gratefully.
- **Laugh, a little, into your sleeve.**
    - **Pooh**: I heard that.
    - **Christopher Robin**: It’s all right. I laughed the first time too. He forgave me by lunch.

#### `reader:a11-01-bees-done` · [main](main.md#a11-01-bees-done)

_Pop goes the popgun. “Ow!” says Pooh._  
**Christopher Robin**: Did I miss?  
**Pooh**: You hit something. It wasn’t the balloon.  
_The second cork gets the balloon. Pooh floats down into your arms like a large, muddy leaf._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, notice): a simile compares two things using like or as  
> _“Pooh floats down into your arms like a large, muddy leaf.” That is a simile: it compares two things using “like” or “as”._  
> _Try one of your own: “Pooh was as round as…” What would you pick?_  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a simile compares two things using like or as._


#### `reader:a11-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a11-01-complete)

_Pooh’s arms have held the string so long that they stay up in the air, like a bear about to be surprised._  
**Christopher Robin**: They’ll come down in a week or so. If a fly lands on his nose, he has to blow it off. Like that. Pooh!  
_Pooh blows a fly off his nose. He looks pleased to be good at something._  
**Christopher Robin**: You can come back, you know. There’s always something on in the Forest. Or nothing, which is better.  
_He ties a blue ribbon round your Nubbin’s middle, in a careful double bow._  
**Christopher Robin**: So everyone knows it’s a Friend. Otherwise Piglet will think it’s a Woozle, and then there’s no talking to him.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiplying days: 2 weeks of 7 days  
> _Christopher Robin says Pooh’s arms will come down in about a week. A week has 7 days. How many days is 2 weeks?_  
> - ✅ 14 days → Yes. Two groups of seven is 2 × 7 = 14.
> - 9 days → Check again: 2 weeks is 7 + 7, which is 14. That is 2 × 7.
> - 12 days → Close. Two weeks is 7 + 7 = 14 days.
> _For grown-ups: Used multiplication to work out the days in two weeks._


### Glossary

- **gravity**: The pull that drags everything towards the ground.
- **motive**: The reason a character does something.
- **suspicious**: Not trusting someone, and thinking they may be up to something.
- **simile**: A comparison that uses “like” or “as”, such as “as round as a ball”.

## A11-2. A Tight Place

- `a11-02-s1`: Go visiting with Pooh, to Rabbit’s. · _Follow Pooh along the sandbank to the hole in it._
- `a11-02-s2`: Fetch Christopher Robin to look at the stuck bear. · _Run up the path to Christopher Robin’s house, then walk him back._
- `a11-02-s3`: Read to Pooh while he gets thinner. · _Sit by Pooh’s head and pick something to read; the week passes as the story goes on._

#### `reader:a11-02-visit` · [main](main.md#a11-02-visit)

_Pooh is humming along the sandbank, which is what he does when he isn’t doing anything else._  
**Pooh**: A hole. A hole means Rabbit, and Rabbit means company, and company means a little something.  
**Pooh**: Hallo? Anybody in?  
**Rabbit**: Nobody’s in. Go away.  
**Pooh**: Then who said that?  
**Rabbit**: Nobody. I said it for him.  
_Rabbit puts his head out, sighs, and looks at your Nubbin._  
**Rabbit**: And what is that?  
**Pooh**: A very small Heffalump.  
**Rabbit**: It is not. It’s wearing a bow. Heffalumps don’t wear bows. Come in, then, both of you. Wipe your feet.  
_Rabbit gets out the honey. Pooh has a little, and then a little more, and then the rest, so it won’t feel left out._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-LS2-2, notice): bees carry pollen from flower to flower while making honey  
> **Christopher Robin**: To make honey, bees drink a sweet juice called nectar from flowers. A single worker makes only about a twelfth of a teaspoon in her whole life.  
> **Christopher Robin**: As she goes from flower to flower, pollen sticks to her. That is how bees help plants make seeds. We say they pollinate.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that bees make honey from nectar and pollinate flowers as they feed._

_Your Nubbin has one crumb. Rabbit watches it eat the crumb as if it might be the start of something._  

#### `reader:a11-02-visit-done` · [main](main.md#a11-02-visit-done)

**Rabbit**: Well. Must you be going? Already? What a shame.  
_Pooh starts out of the front door, and his front half gets out. The rest of him has had too much honey to follow._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): subtracting to find the gap: 45 − 30  
> _Rabbit’s door is 30 cm wide. Pooh, after all that honey, is 45 cm across. By how many centimetres is Pooh too wide?_  
> - ✅ 15 cm → Yes. 45 − 30 = 15. That is a lot of honey.
> - 75 cm → That is 45 + 30. To find the gap, take the smaller from the bigger: 45 − 30 = 15.
> - 5 cm → Try again: 45 − 30. Take away 3 tens to get 15.
> _For grown-ups: Subtracted two measurements to find how much wider Pooh was than the door._

**Pooh**: Oh, dear. I seem to have stopped.  

#### `reader:a11-02-stuck` · [main](main.md#a11-02-stuck)

_Pooh is stuck. His head and arms are out in the morning, and the rest of him is in Rabbit’s hall._  
**Pooh**: I can’t go forwards and I can’t go back. It’s all on account of the door being too narrow.  
**Rabbit**: It’s all on account of somebody eating too much honey. I’m only saying.  
**Rabbit**: You. Keeper. Go and get Christopher Robin. Run.  
_You run. Christopher Robin comes back at a sensible walk, because he has seen this sort of thing before._  
**Christopher Robin**: There’s only one thing for it. He’ll have to stay there until he’s thin again.  
**Pooh**: How long does getting thin take?  
**Christopher Robin**: About a week, I should think.  
**Pooh**: A week! What about meals?  
**Christopher Robin**: No meals. That’s rather the point.  
_A tear rolls down Pooh’s nose and drips on the sand. Your Nubbin sits in front of him, nose to nose._  
**Pooh**: Then would you read to me? Something comforting. A sustaining sort of book, for a Bear in a Tight Place.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): whether to say “I told you so” to someone in trouble  
> _Rabbit says it is all on account of somebody eating too much honey. He is right. But is it kind to say so to someone who is stuck?_  
> - Yes, it is only true → It is true. But true and helpful are not always the same thing. What does Pooh need most right now?
> - No, help first, talk later → Rabbit might agree, if he were not so cross. Help is the thing a stuck bear remembers.
> - It depends how you say it → A good point. A friendly “bit of a squeeze, isn’t it?” is very different from “I told you so”.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether being right justifies saying “I told you so”._


#### `reader:a11-02-stuck-done` · [main](main.md#a11-02-stuck-done)

**Christopher Robin**: We’ll take turns. You read in the mornings, and I’ll read in the afternoons.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.2.4, notice): sustaining means giving strength; context clues  
> **Christopher Robin**: Pooh asked for a sustaining sort of book. Sustaining means something that keeps you going, like a hearty soup.  
> **Christopher Robin**: Could you tell from the sentence? When a word is new, look at the words around it for clues.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the word sustaining and practised using context clues._


#### `reader:a11-02-week` · [main](main.md#a11-02-week)

_So for a week you sit on the sandbank and read to the North end of Pooh. Christopher Robin takes the afternoons._  
_At the South end, inside, Rabbit has hung his washing on Pooh’s back legs._  
**Rabbit**: It’s a waste of a perfectly good pair of legs otherwise. Do you mind, Pooh?  
**Pooh**: I don’t mind. I should like to know what’s on them, though.  
**Rabbit**: Tea towels.  
**Pooh**: Ah.  
_Your Nubbin brings Pooh an acorn to look at, since he isn’t allowed to eat anything._  
**Pooh**: What are we reading today?  

> **Lesson** (maths, do): a tally of the days of a week  
> **Christopher Robin**: A week has seven days. Let us keep a tally: one stroke for each day, four strokes standing up and the fifth across them like a gate.  
> **Christopher Robin**: Make one gate and two more strokes. That is seven, and Pooh is free on the seventh day.  
> Check: `edu:tally:days-of-week:7`
> _For grown-ups: Made a tally of seven, bundling the first five marks into a gate._


**Choices**

- **A story about a bear who was exactly the right size.**
    - **Pooh**: I like him already. Does he get stuck?
    - **You**: Never.
    - **Pooh**: Lucky bear.
- **A book about the sea, where there aren’t any doors.**
    - **Pooh**: No doors at all? That sounds like a very comfortable place for a bear.
- **Just sit with him. Don’t read anything.**
    - You sit by his head and say nothing at all.
    - **Pooh**: That’s the most sustaining one so far.

#### `reader:a11-02-week-done` · [main](main.md#a11-02-week-done)

_On the seventh day Christopher Robin takes Pooh’s front paws. Rabbit takes Christopher Robin._  
_Rabbit’s friends-and-relations take Rabbit, all of them, in a long line. You take the end, and your Nubbin takes you._  
**Christopher Robin**: Pull!  
_For a long time nothing happens. Then, with a pop like a ginger-beer cork, out comes Pooh, and everyone sits down at once._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, notice): unbalanced forces: friction holds, a bigger pull wins  
> **Christopher Robin**: All week the door gripped Pooh tightly. That grip, between surfaces rubbing together, is called friction.  
> **Christopher Robin**: By the seventh day Pooh was thinner, so the grip was weaker, and the long line of friends pulled harder than it held. Unbalanced forces: out he popped.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that friction can hold something until a bigger pull makes it move._


#### `reader:a11-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a11-02-complete)

**Pooh**: Thank you. All of you. Especially the one at the end, who I couldn’t see but could feel.  
_Pooh nods to his friends and walks off into the Forest, humming, a little thinner and not at all wiser._  
**Rabbit**: He’ll be back. He knows where the honey is now. I shall have to get a wider door.  
_Rabbit looks at the hall where the legs were. He looks very nearly as if he misses them._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, notice): feelings can be mixed: glad and a little lonely  
> _Rabbit wanted Pooh out of his door. Now Pooh is out, and Rabbit looks as if he misses him._  
> _Two feelings at once is normal. You can be relieved and a bit lonely in the same minute._  
> _For grown-ups: Heard that people can feel two different things at once._


### Glossary

- **nectar**: The sweet juice inside flowers that bees collect.
- **pollinate**: To carry pollen from flower to flower so plants can make seeds.
- **sustaining**: Keeping you going and giving you strength, like a hearty meal.
- **friction**: The grip between two surfaces that rub against each other, which slows things down.

## A11-3. The Woozle

- `a11-03-s1`: Follow Pooh round the spinney after the mysterious tracks. · _Walk round the larch spinney in the snow behind Pooh and Piglet. Don’t spoil it._
- `a11-03-s2`: Watch from the oak with Christopher Robin until they work it out. · _Climb the big oak by the spinney and keep quiet._

#### `reader:a11-03-tracks` · [main](main.md#a11-03-tracks)

_It is a fine winter’s day. Piglet is brushing snow off his front step when Pooh goes past, round in a circle._  
**Piglet**: Pooh? Why are you going round and round?  
**Pooh**: Following something. I don’t know what yet. That’s the exciting part.  
**Pooh**: Look. Paw-marks. Something has been round here, and round, and I have been round after it.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): tracks tell us size, speed and direction  
> **Christopher Robin**: Animal trackers read prints like a story. The size shows how big the animal is, and the spacing shows how fast it moved.  
> **Christopher Robin**: A line of prints shows where it went. A circle of prints, though, shows somebody going round in circles.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that tracks show an animal’s size, speed and direction._

**Piglet**: Could it be… a Woozle?  
**Pooh**: It might. Woozles are very hard to tell from the front.  
_Piglet looks at your Nubbin, hard. Your Nubbin looks back with great innocence._  
**Piglet**: That one’s got a bow on. Christopher Robin s-said it was a Friend. But a Woozle would say that.  
_Your Nubbin trots after Pooh round the spinney of larches, putting its small feet very carefully in the snow._  

**Choices**

- **Tell them the little prints are your Nubbin’s.**
    - **Pooh**: Yes, yes. And these others are something else. Do keep up.
    - Nobody is listening. It is very hard to spoil a thing when nobody is listening.
- **Say nothing. Let it play out.**
    - You say nothing. It feels like carrying a very full cup, very still.
- **Let your Nubbin go round once more.**
    - Your Nubbin goes round once more, very pleased with itself. Now there is one more set of tracks.

#### `reader:a11-03-tracks-done` · [main](main.md#a11-03-tracks-done)

_Round they go. Every time they come back to the same place, there are more tracks than before._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.OA.C.5, ask): a pattern that follows a rule: adding 2 each lap  
> _Each lap round the larches, Pooh and Piglet each add one set of prints._  
> _After lap 1 there are 2 sets. After lap 2 there are 4. How many after lap 5?_  
> - ✅ 10 sets → Yes. The rule is add 2 each lap: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.
> - 8 sets → That is lap 4. Keep going one more: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.
> - 5 sets → That would be adding 1. The rule here is add 2 each lap, so lap 5 gives 10.
> _For grown-ups: Continued a number pattern by applying the rule add 2._


#### `reader:a11-03-count` · [main](main.md#a11-03-count)

**Pooh**: Look! Two of them now. And one very small. And a third, and something else besides.  
**Piglet**: Three Woozles and a Wizzle, perhaps. Pooh, I’ve just remembered something I meant to do yesterday. I’d better go and do it.  
_Up in a big oak, Christopher Robin is sitting very still. He beckons you up, and your Nubbin rides in your hood._  
**Christopher Robin**: Don’t say anything. He gets there in the end. He always does, and it’s better when he gets there himself.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): letting someone find out for themselves  
> _Christopher Robin says it is better when Pooh gets there himself. Would you have told Pooh the answer early? Why, or why not?_  
> - Yes, tell him early → That is kind, and it would save Pooh a lot of walking. But Christopher Robin thinks finding it himself is worth something.
> - No, let him find out → That is Christopher Robin’s view too. When you work it out yourself, you remember it.
> - Tell him if he asks → A fair middle path. Being ready to help without taking the discovery away from someone is a skill.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about helping someone without taking away their chance to discover._

_Pooh stops and looks at the tracks. He puts his own foot into one of them, and it fits._  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): a hypothesis is an idea you can test  
> **Christopher Robin**: Pooh has an idea: these prints belong to a Woozle. An idea you can test is called a hypothesis.  
> **Christopher Robin**: Pooh tests his by fitting his own foot in the print. It fits, so the hypothesis is in trouble.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a hypothesis is an idea that can be tested._

**Pooh**: Oh.  
_He puts his foot into another. It fits too._  
_Piglet, a little way off, looks at the tiny prints, then at your Nubbin’s feet, and goes pink._  

#### `reader:a11-03-count-done` · [main](main.md#a11-03-count-done)

_Christopher Robin climbs down from the tree._  
**Christopher Robin**: Oh, Pooh. Whose footprints do you think those are?  
**Pooh**: I have been going round and round after my own feet. I am a Bear with no Brain worth mentioning.  
**Christopher Robin**: There isn’t a better bear anywhere. I’d know.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, notice): kind words when someone feels silly  
> _Pooh feels silly: he followed his own feet. Christopher Robin does not laugh. He says something warm._  
> _When someone feels foolish, a kind sentence can turn the whole day around._  
> _For grown-ups: Heard how a kind reply helps someone who feels silly._


#### `reader:a11-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a11-03-complete)

**Pooh**: Being silly is very hungry work. Is it lunch?  
_Pooh and Christopher Robin go home together. Piglet walks a long way round your Nubbin, then, after a while, a shorter way._  
**Piglet**: I knew it wasn’t a Woozle. Mostly.  
_Your Nubbin holds out a paw. Piglet shakes it quickly, and then a second time, slower._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, notice): actions show a character’s feelings  
> _Piglet walks a long way round your Nubbin, then a shorter way, then shakes its paw twice. Nobody says he is less afraid._  
> _His actions tell you instead. Watching what characters do, not only what they say, is how readers find out how they feel._  
> _For grown-ups: Noticed that a character’s actions can show a change of feeling._


### Glossary

- **hypothesis**: An idea about how something works that you can test.

## A11-4. Eeyore’s Tail

- `a11-04-s1`: Find out what is wrong with Eeyore. · _Eeyore stands in the thistly corner of the Forest._
- `a11-04-s2`: Go with Pooh to ask Owl. · _Owl lives at The Chestnuts, high in a tree, behind the grandest door in the Forest._
- `a11-04-s3`: Hold the tail straight while Christopher Robin nails it back on. · _Carry the tail back to the thistles and hold it where Christopher Robin points._

#### `reader:a11-04-gloomy` · [main](main.md#a11-04-gloomy)

_Eeyore, the old grey donkey, stands by himself in a thistly corner of the Forest, with his head on one side._  
_Sometimes he thinks sadly about things. Sometimes he just thinks._  
**Pooh**: Good morning, Eeyore.  
**Eeyore**: Good morning, Pooh. If it is a good morning. Which I doubt.  
**Pooh**: Why, what’s the matter?  
**Eeyore**: Nothing. Nobody minds. Nobody ever does.  
_Pooh walks all the way round Eeyore. Then he walks round again, to be sure._  
**Pooh**: Eeyore. What has happened to your tail?  
_Eeyore turns slowly to look, and turns, and turns, and comes back to where he started._  
**Eeyore**: You’re right. It’s gone. That explains a great deal.  
**Eeyore**: Somebody must have taken it. How like them.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): gloomy does not always mean “leave me alone”  
> _Eeyore says “Nobody minds. Nobody ever does.” Does that mean he wants to be left alone, or something else?_  
> - He wants to be left alone → Sometimes people do. But Pooh asked, and then stayed, and Eeyore did not send him away.
> - He wants somebody to notice → That fits the story. “Nobody minds” is often a quiet way of hoping somebody does.
> - You can only find out by asking → A wise answer. Asking, and then staying close, is how Pooh does it.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what a gloomy friend might really need._


**Choices**

- **Promise you’ll help find it.**
    - **Eeyore**: Kind of you. Don’t trouble. I expect somebody is using it for something important.
- **Sit with him for a moment first.**
    - You sit in the thistles beside him, which are very prickly. Eeyore notices, and doesn’t say so, which is a sort of thank you.
- **Look behind the thistles.**
    - Your Nubbin looks behind the thistles, under a stone and inside its own bow. No tail.

#### `reader:a11-04-gloomy-done` · [main](main.md#a11-04-gloomy-done)

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.C.7, ask): area of a rectangle  
> _Pooh wants the pit floor to be 3 metres long and 2 metres wide. What is its area?_  
> - ✅ 6 square metres → Yes. Area of a rectangle is length × width: 3 × 2 = 6.
> - 5 square metres → That is 3 + 2. For area, multiply: 3 × 2 = 6.
> - 10 square metres → That is the distance round the edge (perimeter). Area is 3 × 2 = 6.
> _For grown-ups: Found the area of a rectangle by multiplying length by width._

> **Lesson** (science, notice): signs animals leave: a hare’s form  
> **Christopher Robin**: A flattened round in the grass is a sign that somebody has rested there. A hare’s resting place is even called a form.  
> **Christopher Robin**: Naturalists read signs like these: flattened grass, feathers, tracks and droppings tell them who has been there, and when.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that flattened grass is a sign an animal has rested there._

_Nova’s atlas lies open at the last page of the Forest, where the ring of trees stands on top of the hill._  
_Beside the trees, in soft pencil you have never noticed, there is a note in the margin. You know the hand._  
_“Read this to the little one, the summer they stayed. They asked where Christopher Robin went.”_  
_“Somewhere, I said. Not away.”_  
_And under it, one letter: H._  
_You don’t remember that summer. Or you do, a little: a lap, a voice, a page turning._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, notice): writing keeps people close  
> _Hazel wrote this note long ago, for a child who would read it later. And here it is, reaching you._  
> _We are made partly of being remembered. A note, a drawing or a letter can keep someone close, long after the pencil has stopped._  
> _For grown-ups: Heard that a written note can keep someone close._
