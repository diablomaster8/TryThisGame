# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A12: The Mirror Shard

## A12-1. The Troll’s Mirror

- `a12-01-s1`: Step into the cut-paper town and take the room under the eaves. · _Touch the page, then climb the attic stairs to Grandmother._
- `a12-01-s2`: Hold the candle for Grandmother’s shadow-show. · _Stand by the attic wall and keep the candle still._
- `a12-01-s3`: Watch the troll’s mirror break. · _Keep the candle steady to the end of the show._

#### `reader:a12-01-arrive` · [main](main.md#a12-01-arrive)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page of cut paper: rooftops, chimneys, and two attic windows with a gutter between them._  
_The paper is so fine it stirs when you breathe. Then it’s a steep wooden stair, and you’re on it, with a bag and your Nubbin._  
**Grandmother**: There you are. The lodger. You’ll want the room under the eaves, and the bed by the chimney.  
**Grandmother**: Mind your head on the beam. Everybody hits it, the first week.  
_Grandmother is small and quick, with her spectacles pushed up into white hair._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, ask): where Denmark is  
> _This town is in Denmark, a small country in northern Europe. It is one long peninsula and hundreds of islands._  
> _Denmark’s peninsula is joined by land to only one other country. Which one?_  
> - ✅ Germany, to the south → That’s right. Walk south out of Denmark and you’re in Germany. Every other way, there’s sea.
> - Australia → Australia is on the other side of the world! Denmark’s only land neighbour is Germany, to the south.
> - No country at all → Most of Denmark is islands, so that’s a good guess. But the peninsula touches Germany, to the south.
> _For grown-ups: Found Denmark on the map of Europe and learned it borders Germany._

_In the window across the gutter, two faces appear: a boy and a girl, both trying to see you at once._  
**Kay**: Is that the new lodger? Has it got a dog?  
**Gerda**: That’s not a dog, Kay. Look at its ears.  
**Grandmother**: And what’s this? It isn’t a cat. I’ve had cats.  

**Choices**

- **Tell her it’s a Nubbin.**
    - **Grandmother**: A Nubbin. Well, it can have the end of the bed and the heel of the loaf, same as a cat.
- **Say it’s your friend, and it goes where you go.**
    - **Grandmother**: Good. Things that go where you go are worth a bed.
- **Let your Nubbin bow.**
    - Your Nubbin bows, nearly falls down the stairs, and is caught by Grandmother without her looking.
    - **Grandmother**: Manners. It can stay.

#### `reader:a12-01-arrive-done` · [main](main.md#a12-01-arrive-done)

**Grandmother**: That’s Kay and Gerda. They live across the gutter, one each side, and they’re in here more than they’re out.  
**Grandmother**: Evenings, there’s a story. You can hold the candle. Lodgers always hold the candle.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): welcoming someone new  
> _Grandmother gives you a job on your first day. Why might that help a newcomer feel at home?_  
> - A job means you belong → That’s a fair answer. When you’re needed, you’re one of us.
> - It stops you feeling shy → It often does. It’s easier to talk when your hands are busy.
> - It doesn’t help at all → Maybe not for everyone. Then a friendly word and a warm bed do the rest.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about the ways people make a newcomer feel welcome._


#### `reader:a12-01-show` · [main](main.md#a12-01-show)

_It’s late summer, and the light goes about nine. Kay and Gerda come across by the gutter boxes, the way they always do in summer._  
_Grandmother sits with a sheet of black paper and a long pair of scissors, snipping without looking._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): who Hans Christian Andersen was  
> _This story was written by Hans Christian Andersen, born in 1805 in Odense, a town in Denmark. It came out in 1844._  
> _Andersen often cut pictures out of paper while he told his stories, just as Grandmother does._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Hans Christian Andersen was a Danish storyteller who made paper cut-outs._

**Grandmother**: Candle a bit higher. There. Now the wall’s a stage, and you’re the sun. Don’t wobble; suns don’t.  
**Kay**: Do the troll one. The troll one’s best.  
**Gerda**: It’s frightening.  
**Kay**: That’s why.  
**Grandmother**: Right. Steady, all of you. This one starts badly and ends well, and those are the best sort.  
_A paper troll steps onto the wall, all knees and grin. He holds a mirror cut so thin the candle shows through it._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-2, ask): shadow size and distance from the light  
> **Grandmother**: If I hold the troll closer to the candle, what happens to his shadow on the wall?  
> - ✅ It gets bigger → Yes. Closer to the light, he blocks more of it, so the shadow grows. Watch him loom.
> - It gets smaller → Try it and see: closer to the candle, he blocks more light, and the shadow gets bigger.
> - It disappears → He’d still block the light, so the shadow stays. It grows bigger as he gets closer.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a shadow grows bigger when the object moves closer to the light._

**Grandmother**: He had made a mirror that was wrong in a particular way. Anything good shrank in it to nearly nothing.  
**Grandmother**: Anything mean or ugly got bigger and worse. A green meadow looked like boiled spinach.  
**Kay**: I’d like to look in it.  
**Gerda**: I wouldn’t.  

#### `reader:a12-01-show-done` · [main](main.md#a12-01-show-done)

_Grandmother does the troll’s voice. It’s lower than you would expect, and very pleased with itself._  
**The Troll**: Ha! Look how small goodness goes! Look how big the rest of it gets! Isn’t it wonderful?  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): character motive  
> _Why might a troll enjoy a mirror that makes good things look small?_  
> - ✅ He likes making people feel bad → Yes. He enjoys spoiling things. That’s what makes him the villain.
> - He wants to help people → Listen to his laugh again. He’s pleased when goodness shrinks, so he isn’t helping.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a character’s motive from what he says and how he says it._


#### `reader:a12-01-shards` · [main](main.md#a12-01-shards)

**Grandmother**: The troll kept a school, if you can believe it, and his pupils carried the mirror all round the world.  
**Grandmother**: Then they thought they’d fly up to the sky with it, and make fun of the angels too.  
_The paper mirror climbs the wall in little jerks. The higher it goes, the wider its painted grin spreads._  
**Grandmother**: And it grinned so hard it shook, and shook, until it slipped right out of their hands.  
_Grandmother opens her fingers. A handful of paper snips falls through the candlelight like snow._  
**Grandmother**: It broke into more pieces than there are people. Some were no bigger than a grain of sand.  
**Grandmother**: Get one in your eye and everything looks wrong. Get one in your heart and it goes hard and cold, like a stone in a frosty field.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): similes  
> _“Cold, like a stone in a frosty field” is a simile: it compares two things using “like” or “as”. What does this one tell you?_  
> - ✅ The heart stops feeling anything → Yes. A stone in frost can’t feel the sun. That’s what a cold heart is like.
> - The heart turns into a real stone → Not really. A simile says it’s like a stone: hard and unfeeling, but still a heart.
> _For grown-ups: Explained what a simile means in a story._

**Kay**: And then what?  
**Grandmother**: Then you need somebody to come and fetch you.  

**Choices**

- **Brush the paper snips off Gerda’s sleeve.**
    - You brush them off. Gerda checks her other sleeve herself, twice, and then Kay’s.
- **Catch a snip as it falls.**
    - You catch one on your palm, and it’s only paper. You look at it anyway, a little longer than you meant to.
- **Ask who comes to fetch you.**
    - **Grandmother**: Whoever loves you most and is too stubborn to stop. That’s usually how it goes.

#### `reader:a12-01-shards-done` · [main](main.md#a12-01-shards-done)

_The troll laughs on the wall until his paper sides split. Grandmother blows out the candle._  
_In the dark, the draught from the window lifts one snip that isn’t paper. It glints once, and is gone._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-2, notice): mirrors and glass reflect light  
> _The candle is out, so where did the glint come from? A little moonlight from the window, bouncing off glass._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a shiny thing glints because it bounces light back to our eyes._


#### `reader:a12-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a12-01-complete)

_Later, in the room under the eaves, you lie in the bed by the chimney. The other bed creaks, as promised._  
_From your window you can see the gutter, the two boxes across it, and two small rose trees still flowering in the dark._  
_Your Nubbin curls up on your feet. Across the town a clock strikes ten, slow and sure._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals in hours  
> _The clock struck ten. If you sleep until seven in the morning, how many hours of sleep is that?_  
> - ✅ 9 hours → Yes. Two hours to midnight, then seven more: nine hours. A good long sleep.
> - 3 hours → Ten to seven isn’t three, because midnight comes in between. Two hours to midnight, plus seven: nine.
> - 17 hours → Ten plus seven is seventeen, but clocks start again at midnight. Two hours, then seven more: nine.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out an overnight time interval in hours._

_You fall asleep to the sound of rain beginning, very softly, on the roof._  

#### `reader:a12-01-ember-forgiven` · [main](main.md#a12-01-ember-forgiven)

_Variant of `a12-01-complete`._

_Your Nubbin is carrying something: a little lantern, and in it Cinder’s ember, small and red._  
_It burns steady, the colour of a hearth with the door shut. Grandmother holds her hands out to it without thinking._  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): what a fire needs  
> _A fire needs three things: fuel to burn, air, and heat. The lantern glass lets air in but keeps the wind from blowing the flame about._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that fire needs fuel, air and heat._

**Grandmother**: Now that’s a good little fire. Bring it up with you; the eaves get cold.  

#### `reader:a12-01-ember-remembered` · [main](main.md#a12-01-ember-remembered)

_Variant of `a12-01-complete`._

_Your Nubbin is carrying something: a little lantern, and in it Cinder’s ember, small and red._  
_It flickers now and then, as if it’s remembering something. It never goes out._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): why flames flicker  
> _Why does a flame flicker?_  
> - ✅ Moving air pushes it about → Yes. Every draught nudges it. A lantern keeps out the worst.
> - It is tired → Flames don’t get tired! Moving air pushes them about.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that flames flicker when air moves around them._

**Grandmother**: That one’s got a story in it. Bring it up with you; the eaves get cold.  

### Glossary

- **lodger**: Someone who pays to live in a room in another person’s house.
- **peninsula**: A piece of land with water on almost every side.
- **simile**: A way of describing something by saying it is like something else.

## A12-2. Two Windows

- `a12-02-s1`: Water the roses in the gutter boxes. · _Climb out to the gutter with the watering can and do the top of the arch._
- `a12-02-s2`: Warm a coin on the stove and melt a peephole in the frozen window. · _Hold a copper coin on the stove, then press it to the glass._
- `a12-02-s3`: Look out of your window that night. · _Wait at your peephole after the house has gone to bed._

#### `reader:a12-02-roses` · [main](main.md#a12-02-roses)

_In the morning you see it all properly: two attics with roofs that nearly touch, and a lead gutter running between them._  
_Long ago the two families each put a box across the gutter. Now peas hang over the edges, and in each box grows a rose tree._  
_The roses have climbed into an arch. Under it are two little stools, and two children who think it’s the best room in town._  
**Gerda**: You’re tallest. You can do the top of the arch. Don’t drown the peas; they sulk.  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-LS2-1, ask): what plants need  
> **Gerda**: Why do roses up on a roof grow so well, do you think?  
> - ✅ Lots of sunlight, and we water them → Exactly. Plants need light and water. Up here there’s sun all day, and we bring the water.
> - Because it’s high up → Being high doesn’t feed them. It’s the sunlight up here, and the water we bring.
> - Roses don’t need anything → Everything living needs something! Roses need light, water and good soil.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that plants need light and water to grow._

**Kay**: Peas don’t sulk.  
**Gerda**: These ones do.  
**Kay**: Gran says the roses are older than both of us put together. She says they’ll still be here when we’re old.  
_Your Nubbin tries to help by sitting in the watering can._  

**Choices**

- **Water the top of the arch, carefully.**
    - You water the high roses. A few drops roll off onto Kay’s head, and he pretends not to notice, very badly.
- **Give the peas a bit extra, in case.**
    - **Gerda**: See? They’re cheering up already.
    - **Kay**: They look exactly the same.
- **Lift your Nubbin out of the can first.**
    - Your Nubbin comes out of the can dripping and offended. Gerda dries it on her apron, and it forgives everybody.

#### `reader:a12-02-roses-done` · [main](main.md#a12-02-roses-done)

**Gerda**: In winter we can’t climb across. We have to go down all our stairs and up all the others.  
**Kay**: It takes years.  
**Gerda**: It takes two minutes.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): minutes and seconds  
> _There are 60 seconds in a minute. How many seconds is Gerda’s two-minute trip?_  
> - ✅ 120 seconds → Yes. Sixty and sixty make a hundred and twenty. Not years at all.
> - 62 seconds → That’s one minute and two seconds. Two whole minutes is sixty plus sixty: one hundred and twenty.
> - 200 seconds → A bit too many. Sixty plus sixty is one hundred and twenty.
> _For grown-ups: Changed minutes into seconds._


#### `reader:a12-02-frost` · [main](main.md#a12-02-frost)

_Winter comes, and the windows freeze over with ferns of ice. You can’t see across the gutter at all._  
_Kay and Gerda warm copper coins on the stove and press them to the glass. Copper passes heat on quickly, so the coin melts the ice fast._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-ESS2-3, notice): how frost forms  
> _Warm air indoors holds water you can’t see, called water vapour. When it touches glass colder than 0 °C (32 °F), it freezes._  
> _It turns straight into tiny ice crystals, which grow into those feathery ferns._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that frost forms when water vapour freezes on a very cold surface._

_Each coin melts a round peephole, as neat as a button. A bright eye looks through each one, across the snow._  
**Grandmother**: Look at the white bees swarming.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): metaphors  
> _Grandmother calls the snowflakes “white bees”. Why is that a good metaphor?_  
> - ✅ They swarm and buzz about in the air → Just so. Snow in the wind whirls about like a swarm of bees, and every swarm has a queen.
> - Snowflakes can sting you → They don’t sting! It’s the swarming that’s alike: thousands of little white things, whirling.
> _For grown-ups: Explained a metaphor that compares snowflakes to bees._

**Kay**: Have they got a queen? Like real bees?  
**Grandmother**: They have. She flies where the swarm is thickest, and she never stays on the ground for long.  
**Grandmother**: Some nights she flies through the streets and looks in at the windows. That’s when the frost comes up in flowers.  
**Kay**: Let her try. I’d put her on the hot stove, and she’d melt.  

**Choices**

- **Tell Kay that’s no way to treat a guest.**
    - **Kay**: She isn’t a guest. She’s weather.
- **Ask Grandmother if she’s ever seen her.**
    - **Grandmother**: Once or twice. Through the glass. Not close, and that’s how I like it.
- **Look out of your own peephole.**
    - The flakes go past your peephole in their thousands. One of them seems, for a moment, to look back.

#### `reader:a12-02-frost-done` · [main](main.md#a12-02-frost-done)

_Grandmother smooths Kay’s hair and starts a story about a goose instead._  
_She doesn’t mention the queen again._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): reading other people’s feelings  
> _Why do you think Grandmother changes the subject so quickly?_  
> - Kay’s boasting worried her → That’s a thoughtful answer. Grown-ups sometimes change the subject when something worries them.
> - She just likes geese → Maybe! But notice she smooths his hair first, which looks like care, or worry.
> - She was tired of the story → Could be. Watch her face next time Kay talks about the queen, and see what you think.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what a character’s actions show about her feelings._


#### `reader:a12-02-night` · [main](main.md#a12-02-night)

_That night you can’t sleep. Your Nubbin can’t either; it sits on the sill with its nose to your peephole._  
_Across the gutter Kay is up too, standing on a chair at his window._  
_A few flakes drift down. One, bigger than the rest, settles on the edge of a flower box, and stays._  
_It grows into a woman, tall and fine, dressed in white gauze made of a million starry flakes._  
_She is lovely, and she is ice, all of her, dazzling and glittering. And she is alive._  
_Her eyes are like two bright stars, but there is no rest in them at all._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.3, ask): describing a character  
> _Which words tell you most clearly that the woman is not a warm, ordinary person?_  
> - ✅ “She is ice, all of her” → Yes. She looks like a woman, but she’s made of ice. That’s the clue that matters.
> - “Tall and fine” → Lots of people are tall and fine. The clue is “she is ice, all of her”.
> _For grown-ups: Used the words of a description to understand a character._

_She looks in at Kay’s window. She nods to him, and lifts one hand, and beckons._  

**Choices**

- **Knock on your window.**
    - You knock hard on the glass. Kay jumps down off his chair, and the woman turns her head towards you, slowly.
    - Then she isn’t there. Something large and white goes past the window, like a bird.
- **Call Kay’s name.**
    - You call his name across the gutter. Kay startles and jumps down from his chair.
    - When you look again there’s nobody on the box. Something white goes past, like a big bird in the dark.
- **Pull your Nubbin back from the glass.**
    - You lift your Nubbin away from the window. It’s cold right through, and it burrows into your shirt.
    - Kay jumps down off his chair. When you look again a big white bird is passing, and the box is empty.

#### `reader:a12-02-night-done` · [main](main.md#a12-02-night-done)

_You sit up until morning._  
_The frost on your window has grown into flowers you have never seen in any garden._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-ESS2-3, notice): ice crystals grow  
> _Frost keeps growing all night. More water vapour freezes onto the crystals already there, so the patterns spread across the glass._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that frost patterns grow as more water vapour freezes onto them._


#### `reader:a12-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a12-02-complete)

**Kay**: It was a dream. I dreamed a lady on the box. That’s all.  
**Gerda**: I dreamed you were standing on a chair.  
**Kay**: Well, I wasn’t.  
_Grandmother, cutting the bread, looks at Kay a moment longer than usual._  
_Then the thaw comes, and the swallows come back, and the roses wake up in the gutter as if nothing at all has happened._  
_You have seen late summer, then deep winter, and now spring. The year is turning._  

> **Lesson** (science, K-ESS2-1, ask): the seasons in order  
> _The story began in late summer. Which season comes next, after this spring?_  
> - ✅ Summer → Yes. Summer, autumn, winter, spring, and round to summer again.
> - Autumn → Autumn comes after summer. After spring comes summer again.
> - Winter → Winter came before spring. Next it’s summer: the year goes round like a wheel.
> _For grown-ups: Put the four seasons in order._


### Glossary

- **gutter**: A channel along the edge of a roof that carries rainwater away.
- **water vapour**: Water that has turned into an invisible gas in the air.
- **beckons**: Signals to someone with a hand to come closer.

## A12-3. The Splinter

- `a12-03-s1`: Sit under the rose arch with Kay and Gerda. · _Climb out to the gutter and take the spare stool._
- `a12-03-s2`: Put the rose box straight after Kay kicks it. · _Lift the box back onto the gutter and press the earth round the roots._
- `a12-03-s3`: Go out in the snow when Kay calls you to see his glass. · _Follow Gerda down to the street and look where Kay shows you._

#### `reader:a12-03-arch` · [main](main.md#a12-03-arch)

_It’s summer, and the roses have never been so good. Gerda has learned a hymn with roses in it, and hums it all day._  
_She hums it to Kay under the arch, and he hums it back. Your Nubbin hums the tune a beat behind them both._  
_They’re looking at a picture book of animals and birds. Kay is doing the noises, and the noises are terrible._  
**Kay**: That’s an elk. It goes like this.  
**Gerda**: It does not.  
_Across the town, the church clock begins to strike five._  
**Kay**: Ow! Something stung me. In my heart. And my eye, something’s gone in my eye.  
_In the low sun you see it: a glint, smaller than a grain of sand, there and gone._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): how eyes protect themselves  
> _Eyes have their own defences. Blinking sweeps dust away, and tears wash out what blinking can’t._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how blinking and tears protect our eyes._


**Choices**

- **Look in Kay’s eye.**
    - You hold his eyelid gently and look, and there’s nothing there. You are almost sure there’s nothing there.
- **Hold Kay’s hand till it stops.**
    - You hold his hand, and it’s warm. After a moment it isn’t quite as warm, and you tell yourself it is.
- **Call Grandmother.**
    - Grandmother comes up, peers into his eye with her spectacles on, and finds nothing at all.
    - **Grandmother**: Blink. Again. There. Gone.

#### `reader:a12-03-arch-done` · [main](main.md#a12-03-arch-done)

**Kay**: It’s gone. I think it’s gone.  
_It hasn’t gone._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): point of view  
> _Kay thinks it’s gone, but the narrator says it hasn’t. Who knows more right now?_  
> - ✅ The narrator, and so do you → Yes. The storyteller lets you know something Kay doesn’t. That’s what makes you worry for him.
> - Kay → Kay can only feel the sting fading. The narrator sees the whole story, and says it’s still there.
> _For grown-ups: Compared what a character knows with what the reader knows._


#### `reader:a12-03-kick` · [main](main.md#a12-03-kick)

**Gerda**: Kay? Are you all right?  
**Kay**: Why are you crying? It makes you look ugly.  
**Kay**: There’s nothing the matter with me. That rose has a worm in it. And that one’s all crooked.  
**Kay**: They’re ugly roses. They match the ugly boxes.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): whose fault unkindness is  
> _Is Kay choosing to be unkind, or is the splinter doing it? What do you think?_  
> - The splinter is doing it → That’s fair: something has got in front of the real Kay. It still hurts Gerda, though.
> - Kay is choosing it → That’s fair too. Even with a splinter, his words land on a real person.
> - A bit of both → A thoughtful answer. Hard days can make us unkind, and we still owe a sorry after.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why people are sometimes unkind and how it affects others._

_He kicks the box. It skids along the gutter, and he snaps off two roses and drops them over the edge._  
_You reach for him, and then you understand what you saw._  
_You can’t take a splinter out of a heart. Not with your hands, and not with anything you own._  
_Kay goes in and slams his window. Gerda stands in the gutter with her picture book held against her chest._  
**Gerda**: He said it was a book for babies.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, notice): actions that show feelings  
> _Look at Gerda: she holds her book tight against her chest. Writers often show a feeling through what a character does._  
> _She is hurt, and that’s allowed. When someone is unkind, it helps to be near a friend._  
> _For grown-ups: Noticed how a character’s actions show her feelings._


**Choices**

- **Fetch the two roses back for Gerda.**
    - You lean over the gutter’s edge and fish them back. Gerda puts them in a cup of water on her sill.
- **Knock on Kay’s window.**
    - You knock. Kay opens it, looks at you, and does a perfect, horrible impression of your face.
    - Then he shuts it again.
- **Say nothing. Straighten the box together.**
    - You and Gerda lift the box back into place without a word. Your Nubbin pats the earth flat round the roots.

#### `reader:a12-03-kick-done` · [main](main.md#a12-03-kick-done)

_The rose tree has lost two flowers and a bit of bark._  
_Roses forgive that sort of thing, if they’re left alone. It still has ten flowers open, and more in bud._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 1.OA.A.1, ask): subtraction word problem  
> _Kay snapped two flowers off the rose tree, and ten are left. How many were there before?_  
> - ✅ 12 → Yes. Ten left, plus the two he snapped: twelve.
> - 8 → Eight would be ten take away two. But we want what was there before: ten plus two, twelve.
> - 10 → Ten is how many are left now. Add back the two he snapped: twelve.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a word problem by working backwards with addition._


#### `reader:a12-03-glass` · [main](main.md#a12-03-glass)

_All that autumn Kay is clever and cruel. He puts on Grandmother’s spectacles and does her voice, and the whole street laughs._  
_He can do anybody. He does Gerda best of all, and he only does the bits that hurt._  
**Grandmother**: He’s a sharp lad. Too sharp, lately.  
_When the snow comes he runs out with a big burning-glass, and holds out the blue tail of his coat to catch the flakes._  
**Kay**: Look in the glass, Gerda. Look how perfect they are.  
_Under the glass each flake is a star, sharp and exact, every arm the same as every other._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-ESS2-3, do): snowflakes have six arms  
> _Count the arms on the flake under the glass. Snow crystals almost always have six._  
> _When water freezes, its tiny particles lock together in a six-sided pattern, so the crystal grows six arms._  
> Check: `edu:count:snowflake-arms:6`
> _For grown-ups: Counted the six arms of a snowflake and learned why there are six._

_You think of the night you trusted someone with your friend. It came back, and so, it seems, did something else._
