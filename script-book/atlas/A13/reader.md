## A13-1. The Piece of Wood

- `a13-01-s1`: Step into the brown-ink page, and out of the rain. · _Touch the page. Then find the open door with sawdust on its step._
- `a13-01-s2`: Stay in Master Cherry’s shop while he planes the log. · _Stand by the bench. When the old men start, keep your Nubbin out from under their feet._

#### `reader:a13-01-page` · [main](main.md#a13-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page in brown ink: a hill town, a long white road, and along the bottom edge, the sea._  
_The words across the top begin the way stories for children do. Once upon a time there was… a king, you think._  
_No. Once upon a time there was a piece of wood._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.3-5, notice): Pinocchio’s author, Carlo Collodi, and the Tuscany he wrote about  
> _This page is a real place: Tuscany, in the middle of Italy, with hill towns, vineyards and a coast far to the west._  
> _The writer was Carlo Lorenzini. He called himself Collodi, after a Tuscan town, and his story first ran in a children’s newspaper in 1881._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Pinocchio comes from Tuscany in Italy, and that Collodi first published it in a children’s newspaper in 1881._

_The ink goes wet under your fingers, and then it is rain. You and your Nubbin are standing in a steep stone lane, getting wetter._  
_One door is open, with sawdust on the step and a warm smell of pine. You go in, because the rain has decided for you._  
**Master Cherry**: Come in, come in, shut the weather out. Mind the shavings. Mind the cat.  
**Master Cherry**: There isn’t a cat. Mind it anyway. It saves arguments.  
_The carpenter’s nose is so round and red and shiny that it looks like a ripe cherry. Everyone calls him Master Cherry._  
**Master Cherry**: I’m making a table leg. Out of this. A firewood log, a nothing log. Nothing special about it at all.  
_He spits on his hands and lifts the axe._  
**A Small Voice**: Don’t hit me too hard!  

#### `reader:a13-01-page-clank` · [main](main.md#a13-01-page-clank)

_Variant of `a13-01-page`._

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page in brown ink: a hill town, a long white road, and along the bottom edge, the sea._  
_The words across the top begin the way stories for children do. Once upon a time there was… a king, you think._  
_No. Once upon a time there was a piece of wood._  
_The ink goes wet under your fingers, and then it is rain. You, your Nubbin and Clank are standing in a steep stone lane, getting wetter._  
**Clank**: Announcement: it’s steps. It’s ALL steps. This whole country is made of steps!  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-5-ETS1-2, ask): a ramp is a simple machine (an inclined plane) that trades distance for force  
> _Steps are a problem for Clank’s wheel. Byte’s ramp solves it._  
> _Why is it easier to roll up a long gentle slope than to lift over one tall step?_  
> - ✅ A long slope spreads the lift over a longer distance, so it takes less push at once. → Yes. A ramp is an inclined plane. You travel farther but push less hard. That is the trade every simple machine makes.
> - A ramp makes Clank lighter. → Clank weighs the same on a ramp. It is the push needed at any moment that gets smaller.
> - Ramps are only for show. → Ramps are used everywhere: wheelchair ramps, loading ramps, even the road up a hill. They really work.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a ramp is a simple machine that makes lifting easier by spreading the effort over a longer distance._

_Clank unfolds the ramp Byte made him for the trip: three hinged boards, sanded smooth, with BYTE pencilled on the underneath._  
**Clank**: Unfold, roll, fold up. Byte made me practise forty times. Forty-one. The last one was for luck. Byte doesn’t believe in luck.  
_One door is open, with sawdust on the step and a warm smell of pine._  
_You go in, and Clank comes in after you, down his ramp._  
**Master Cherry**: Come in, come in, shut the weather out. Mind the shavings. And bring your… kettle. Is that a kettle?  
**Clank**: Announcement: I am Clank! I am a guest! Is this a house? I love it!

#### `reader:a13-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a13-01-page-done)

_Master Cherry looks under the bench, in the cupboard and up the chimney. There is nobody in the shop but you, and you are a stranger._  
**Master Cherry**: Did you say that? You didn’t say that. Your little friend didn’t say that. Nobody said that.  
**Master Cherry**: I imagined it. Wine and weather. That’s all it was.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, ask): tree rings: one ring for each year of growth  
> _Master Cherry points at the end of the log. Inside it are rings, like a target. How might you tell how old the tree was?_  
> - ✅ Count the rings. Each one is a year. → Yes. Trees add one ring a year. A wide ring means a good year with plenty of rain and sun. A thin ring means a hard one.
> - Measure how tall the log is. → A log can be any length. The rings inside tell the age: one ring for every year the tree grew.
> - You cannot tell. → You can! Count the rings from the middle outwards. Each ring is one year.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a tree grows one ring each year and that rings can be counted to find its age._


#### `reader:a13-01-plane` · [main](main.md#a13-01-plane)

_He gives the log a proper whack this time._  
**A Small Voice**: Ow! That hurt!  
_Master Cherry sits down on the floor, very suddenly. Then he gets up, very slowly, and picks up his plane instead._  
_He planes. The log giggles._  
**A Small Voice**: Stop it! You’re tickling!  
_Master Cherry falls flat like a sack of meal. His red nose goes quite blue with fright._  
_Somebody knocks, and in comes a little old man in a bright yellow wig._  
_The wig is exactly the colour of polenta, so the boys of the town call him Polendina. He hates it more than anything in the world._  
**Geppetto**: Good morning, Master Antonio. What are you doing down there?  
**Master Cherry**: Teaching the ants their letters. What brings you, Geppetto?  
**Geppetto**: An idea. A wooden puppet that dances and fences and turns somersaults. I’ll take him round the world and earn my crust.  
**A Small Voice**: Good idea, Polendina!  
_Geppetto turns the colour of a beetroot, and looks straight at Master Cherry._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): seeing a scene from two points of view  
> _You know the log spoke. Geppetto doesn’t. He thinks his friend called him a rude name._  
> _Is it fair to be angry with someone before you know the facts?_  
> - No. Find out first, then decide. → That’s a fair answer. Geppetto only has half the story, and so he is cross with the wrong person.
> - Yes. The name is rude whoever said it. → That’s fair too. Being called a name hurts. But the person who said it matters, and he has picked the wrong one.
> - You should tell him it was the log. → That’s fair. You do know the truth, and a true word can stop a quarrel. Whether he would believe it is another matter.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how knowing only half the story changes how we judge someone._


**Choices**

- **Tell Geppetto it was the log.**
    - **Geppetto**: The log. A log called me Polendina. And I suppose the bench calls me Grandad.
    - So that went well.
- **Keep out of it. It’s their argument.**
    - You keep out of it. That lasts about a second and a half.

#### `reader:a13-01-plane-done` · [main](main.md#a13-01-plane-done)

_The two old men shout and tug at each other, loud as two cats._  
_When they come apart, Geppetto is wearing Master Cherry’s grey wig, and Master Cherry the yellow._  
_They swap back, shake hands, and swear to be friends for the rest of their lives._  
_Master Cherry gives him the log very gladly. It jumps out of his hands and cracks Geppetto on the shins, and they start all over again._  
_In the end, peace. Geppetto puts the log on his shoulder and limps off, and the rain has stopped._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): what helps repair a friendship after a quarrel  
> _The old men argued, swapped wigs and made peace twice in one morning. What mends a quarrel best?_  
> - ✅ Listening, saying sorry, and shaking hands. → Yes. Listening shows you care what the other person felt, and sorry and a handshake close it.
> - Waiting for the other person to apologise first. → That feels fair, but it can leave the quarrel open for a very long time. Someone has to go first.
> - Pretending it never happened. → That sometimes works for tiny things. For bigger ones, the hurt stays unless somebody talks about it.
> _For grown-ups: Talked about listening, apologising and shaking hands as ways to mend a quarrel._


#### `reader:a13-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a13-01-complete)

**Geppetto**: You. The traveller. You’ve no roof, have you? I can always tell. I’ve hardly a roof myself. Come on.  
_His room is under the stairs, lit by one small window. A chair, a poor sort of bed, and a table that has seen better tables._  
_In the back wall there is a fireplace with a fire in it and a pot on top, merrily steaming. It is painted._  
**Geppetto**: It’s a very good painting. It keeps me warm, if I don’t think about it.  
**Geppetto**: You sleep in the corner. I’d offer you supper, but that’s painted too.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.3, ask): what a setting tells us about a character  
> _Geppetto has a painted fire and painted supper. What does that tell you about him?_  
> - ✅ He is very poor, but cheerful and inventive. → Yes. He can’t afford a real fire, so he paints one and makes a joke of it. The room tells us who he is.
> - He is a bad painter. → The painting is called “very good” in the story. It is meant to tell us about Geppetto, not about his brushwork.
> - He is rich and plays tricks. → There’s a poor bed and a tired table. Look at the details: the room says poor, not rich.
> _For grown-ups: Used details of a setting (a painted fire) to understand a character’s life and spirit._

_He hangs his felt cap on the nail by the door._  
_Your Nubbin climbs into it, turns round twice and settles, like a pip in a pod._  
**Geppetto**: Ha. It may stay. It’s the warmest thing in the house.  

### Glossary

- **plane**: a tool that shaves thin curls off a piece of wood to make it smooth
- **crust**: a living, the money you earn to eat; also the hard outside of bread

## A13-2. Geppetto Carves a Puppet

- `a13-02-s1`: Hold the lamp while Geppetto carves. · _Stand at his elbow with the lamp. Watch the nose._
- `a13-02-s2`: Help Geppetto teach the puppet to walk. · _Hold one wooden hand; Geppetto holds the other. Mind the door._

#### `reader:a13-02-carve` · [main](main.md#a13-02-carve)

_Geppetto lays the log on his bench, rolls up his sleeves and looks at it for a long time, the way you look at a letter before you open it._  
**Geppetto**: What shall I call him? Pinocchio. I knew a whole family of Pinocchios once, and they all did splendidly.  
**Geppetto**: The richest of them begged for a living. You see? Lucky.  
_A pinocchio is a pine nut. It suits a thing made of pine, with a hard little head on it._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): pinocchio means pine nut, and a name can carry a joke  
> _Pinocchio is a real Tuscan word for a pine nut, the small seed inside a pine cone. People in Italy put pine nuts in pesto._  
> _A pine nut is hard, small and tough. Collodi chose it to name a hard-headed little puppet carved from pine._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that pinocchio is a Tuscan word for a pine nut and why the name suits the puppet._

_He carves the hair, then the forehead, then the eyes. The eyes move, and stare at him._  
**Geppetto**: Wooden eyes, why are you looking at me?  
_Nobody answers. He carves the nose, and the nose begins to grow._  
_He cuts it shorter, and it grows longer. He cuts it again, and it grows again, until he gives up and leaves it._  
_He carves the mouth. Before he has quite finished, it laughs at him and sticks its tongue out._  
**Geppetto**: Stop laughing!  
_The mouth stops laughing. It goes on sticking its tongue out._  

**Choices**

- **Point out that the nose is growing again.**
    - **Geppetto**: I know. I’ve decided not to notice. You should try it.
- **Keep the lamp steady and say nothing.**
    - You keep the lamp steady. Geppetto works faster, the way people do when their work is watching them back.

#### `reader:a13-02-carve-done` · [main](main.md#a13-02-carve-done)

_Geppetto carves the hands. The moment they are finished, they snatch the yellow wig off his head and put it on their own._  
**Geppetto**: Pinocchio! Give that back this minute!  
_Pinocchio gives it back. His eyes are laughing._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): burattino is Italian for a hand puppet; the book’s puppet has no strings  
> _Collodi’s word for Pinocchio is burattino, an Italian puppet worked by a hand inside it, not by strings._  
> _That is why this page never mentions strings. Pinocchio moves on his own, which is the trouble._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the Italian word burattino and that Collodi’s puppet is not a string puppet._


#### `reader:a13-02-legs` · [main](main.md#a13-02-legs)

**Geppetto**: You young rascal. You’re not even finished, and already you have no respect for your father.  
_He carves the legs and the feet. The first thing the feet do is kick him on the end of the nose._  
**Geppetto**: I deserved that. I ought to have thought of it before I made them.  
_He sets Pinocchio on the floor to teach him to walk. You take one wooden hand, and he takes the other._  
_The legs are stiff, then less stiff. Pinocchio takes one step, and another, and then he is running._  
**Pinocchio**: Ha! Legs! I’ve got LEGS!  
_He is out of the door before either of you can shut it, and away down the street, clack-clack-clack on the cobbles._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, notice): walking works because the foot pushes back on the ground and friction grips  
> _To walk, your foot pushes backwards on the ground. Friction grips, and the ground pushes you forwards. That is an unbalanced force._  
> _Pinocchio’s first steps are wobbly because his balance and his grip are brand new. Babies learn it the same way._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that walking uses a push against the ground and the grip of friction, which are forces._

**Geppetto**: Catch him! Catch him!  
_People in the street watch him go by, and nobody catches him. They are laughing too hard to catch anything._  

#### `reader:a13-02-street` · [main](main.md#a13-02-street)

_At the bottom of the street a carabineer steps out and catches Pinocchio neatly by the nose. It is a very convenient nose for it._  
_Geppetto arrives out of breath and gives him a good shake. Pinocchio throws himself on the cobbles and refuses to walk another step._  
_A crowd gathers. The crowd decides, as crowds do, that the poor little puppet is frightened of the dreadful old man._  
**Carabineer**: Well. If that’s how he treats them, he can come along with me.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): judging a situation from only part of what you saw  
> _The crowd saw a puppet on the floor and a shaking old man, and decided who was in the wrong._  
> _Have you ever judged a situation from only part of it?_  
> - Yes. I got it wrong, and I remember how it felt. → That’s a fair answer. Looking back is how we get better at pausing before we decide.
> - I try to ask what happened first. → That’s a fair answer. Asking questions first is how a crowd could have helped Geppetto.
> - I am not sure. It is hard to know what you missed. → That’s a fair answer, and it is the heart of the problem. If you cannot tell what you missed, it is wiser to wait and ask.
> _For grown-ups: Reflected on how seeing only part of an event can lead to the wrong judgement._


#### `reader:a13-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a13-02-complete)

_So it is Geppetto who is marched off to prison, crying all the way._  
**Geppetto**: Wretched boy! I worked so hard to make him a good puppet. It serves me right. I should have thought.  
_Your Nubbin, still in the felt cap he snatched up as he ran, jumps out as he goes._  
_The carabineer walks him round the corner, and the street is empty._  
_Pinocchio picks himself up, brushes off his knees and runs home over the fields, as fast as a hare with somewhere to be._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): speed, distance and time  
> _A hare can run about 60 kilometres every hour, which is 1 kilometre every minute._  
> _If Pinocchio could keep up that pace for 5 minutes, how far would he get?_  
> - ✅ 5 kilometres → Yes. 1 kilometre a minute for 5 minutes is 5 kilometres. Pinocchio is not quite that quick, but the idea is the same.
> - 60 kilometres → That is how far in a whole hour. In 5 minutes it is only a small slice of the hour: 5 kilometres.
> - 15 kilometres → Try 1 kilometre a minute: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. After 5 minutes, 5 kilometres.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a speed, distance and time problem: 1 km a minute for 5 minutes is 5 km._

_You follow. The room under the stairs is very quiet without the old man in it._  

### Glossary

- **carabineer**: an Italian police officer; the word comes from the carbine, a short rifle
- **burattino**: the Italian word for a puppet worked by a hand inside it, not by strings

## A13-3. The Talking-cricket

- `a13-03-s1`: Find what is going cri-cri-cri in the dark. · _Listen along the wall by the hearth._
- `a13-03-s2`: Listen to the Talking-cricket. Then decide what to do about the hearth. · _Hear him out. When it goes quiet, you’ll know what you can do._

#### `reader:a13-03-cri` · [main](main.md#a13-03-cri)

_Pinocchio bangs the door shut, sits on the floor and lets out a great sigh. A whole room, and nobody in it to tell him anything._  
**Pinocchio**: Mine. All of it. The chair, the table, the painted fire. You can stay too, if you don’t tell me anything.  
_Then, from somewhere in the room, a small sound: cri-cri-cri._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-1, notice): crickets make sound by rubbing their wings, and chirp faster when it is warmer  
> _A real cricket sings by rubbing one wing against a rough ridge on the other._  
> _The rubbing makes the wing vibrate, and a vibration is a sound._  
> _It chirps faster on a warm night and slower on a cool one. Some people count the chirps to guess the temperature._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that crickets chirp by vibrating their wings and chirp faster when it is warm._

**Pinocchio**: Who’s that?  
_Cri-cri-cri, from the wall by the hearth. Your Nubbin’s ears go straight up._  
**Pinocchio**: It’s you, isn’t it? Doing a voice. Stop doing a voice.  

#### `reader:a13-03-cri-done` · [main](main.md#a13-03-cri-done)

_There on the wall, climbing slowly upwards, is a big cricket. He has the air of somebody who has paid the rent._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): figurative language: “the air of somebody who has paid the rent”  
> _The narrator says the cricket has “the air of somebody who has paid the rent”. What does that tell you about how he behaves?_  
> - ✅ He acts as if he belongs there and has every right. → Yes. Someone who has paid the rent feels at home and expects respect. It is a joke about how grand a small cricket can look.
> - He is carrying coins. → The cricket doesn’t carry coins. It is an image, a picture in words, for how he stands and moves.
> - He is angry about money. → He isn’t cross about money. The phrase is about confidence: he is the oldest resident, and he knows it.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the meaning of a figure of speech and how it shows character._

**The Talking-cricket**: It is I. The Talking-cricket. I have lived in this room for more than a hundred years.  

#### `reader:a13-03-cricket` · [main](main.md#a13-03-cricket)

**Pinocchio**: Well, it’s my room now. Off you go. And don’t look back.  
**The Talking-cricket**: I shan’t go until I have told you one great truth.  
**The Talking-cricket**: Boys who turn their backs on their fathers, and run off from home to please themselves, come to no good. Sooner or later. Usually sooner.  
**Pinocchio**: Sing away, Cricket. I’m off at first light. If I stay, they’ll send me to school, and I’d rather chase butterflies.  
**The Talking-cricket**: Then you’ll grow up a perfect donkey, and everyone will laugh at you.  
**The Talking-cricket**: At least learn a trade. Something to earn an honest crust.  
**Pinocchio**: There’s only one trade I fancy. Eating, drinking, sleeping, having fun, and wandering about from morning till night.  
**The Talking-cricket**: Everyone who follows that trade ends up in hospital or in prison. I am sorry for you, Pinocchio.  
**Pinocchio**: Sorry? Why?  
**The Talking-cricket**: Because you are a puppet. And, worse, because you have a wooden head.  
_Pinocchio grabs the mallet off the bench and throws it, not really aiming. That is the trouble with not really aiming._  
_It catches the cricket and knocks him clean off the wall, down into the cold ash of the hearth. Then nothing._  
**Pinocchio**: I didn’t… He shouldn’t have said wooden head. He shouldn’t have.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): responsibility for something you did without meaning to  
> _Pinocchio says “not really aiming”. He didn’t mean to hit the cricket. Does that make it not his fault?_  
> - It still matters. He threw it, so he should put it right. → That’s a fair answer. Not meaning to hurt someone doesn’t take the hurt away, and putting it right is what makes it better.
> - Not at all his fault. It was an accident. → That’s a fair answer. Accidents happen. But a careless throw while you are angry is not quite the same as a true accident.
> - Partly. The cricket provoked him, but he still threw it. → That’s a fair answer, and probably the closest to the truth. Both things can be true at once.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about responsibility when you hurt someone without quite meaning to._


**Choices**

- **Search the hearth.**
    - You kneel at the hearth and sift the ash with your fingers. Your Nubbin finds him first, and squeaks.
    - He is on his back, grey with ash, one leg going round and round like a little wheel. He is very much alive, and very cross.
    - **The Talking-cricket**: I am not hurt. I am insulted. There is a difference, and I am a hundred years old, and I know it.
    - You carry him out in your cupped hands to the hedge by the door, and set him on a leaf.
    - He straightens his feelers with great dignity.
    - **The Talking-cricket**: Thank you. Tell him nothing. He wouldn’t listen. He never listens.
- **Leave it. It’s his mess to put right.**
    - You leave it. Pinocchio looks at the hearth for a long time, and then turns his back on it.
    - Later, when he isn’t looking, you look. There is a tiny track through the ash, all the way to the crack under the door.
    - Whatever made it was walking. Slowly, and in a temper, but walking.

#### `reader:a13-03-cricket-done` · [main](main.md#a13-03-cricket-done)

_The room is quiet._  
_Pinocchio sits at the table with his chin on his fists and says nothing for a long time, which for him is a minute._  
**Pinocchio**: Anyway. I’m hungry.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): a character’s motive when he changes the subject  
> _After a long silence Pinocchio says, “Anyway. I’m hungry.” Why might he change the subject like that?_  
> - ✅ He feels guilty and does not want to talk about it. → Yes. People often change the subject when something is too uncomfortable to face.
> - He has completely forgotten the cricket. → He sat in silence for a whole minute, which for him is a long time. He hasn’t forgotten.
> - He is just greedy. → He is hungry, but the long quiet before it hints at something else underneath.
> _For grown-ups: Inferred a character’s hidden feeling from how he behaves._


#### `reader:a13-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a13-03-complete)

_Night comes on, and with it a hunger like a small dog barking inside him._  

> **Lesson** (health, 4-LS1-1, notice): tummy rumbles come from the gut moving food and air about  
> _When your stomach rumbles, the muscles in your gut are squeezing air and liquid along._  
> _This happens all the time, and you hear it more when your stomach is empty._  
> _So a rumble does not mean something is wrong. It means your body is getting ready for the next meal._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a rumbling tummy is the gut’s muscles moving air and liquid, and is normal._

**Pinocchio**: Do you think the cricket was right? No. Don’t answer. I’m too hungry for answers.  
_You don’t answer, and your Nubbin curls up against your boot. Outside, the wind is getting up._  
**Pinocchio**: Is there any supper? There’s always supper. Somewhere.  

### Glossary

- **provoke**: to make someone angry or upset on purpose or by accident

## A13-4. The Flying Egg

- `a13-04-s1`: Help Pinocchio look for something to eat. · _Try the cupboard, the drawers, and the heap of sweepings in the corner._
- `a13-04-s2`: Watch Pinocchio cook his egg. · _Stand back from the pan. Something is about to happen to it._
- `a13-04-s3`: Go with Pinocchio into the storm, to the village. · _Keep your Nubbin under your coat. It’s a long way for very little._

#### `reader:a13-04-hungry` · [main](main.md#a13-04-hungry)

_Pinocchio runs to the fire to lift the lid off the pot. His nose goes straight through the canvas, where the pot is painted on it._  
**Pinocchio**: Painted. Even the soup’s painted. What kind of house is this?  
_He tries the drawers: a button, a cork, a bent nail. He tries the cupboard: dust, and a smell of dust._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-1, do): sorting materials by their properties: cork floats, a nail sinks  
> _Sort what is in Geppetto’s drawer by what it is made of: wood and cork on one side, iron on the other._  
> _Then guess which will float. Cork and wood are light for their size, so they float._  
> _A bent iron nail is heavy for its size, so it sinks._  
> Check: `edu:sort:drawer-things:float-or-sink`
> _For grown-ups: Sorted everyday objects by material and predicted which would float or sink._

_He would eat anything now: a cherry stone, a fish bone, the crust off a crust._  
**Pinocchio**: The cricket said this would happen. Not this exactly. He didn’t mention soup.  
_Your Nubbin goes to the heap of sweepings in the corner and begins to dig, as if it has an idea._  

**Choices**

- **Dig with your Nubbin.**
    - You dig too. It isn’t dignified, but it’s company.
- **Tell Pinocchio there’s sure to be something.**
    - **Pinocchio**: There’d better be. I’m wooden all the way through, and all of it is empty.
