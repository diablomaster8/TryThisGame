# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A14: Homecoming

## A14-1. The Loom

- `a14-01-s1`: Step onto Ithaca and wait at the gate. · _Touch the page, then walk up to the courtyard gate._
- `a14-01-s2`: Sit with Telemachus and the old traveller, and listen. · _Take the seat by the far pillar, out of the suitors’ way._
- `a14-01-s3`: Watch from the stair as Penelope comes down. · _Stand at the foot of the stair, by the hall door._

#### `reader:a14-01-gate` · [main](main.md#a14-01-gate)

_Nova’s atlas opens on a page of blue: the sea, a scatter of islands, and one rough island with goats on it._  
_The blue turns warm and salty under your fingers. Then you are standing at a courtyard gate with your Nubbin._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, ask): find Greece and Ithaca on a map and say which way home lies from Troy  
> **Mentes**: This page shows Greece, in the south-east of Europe, with its ragged coast and hundreds of islands. Ithaca is one, out in the Ionian Sea.  
> **Mentes**: Troy stood far to the east, across the sea, in what is now Turkey. Odysseus is trying to sail home from there to Ithaca.  
> **Mentes**: Which way must he mostly sail?  
> - ✅ West → Yes. Troy is to the east of Ithaca, so home lies to the west. Greeks have sailed these seas for thousands of years.
> - East → Troy is already in the east. Going east would take him further from Ithaca. Home is back the way the sun sets: west.
> - North → Troy is a little north of Ithaca’s line, but mostly it lies far to the east. Home is mostly west.
> _For grown-ups: Located Greece, Ithaca and Troy and used compass directions: home from Troy lies to the west._

_Inside, young men lounge about playing counters. The air smells of roast meat and spilt wine._  
_Nobody comes to the gate. An old traveller with a bronze spear waits beside you._  
**Mentes**: A stranger should never be left standing at a gate. This was a good house once.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): xenia: the Greek word for guest-friendship  
> **Mentes**: The Greeks had a word for this: xenia (ZEN-ee-ah). It means guest-friendship.  
> **Mentes**: You feed a stranger and give them a seat before you ask a single question. Even your enemy’s guest was safe at your table.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the Greek word xenia, guest-friendship, and the custom of feeding strangers before asking their names._

_A young man sits among the noise as if he were somewhere else. He sees you, and goes red to the ears._  
**Telemachus**: Strangers at my gate, and nobody went out to you. Forgive us. Come in, both of you. Eat first, then tell me what you need.  

**Choices**

- **Wait to be asked in properly.**
    - **Telemachus**: Now you’re asked. Properly. I am Telemachus. It’s my house, for what that’s worth these days.
- **Step in. You’re hungry.**
    - You step in. The old traveller nods at you, as if that was the right answer too.
- **Let your Nubbin go first.**
    - Your Nubbin trots in ahead of you and waits by the hearth, as if it knows where guests go.
    - Telemachus laughs, for the first time in a while.

#### `reader:a14-01-gate-done` · [main](main.md#a14-01-gate-done)

_Telemachus lifts the old man’s spear and stands it in a rack of other spears. They are all dusty._  
_They were his father’s._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): read feelings from how someone acts  
> _Telemachus went red to the ears when he saw you waiting, and then looked at his father’s spears. What do you think he is feeling?_  
> - ✅ Embarrassed and sad. A host should have come out, and he misses his father. → Yes. His face, his hands and his eyes all tell you, even though he hasn’t said it. Noticing that is called empathy.
> - Angry with you for turning up. → He isn’t cross with you. He apologised straight away. Look at what he does: he blushes, then looks at the spears and goes quiet. That says embarrassed and sad.
> - Sleepy. → Sleepy people yawn and droop. Telemachus went red and stared at his father’s spears. That points to embarrassed and sad.
> _For grown-ups: Read a character’s feelings from his face and actions (embarrassed, missing his father)._

**Telemachus**: Over here, away from that lot. You won’t hear yourself think near them.  

#### `reader:a14-01-hearth` · [main](main.md#a14-01-hearth)

_A maid pours water over your hands from a golden jug, then brings bread and meat. The traveller eats like someone who has walked far._  
**Mentes**: I am Mentes. My father and yours were guest-friends once. I hoped to find your father at home.  
**Telemachus**: Then you are twenty years late, like everyone. He sailed to Troy when I was a baby, and he never came back.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): add two parts to make twenty years  
> _Odysseus spent ten years fighting at Troy, then another ten years trying to get home. How long has he been away in all?_  
> - ✅ 20 years → Yes: 10 + 10 = 20. Telemachus was a baby when he left, so he is about twenty himself now.
> - 10 years → Ten years is only the war. Add the ten years of the voyage home: 10 + 10 = 20.
> - 2 years → That is far too short. Ten years at war plus ten more at sea makes 10 + 10 = 20 years.
> _For grown-ups: Added 10 + 10 to find that Odysseus has been away for twenty years._

**Telemachus**: Those men are suitors. They want my mother to marry one of them, so until she chooses, they eat here. Every day.  
**Telemachus**: Pigs, sheep, goats, wine. They are eating my father’s house one dinner at a time.  
**Mentes**: You have his eyes. I knew them well.  
**Telemachus**: My mother says I am his son. I never saw him, so I can’t say.  
**Mentes**: He is not dead. He is held somewhere on the wide sea, and he is trying. He is a man who finds a way.  
**Mentes**: Call the men of Ithaca to an assembly. Then take a ship and go asking: old Nestor at Pylos, then Menelaus at Sparta.  

**Choices**

- **Ask Mentes how he knows.**
    - **Mentes**: Some things I know because I’ve seen them. Some, because I know him.
- **Tell Telemachus you’ll go with him.**
    - **Telemachus**: You would? You don’t even know us.
    - Mentes smiles into his cup. Here, it seems, a guest becomes part of the house for as long as they stay.
- **Eat, and listen.**
    - You eat, and listen. The bread is good: it’s the last of the good flour, and Telemachus gave it to the strangers.

#### `reader:a14-01-hearth-done` · [main](main.md#a14-01-hearth-done)

_Mentes crosses the yard. A bird flies up through the smoke-hole in the roof, and there is no Mentes._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, ask): warm air rises, which carries smoke upwards  
> _The bird went up through the hole in the roof, the same way the smoke goes. Why does smoke rise instead of staying in the room?_  
> - ✅ Hot air is lighter than the cooler air around it, so it floats up. → Yes. The fire heats the air, the hot air rises, and it carries the smoke with it. That is why old halls had a hole in the roof.
> - The smoke is trying to escape. → Smoke has no plans. It rises because the hot air carrying it is lighter than the cool air around it.
> - The roof pulls it up. → Nothing pulls it. Hot air is lighter than cold air, so it floats upwards, and the smoke goes along.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that heated air is lighter than cool air and rises, carrying smoke up and out._

**Telemachus**: That was no traveller.  
_He stands differently after that, as if someone has just told him whose son he is._  

#### `reader:a14-01-stair` · [main](main.md#a14-01-stair)

_In the hall the bard Phemius is singing for the suitors, about the Greeks coming home from Troy and the ones who never did._  

> **Lesson** (music, 1-PS4-1, ask): a thinner string makes a higher note  
> _Phemius plays a lyre. When he plucks a string it wobbles to and fro, and that wobble, called a vibration, is the sound you hear._  
> _His lyre’s strings are all about the same length, but some are thicker than others. Which string will sound the highest note?_  
> - ✅ The thinnest one → Yes. A thin string wobbles quickly, and quick wobbles make a high note. Thick strings wobble slowly and sound low.
> - The thickest one → That one wobbles slowly, and slow wobbles make a low note. For a high note you want a thin string.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that sound is vibration and that thin strings sound higher than thick ones._

_A woman comes down the stair with two maids, and stops in the doorway with a veil across her face._  
**Penelope**: Phemius, you know a hundred songs. Sing them another one. Not that one, not in this house.  
**Telemachus**: Mother, it is not his fault what happened at Troy. Let him sing. Many men did not come home.  
_Penelope looks at her son as if he has grown taller. Then she goes back up the stair._  
_Through her door you glimpse a great loom, with a cloth on it as wide as a sail, the colour of undyed wool._  
_Your Nubbin climbs two stairs to look. A maid turns it gently round and points it back down._  

#### `reader:a14-01-stair-done` · [main](main.md#a14-01-stair-done)

**Telemachus**: That cloth has been on that loom as long as the suitors have been in this hall. Ask Antinous about it. He loves telling it.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): use a small clue to work out what is odd  
> _A clue is a small detail that points to something bigger._  
> _Telemachus says the cloth has been on the loom for as long as the suitors have been eating his house. What is odd about that?_  
> - ✅ A cloth should be finished long before that. → Yes. Even a very big cloth gets finished in a few months. A cloth that is never done suggests somebody doesn’t want it done.
> - Nothing is odd. Cloth takes ages. → It does take time, but not years and years. A weaver who works every day should finish. Telemachus is giving you a clue.
> _For grown-ups: Used a small clue (a cloth that never gets finished) to notice that something is odd._


#### `reader:a14-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a14-01-complete)

_Night. The suitors go home to bed, promising to be back for breakfast, and they always keep that promise._  
_Old Eurycleia, who nursed Telemachus and his father before him, lights him upstairs with a torch._  
**Eurycleia**: I had your father on my knee, and then you. I will see you both in one room before I am done.  
**Telemachus**: Tomorrow I’ll call the assembly. Will you stand with me? Just stand. You needn’t say anything.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): think about sharing when you have little  
> _Telemachus’s house is being eaten bare, yet he gave two strangers his best bread. Why do you think he did?_  
> - Because a host looks after guests, however hard things are. → That’s a fair answer. In his world, welcoming a guest was close to a law, and he is proving he is not like the suitors.
> - Because sharing felt better than being as mean as the suitors. → That’s a fair answer too. He is choosing to be a different kind of person from the men who are taking from him.
> - Because he wanted something from you, such as help. → That’s a fair thought. People do ask for help from guests. But he fed you before he asked for anything, and that says something about him.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why someone might share when they have little (a think question with no single answer)._

_Eurycleia folds his tunic onto a peg and pulls the door shut by its silver ring._  
_You and your Nubbin sleep on fleeces in the porch, as guests do here. Somewhere above, very quietly, a loom is working._  
_It sounds as if it is going backwards._  

### Glossary

- **xenia**: The Greek word for guest-friendship: the custom of welcoming and feeding strangers.
- **suitor**: A man who wants to marry someone and keeps coming to ask.

## A14-2. The Assembly

- `a14-02-s1`: Stand behind Telemachus at the assembly. · _Follow him to the meeting-place and stand behind his father’s seat._
- `a14-02-s2`: Listen to Antinous tell the story of the web. · _Stay by Telemachus while Antinous speaks._
- `a14-02-s3`: Hold the torch while Penelope works. · _Keep the torch high and steady by the loom._

#### `reader:a14-02-assembly` · [main](main.md#a14-02-assembly)

_At dawn the heralds call the men of Ithaca to the meeting-place. Nobody has called them together since Odysseus sailed._  
_Telemachus comes in with a spear in his hand and two hounds at his heels. The old men make room for him in his father’s seat._  
_You stand behind the seat with your Nubbin. The suitors sit in a row along the front, and grin._  
**Telemachus**: Nobody’s army is coming. I called you about my own trouble. My father is lost, and my house is being eaten.  
**Telemachus**: Go home, all of you, and eat your own dinners. Aren’t you ashamed?  
_He throws the speaker’s staff on the ground, and his eyes fill with tears. For a moment nobody says anything._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): a meeting needs a way to give people turns  
> _At Ithaca’s meetings, only the person holding the speaker’s staff could talk. Why might a meeting need something like that?_  
> - ✅ So everyone knows whose turn it is, and one person talks at a time. → Yes. Taking turns lets every voice be heard. Much later, in the city of Athens, citizens met in an assembly and used rules like this to make decisions together.
> - So the speaker looks taller. → Nice idea, but no. It was for turns: whoever held the staff had the right to speak, and the others waited and listened.
> - So nobody has to listen. → The opposite. While someone holds the staff, everyone else is meant to listen, and then it is their turn.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a speaker’s staff was a way of taking turns in an early assembly._

_Then someone in the front row laughs, and the rest of the row joins in._  

**Choices**

- **Pick up the staff and hand it back to him.**
    - You pick up the staff and give it back to him. He takes it without looking at you, and holds it like a rope.
- **Stare at the one who laughed first.**
    - You look straight at him. He stops laughing, remembers he’s a suitor, and starts again, a bit louder.
- **Stay where you are, right behind him.**
    - You don’t move. Telemachus feels it, and his back straightens a little against the noise.

#### `reader:a14-02-assembly-done` · [main](main.md#a14-02-assembly-done)

_A tall man in a bright cloak stands up from the front row: Antinous, the loudest of the suitors._  
**Antinous**: Blame us? Blame your mother. She is the cleverest woman in Greece, and she has led us on for years.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): who is to blame, and who made which choice  
> _Antinous says it is Penelope’s fault that the suitors are eating Telemachus’s house. Is he right?_  
> - No. Coming and staying was the suitors’ own choice. → That’s a fair answer. Penelope may have kept them waiting, but nobody made them come, and nobody made them eat the pigs.
> - Partly. She did keep them waiting. → That’s a fair answer too. Both can be true, but only one side was taking things that weren’t theirs.
> - It’s hard to say when everyone blames someone else. → That’s a wise thought. When people point fingers, it helps to ask who made which choice.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about blame: separating one person’s choices from another’s (a think question)._


#### `reader:a14-02-antinous` · [main](main.md#a14-02-antinous)

**Antinous**: Three years she promised, and three years we waited. Shall I tell you how she did it? Everyone else knows.  
**Antinous**: She put up a great wooden frame in her room, and began weaving the finest, biggest cloth you ever saw.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): the word text comes from a Latin word for weaving  
> _Weaving gave us words. The Latin word texere means “to weave”, and it gave us textile, and also the word for the writing on this page._  
> _Which of these words comes from texere?_  
> - ✅ text → Yes. A text is something woven out of words, the way a textile is woven out of thread. That’s why we talk about weaving a tale.
> - tent → A tent comes from a Latin word meaning “stretch”, not “weave”. The word from texere is text: words woven together.
> - tell → Tell comes from Old English, where it meant both “count” and “relate”. It isn’t from texere. The one that is: text.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that “text” and “textile” both come from the Latin for weaving._

**Antinous**: She said it was a shroud, a funeral cloth, for old Laertes, Odysseus’s father. “Let me finish it first,” she said, “or the women will talk.”  
**Antinous**: Reasonable. We are reasonable men. We waited.  
_As he talks, the edges of the meeting-place go soft, as if the page itself were listening._  
_The stone turns to floorboards, and the daylight to torchlight. You are in an upstairs room, holding a torch you don’t remember picking up._  

#### `reader:a14-02-antinous-done` · [main](main.md#a14-02-antinous-done)

_In front of you stands the great loom. Penelope sits at it, alone, with a small knife._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiply months in a year by three years  
> _Antinous says Penelope kept the suitors waiting for three years. There are 12 months in every year. How many months is three years?_  
> - ✅ 36 months → Yes. Three groups of twelve: 12 + 12 + 12 = 36, or 3 × 12 = 36.
> - 15 months → That is 3 + 12. We need three whole years of twelve months each: 3 × 12 = 36.
> - 24 months → That is two years (2 × 12). Three years is one more twelve: 24 + 12 = 36.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied 3 × 12 to turn three years into 36 months._


#### `reader:a14-02-loom` · [main](main.md#a14-02-loom)

**Antinous**: Every day we watched her weave. And every night, by torchlight, she took the stitches out again.  
_Penelope pulls a thread, and a day’s work comes undone in one long, soft sigh. She winds it back on the shuttle for tomorrow._  
**Penelope**: Hold the torch a little higher. There. Thank you.  
**Penelope**: Every row I weave is one more day for him to come home. Every row I take out gives that day back.  
**Penelope**: The long threads stand still and wait. The short one goes back and forth, back and forth. I know which one I am.  

> **Lesson** (art, L.3.4, ask): how a loom works: warp and weft  
> _On a loom, long threads are stretched tight and held still. They are called the warp._  
> _A shorter thread, the weft, is passed over one warp thread and under the next, back and forth._  
> _Penelope says the short one goes back and forth. Which thread is the weft?_  
> - ✅ The short thread that goes back and forth → Yes. Over, under, over, under: that pattern is how the weft locks the warp into cloth.
> - The long threads that stand still → Those are the warp, held tight on the frame. The weft is the short thread that travels across them.
> _For grown-ups: Learned the parts of a loom: still warp threads and a back-and-forth weft thread woven over and under._

_Your Nubbin holds the loose end of the thread so it doesn’t tangle. It is very serious about this._  
**Penelope**: They think I am slow. I am the fastest weaver on Ithaca. I have to be, to unpick it all by dawn.  
_Out in the dark passage, a floorboard creaks. Someone has been listening at the door._  

**Choices**

- **Go and look in the passage.**
    - You look, and a maid’s skirt whisks round the corner and is gone. You know where she’s going.
- **Tell Penelope someone heard.**
    - **Penelope**: I know. I’ve known for a week which one it would be. I thought I’d finish tonight’s row anyway.
- **Hold the torch steady. Keep going.**
    - You keep the torch steady, and she keeps going. Neither of you looks at the door.

#### `reader:a14-02-loom-done` · [main](main.md#a14-02-loom-done)

_The torchlight goes thin, and it is daylight again. You are standing in the meeting-place with empty hands._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): add and subtract to see why a total stays the same  
> _Say Penelope wove 12 rows on Monday and unpicked 12 that night. She did the same on Tuesday._  
> _How many rows does the cloth have on Wednesday morning?_  
> - ✅ 0 rows → Yes: 12 − 12 = 0 each night, so she is back where she started. That is why the cloth never got finished.
> - 24 rows → That is 12 + 12, the rows she wove. But she took out 12 each night as well: 24 − 24 = 0.
> - 12 rows → Close. Monday ends at 0 (12 − 12), and Tuesday ends at 0 again. So Wednesday begins with no rows at all.
> _For grown-ups: Used addition and subtraction to see that weaving 12 rows and unpicking 12 leaves 0._

**Antinous**: A maid told us. We caught her at it, so she had to finish the thing.  
**Antinous**: Send your mother back to her father’s house, boy, and let him marry her off. Until then, we eat.  

#### `reader:a14-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a14-02-complete)

_The assembly breaks up, and nobody helps Telemachus. One old man, Mentor, tries, and is shouted down._  
_That evening at the shore, Mentor finds him again: or someone with Mentor’s face, who walks too lightly for an old man._  
**Mentor**: A ship, and twenty men to row it. I have found both. Fetch food for the voyage, and tell no one.  
_Eurycleia fills twelve jars of wine and twenty measures of barley-meal, crying the whole time._  
**Eurycleia**: Your father sailed over the sea and never came back. Now you. Who will I have left to scold?  
**Telemachus**: Don’t tell my mother. Not for eleven or twelve days, unless she asks.  
_At nightfall the ship slides out of the harbour. You sit in the stern with your Nubbin, and a wind comes up behind you from nowhere._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): a sail catches the push of the wind  
> _A sail is a big sheet that catches moving air. The wind pushes on it, and that push moves the whole ship._  
> _Which wind will carry your ship straight ahead?_  
> - ✅ A wind blowing from behind the ship → Yes. A wind from behind pushes the sail forwards, and the ship follows. Sailors call it a following wind.
> - A wind blowing straight at the front → That wind pushes against the ship and slows it. A ship wants the wind at its back.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that wind pushes on a sail, and a wind from behind moves a ship forwards._

_Mentor sits by the steering oar and looks up at the sail. The wind is exactly as strong as it needs to be._  

### Glossary

- **shroud**: A cloth used to wrap someone who has died.
- **warp**: The long threads held tight on a loom while the cloth is woven across them.
- **weft**: The thread that goes back and forth over and under the warp to make cloth.

## A14-3. Nestor and Menelaus

- `a14-03-s1`: Land at Pylos and ask old Nestor for news. · _Walk up the beach to the feast and sit where you’re put._
- `a14-03-s2`: Ride to Sparta with Nestor’s son. · _Climb into the chariot behind Telemachus._
- `a14-03-s3`: Hear what the Old Man of the Sea told Menelaus. · _Sit with Menelaus by the fire in the morning._

#### `reader:a14-03-pylos` · [main](main.md#a14-03-pylos)

_Sandy Pylos, at sunrise. The whole town is on the beach at a great feast to honour Poseidon, god of the sea._  
**Telemachus**: I can’t. I don’t know how to talk to an old king. What if I get it wrong?  
**Mentor**: Some of it you will think of yourself. The rest will be given you. Go on.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): find ways to feel braver before something nerve-racking  
> _Telemachus is frightened of talking to an old king and getting it wrong. What could help a person feel braver at a moment like that?_  
> - A slow breath, and remembering why he came. → That’s a fair answer. Slow breathing calms the body, and remembering your reason gives you something stronger than the fear.
> - A friend standing close by. → That’s a fair answer. You are that friend, right now. Feeling that someone is on your side can make a hard thing smaller.
> - Practising a first sentence in his head. → That’s a fair answer. A first sentence gets you started, and the rest often follows. Telemachus has one ready: his name and his father’s.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about ways to manage nerves before a hard conversation (a think question)._

_Nestor’s son runs to meet the strangers before anyone asks who they are, and sits you all on soft fleeces on the sand._  
_Nestor is so old that he was old at Troy. He has outlived nearly everyone he went there with._  
**Nestor**: Eat first. Then names. Then, if you like, sorrows. There is always time for sorrows.  
**Telemachus**: I’m Odysseus’s son. I have come for news of my father. Any news, even bad.  
**Nestor**: His son! You talk just like him. I would never have thought a young man could sound so like him.  
**Nestor**: But I know nothing, child. We left Troy together, quarrelled, and parted at sea. He turned back. I came home.  

**Choices**

- **Ask who might know.**
    - **Nestor**: Menelaus. He was blown about longer than any of us, and came home last. Go to Sparta. My son will drive you.
- **Ask if there’s any hope.**
    - **Nestor**: With that one? There is always hope. I never once saw him stuck for long.
- **Let your Nubbin sit by the old king.**
    - Your Nubbin climbs onto the fleece beside Nestor. He rests an old hand on it, as if it were a grandchild, and goes on talking.

#### `reader:a14-03-pylos-done` · [main](main.md#a14-03-pylos-done)

_When the feast is over, Mentor rises, and is a sea-eagle, and is gone over the water. Everyone on the beach falls silent._  
**Nestor**: That was Athena, daughter of Zeus. She stood by your father at Troy. If she walks beside you, you won’t go far wrong.  
_Nestor’s people honour her on the shore in the old way. You stand back with your Nubbin and keep quiet, as guests should._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): show respect at someone else’s ceremony  
> _Nestor’s people are honouring Athena in their own way. You and your Nubbin stand back and keep quiet. Why is that good manners for a guest?_  
> - ✅ It shows respect for what matters to other people. → Yes. You don’t have to share a belief to be respectful of it. For some people in Greece today, these old gods are still honoured, so a quiet guest is a kind guest.
> - It means nobody will notice you. → That might be a side effect, but it isn’t the reason. Standing quietly says: this matters to you, so it matters to me to be careful.
> - Guests aren’t allowed to see ceremonies. → Guests were often welcome to watch. The point is how you behave: quietly and politely, so you don’t spoil it for the people it belongs to.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that guests show respect at other people’s ceremonies by staying quiet and polite._


#### `reader:a14-03-sparta` · [main](main.md#a14-03-sparta)

_Two days in a chariot over the hills, with Nestor’s son Pisistratus at the reins. Your Nubbin rides up front and leans into the corners._  
_Sparta lies in a hollow among the hills. Menelaus’s house gleams with bronze and gold, amber, silver and ivory._  
**Telemachus**: Pisistratus, look at it. Is this what the house of Zeus is like?  
**Menelaus**: No mortal’s house is like Zeus’s, boy. And I would give two-thirds of all this to have back the friends who died at Troy.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.NF.A.1, ask): two-thirds means two of three equal parts  
> _Menelaus says he would give away two-thirds of his treasure. Picture it split into 3 equal piles, each one third._  
> _How many piles would he give away?_

> **Lesson** (science, ask): whirlpools form where strong currents squeeze through a gap or meet  
> _Real whirlpools form where strong currents squeeze through a narrow gap or crash into each other._  
> _Many people think this story was inspired by the narrow Strait of Messina, between Italy and Sicily. Where is a whirlpool most likely?_  
> - ✅ A narrow strait with strong currents → Yes. Squeezed water speeds up and starts to spin. Sailors in Homer’s time had good reason to fear places like that.
> - A still pond on a calm day → A still pond has no strong currents, so there is nothing to make the water spin.
> - A puddle in the road → A puddle has no current at all. Whirlpools need moving water squeezed into a narrow space.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that whirlpools form where strong currents squeeze through narrow gaps._

> _Telemachus wraps an olive cutting in wet cloth so that it can grow into a new tree. Why keep the cutting wet?_

#### `reader:a14-margin-hazel` · [main](main.md#a14-margin-hazel)

_Nova’s atlas lies open at the Argos page: the gate, the heap, the old dog in the sun._  
_In the margin, in soft pencil you have never noticed, there is a note. You know the hand._  
_“Argos waited twenty years and still knew him, even in rags.”_  
_“That’s why our gate sticks.”_  
_“So I’d hear. H.”_  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): cause and effect: a note explains why the gate sticks  
> _Hazel’s note says Argos waited, and that is why the gate sticks, “so I’d hear”. What does she mean by that?_  
> - ✅ A sticking gate lets you hear anyone coming, the way Argos waited and listened. → Yes. She links a cause (the gate sticks) to an effect (you hear visitors) and to the old dog who waited. A margin note can say a lot in a few words.
> - Gates are always broken. → The note says the sticking is on purpose: it’s a way of hearing people come.
> - Argos opened the gate himself. → Argos is only in the story on the page. The gate sticks so that the Keeper will always hear who is arriving.
> _For grown-ups: Read a margin note for cause and effect (the gate sticks so you will hear who comes)._

_You sit with the page open. Outside, faintly, the gate creaks in the wind, and doesn’t open._
