# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A15: The Humbug

## A15-1. The Grey Prairie

- `a15-01-s1`: Step into the grey page and find the farmhouse. · _Touch the page, then walk across the prairie to the only house on it._
- `a15-01-s2`: Help Uncle Henry and Aunt Em make ready for the storm. · _Watch the sky to the north, then help at the square door in the floor._
- `a15-01-s3`: Ride out the cyclone, then step out of the page where the house comes down. · _Keep hold of your Nubbin. When the edge of the page curls, step through._

#### `reader:a15-01-page` · [main](main.md#a15-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page that is almost all grey: flat land to the edges of the paper, and one small house._  
_Someone painted the house once, long ago. Years of sun and rain have worn every scrap of colour away._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-ESS2-1, ask): weathering: sun, wind and rain wear things away  
> _The paint on the house has worn off. What slowly wore it away?_  
> - ✅ Sun, wind and rain, over many years → Yes. Slow wearing away is called weathering. It wears down rock the same way, only far more slowly.
> - One big snowstorm → Snow can help, but this took years. Sun, wind and rain, bit by bit, is weathering.
> - Toto → Toto is good at many things, but not that one. It was the weather, over many years.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that sun, wind and rain slowly wear materials away (weathering)._

_The grey goes soft under your fingers. Then it is dry grass, and you and your Nubbin are standing in it._  
_There is no tree, no hill, no fence. The sky comes all the way down to the ground on every side._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, 4-ESS2-2, ask): what a prairie looks like; where Kansas is  
> _Kansas is in the middle of the United States, on the Great Plains. What would you see for miles around?_  
> - ✅ A flat sea of grass → Yes. Wide, flat grassland is called prairie. In 1900 the farms out here were far apart.
> - Snowy mountains → The Rocky Mountains are far to the west. Kansas itself is flat grassland, called prairie.
> - A rainforest → A rainforest is hot and wet and full of trees. Kansas is flat, dry grassland, called prairie.
> _For grown-ups: Read the land: Kansas is on the flat grassland of the Great Plains, called prairie._

_A small black dog races up and barks at your Nubbin, as if he has waited all day for something new._  
**Dorothy**: Toto! Come back!  
**Dorothy**: Oh. Hello.  
**Dorothy**: I didn’t know anybody lived out this way.  
**Dorothy**: I’m Dorothy, and that’s Toto. He’s the only thing round here that isn’t grey, and he knows it.  
_Your Nubbin and Toto sniff noses. Toto decides it is all right, and sneezes._  

**Choices**

- **Tell her your name.**
    - **Dorothy**: That’s a nice name. It sounds like it comes from somewhere with trees.
- **Ask why everything is grey.**
    - **Dorothy**: The sun, mostly, and the wind. Aunt Em came out here young and pretty, Uncle Henry says.
    - **Dorothy**: It took the shine off her.
- **Let your Nubbin play with Toto.**
    - Your Nubbin and Toto race twice round you and tumble in the dust. It is the first noise the prairie has heard all day.

#### `reader:a15-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a15-01-page-done)

**Dorothy**: Come up to the house. Aunt Em will want to look at you.  
**Dorothy**: She watches the sky like a sailor watching the sea. She is always waiting for the weather to turn.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): similes  
> _Dorothy says Aunt Em watches the sky like a sailor watching the sea. What is being compared, and why?_  
> - ✅ Aunt Em and a sailor; both watch for weather that could turn dangerous → Exactly. A comparison using “like” or “as” is called a simile. This one tells you Aunt Em is wary.
> - The sky and the sea; both are blue → Good eye, but look who is doing the watching. Aunt Em is compared to a sailor.
> - Dorothy and Toto → Not quite. The sentence compares how Aunt Em watches with how a sailor watches.
> _For grown-ups: Read a simile and said what it compares and what it tells us about a character._

_The house is a long way off across the grass. It is the only thing to walk towards._  

#### `reader:a15-01-storm` · [main](main.md#a15-01-storm)

_The house is one room: a stove, a table, a cupboard, two beds and a square door in the floor._  
_Uncle Henry stands on the step, looking at the sky. It has gone a darker grey than usual._  
**Uncle Henry**: Storm’s coming, Em. A big one.  
**Uncle Henry**: I’m going to see to the animals.  
_From the north comes a low wail. The long grass bows flat, all the way to the edge of the world._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-ESS2-1, ask): tornadoes come when warm damp air meets cold dry air, mostly in spring  
> **Uncle Henry**: Storms like this build when warm, damp air from the south meets cold, dry air from the north. When does that happen most in Kansas?  
> - ✅ In spring and early summer → That’s the one. Spring and early summer bring most of the tornadoes. Baum’s book calls them cyclones.
> - In the middle of winter → Winter has its own storms, but the big spinning ones come mostly in spring and early summer.
> - Only in autumn → Autumn can have them, but spring and early summer are the busiest time.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that tornadoes (Baum’s “cyclones”) form where warm damp air meets cold dry air, mostly in spring._

**Aunt Em**: Dorothy! The cellar!  
**Aunt Em**: Now, child!  
_Aunt Em flings open the trap door and climbs down the ladder into the small dark hole beneath._  
_Toto jumps out of Dorothy’s arms and hides under the bed. She goes down on her knees after him._  
**Dorothy**: Toto! Come out!  
**Dorothy**: Oh, you bad dog, come out this minute!  

**Choices**

- **Hold the trap door open for her.**
    - You kneel and hold the trap door. Aunt Em is calling from below, and the whole house has begun to shake.
- **Reach under the bed for Toto.**
    - You get a hand on Toto’s collar and pass him, wriggling, to Dorothy. The floor is already shaking.
- **Grab your Nubbin first.**
    - Your Nubbin is in your arms before you know you have reached for it. It is trembling, and so is the floor.

#### `reader:a15-01-storm-done` · [main](main.md#a15-01-storm-done)

_The wind gives a great shriek. The house shakes so hard that Dorothy sits down on the floor._  
_Then the house turns round twice, quite slowly, and lifts off the ground like a balloon._  
_Far below, the cellar stays dark and still, and Aunt Em is safe in it._  

> **Lesson** (health, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): where to shelter in a tornado  
> _Aunt Em knew where to go. Where is the safest place in a tornado?_  
> - ✅ Underground, or in a room with no windows on the lowest floor → Quite right. Glass breaks, and wind throws things about. Low and windowless is best, and go early.
> - Next to a window, to watch it come → Never. Windows shatter. Go to the lowest room with no windows, and stay away from glass.
> - In a parked car → A car can be tossed about. A cellar, or a low inside room with no windows, is far safer.
> _For grown-ups: Learned where to shelter in a tornado: underground, or a low room with no windows._


#### `reader:a15-01-cyclone` · [main](main.md#a15-01-cyclone)

_The house is carried up into the very middle of the cyclone, where it is strangely quiet. It rocks along like a cradle._  
_Toto runs about barking. Then he goes too near the open trap door and falls in._  
_The wind holds him up. Dorothy lies flat, reaches down, and pulls him back by one ear._  
**Dorothy**: There. That is quite enough adventure for one dog.  
_Hours go by, three of them, at sixty kilometres an hour. Dorothy shuts the trap door and lies down with Toto._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): multiplying a speed by a time to find a distance  
> _Suppose the house flies at 60 kilometres an hour for 3 hours. How far does it travel?_  
> - ✅ 180 km → Yes. 60 + 60 + 60 = 180, or 3 × 60 = 180. That is a long way to travel in a house, and nobody even steered.
> - 63 km → That adds 60 and 3, but speed and time are multiplied. Three hours of 60 km each is 3 × 60 = 180 km.
> - 120 km → That is only two hours, and there are three: 60 + 60 + 60 = 180 km.
> _For grown-ups: Found a distance from a speed and a time: 3 × 60 = 180 km._

**Dorothy**: If the house is going to fall, I would rather be asleep for it.  
_You stay awake with your Nubbin in your lap. Somebody ought to._  
_The edge of the page has begun to curl. Through the gap you can see grass: green grass, and a great deal of it._  

**Choices**

- **Step through the gap with your Nubbin.**
    - You step through. Behind you the house drops the last few feet, with a bump that makes the whole page jump.
- **Take a last look at Dorothy, asleep.**
    - She is fast asleep, with one arm over Toto. You step through the gap, and the house comes down behind you.

#### `reader:a15-01-cyclone-done` · [main](main.md#a15-01-cyclone-done)

_You land in soft grass. After all that grey everything dazzles: green banks, tall trees heavy with fruit, a brook, birds._  
_Behind you the grey farmhouse sits in the middle of it all, a little crooked, as if it had sat down suddenly._  
_Your Nubbin cannot decide whether to be thrilled or nervous. It does a bit of both, and falls over._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): feeling two things at once  
> _Your Nubbin is thrilled and nervous at the same time. Can a person feel two feelings at once?_  
> - Yes, they mix together → That’s a fair answer. Feelings often arrive in a pair, like excitement and nerves before something new.
> - No, only one at a time → That’s a fair answer. Sometimes one feeling is so big it hides the other. Notice what the quiet one says.
> - I am not sure → That’s a fair answer, and an honest one. Next time something new happens, check: is there more than one feeling in there?
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether two feelings can happen together, using a new place as an example._


#### `reader:a15-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a15-01-complete)

_Your Nubbin rolls over and over in the green grass, the way it does in the Grove after rain._  

> **Lesson** (art, RL.4.3, notice): contrast: a dull colour makes a bright one glow  
> _The artist William Wallace Denslow drew the pictures for the first edition of this book, in 1900._  
> _Artists often put a dull colour next to a bright one so the bright one seems to glow._  
> _That is why green looks so rich after all that grey._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that contrast between a dull and a bright colour makes the bright one stand out._

_The door of the house opens. Dorothy looks out with Toto in her arms, and her mouth falls open._  
**Dorothy**: Oh. Oh, my.  
**Dorothy**: Where has Kansas gone?  
_Little people in blue are coming towards the house, slowly, as if not sure it will stay where it is._  
_Under one corner of the house two feet in silver shoes are sticking out. Only your Nubbin has noticed._  

### Glossary

- **prairie**: A wide, flat stretch of grassland with very few trees.
- **cyclone**: Baum’s word for a tornado: a spinning funnel of wind that comes down from a storm cloud.
- **cellar**: A small room under a house, below the ground.

## A15-2. The Council with the Munchkins

- `a15-02-s1`: Greet the little people and the old woman in white. · _Walk round the house to where the Munchkins are waiting._
- `a15-02-s2`: Ask the way home, and see what the slate says. · _Watch the Witch of the North’s hat while she counts._
- `a15-02-s3`: Help Boq’s household lay the table for the night. · _Carry the blue dishes from the dresser to the long tables outside._

#### `reader:a15-02-council` · [main](main.md#a15-02-council)

_Three men and a woman come up, no taller than Dorothy. They wear blue, and round pointed hats with little bells that tinkle._  
_The woman is very old, in a white gown sprinkled with stars. She bows low to Dorothy._  
**The Witch of the North**: Welcome to the land of the Munchkins, my dear. This is the East, and they thank you for setting them free.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, ask): compass directions  
> _The old woman says this is the East. If you stand facing north, which hand points east?_  
> - ✅ Your right hand → Yes. Facing north, east is on your right and west is on your left. “Never Eat Soggy Waffles” goes clockwise: north, east, south, west.
> - Your left hand → That one is west. Facing north, east is on your right.
> - Straight ahead → Straight ahead is north. East is a quarter turn to your right.
> _For grown-ups: Used a compass: facing north, east is on your right._

**Dorothy**: I think you have muddled me up with somebody else. I haven’t done anything.  
**The Witch of the North**: Your house did, which comes to the same. Look there, under the corner.  
**Dorothy**: Oh. Oh, the poor thing.  
**Dorothy**: It came down right on top of her.  
**The Witch of the North**: She was the Wicked Witch of the East. She kept these people working for her night and day.  
**A Munchkin**: Night and day! For years!  
**A Munchkin**: And now we are free!  
_When you look again, the feet are gone. Only the silver shoes are left, lying in the grass._  
**The Witch of the North**: The shoes are yours now. They hold some magic, though nobody ever learned what.  

**Choices**

- **Tell Dorothy to try them on.**
    - **Dorothy**: They fit exactly, as if they had been waiting for me.
- **Tell her to be careful with them.**
    - **The Witch of the North**: Wise. A charm is like a dog: best to know what it will do before you take it for a walk.
- **Let your Nubbin sniff the shoes.**
    - Your Nubbin sniffs, sneezes, and sits on the shoes, as if somebody ought to keep them warm.

#### `reader:a15-02-council-done` · [main](main.md#a15-02-council-done)

**Dorothy**: Thank you. But please, can you help me get back to my Aunt Em and Uncle Henry?  
**Dorothy**: They will be so worried.  
**The Witch of the North**: Ah. That is harder.  
**The Witch of the North**: Let me see.  
_Dorothy’s voice wobbled when she asked. Asking was not easy, and she did it anyway._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): asking for help  
> _Dorothy asked strangers for help. Is asking for help a sign of weakness or of strength?_  
> - Strength: it takes courage → That’s a fair answer. It often takes more courage to ask than to struggle on alone.
> - Weakness: you should manage alone → That’s a fair answer, and many people feel it. Yet even the strongest people ask. Dorothy could not cross a desert alone.
> - It depends what you ask → That’s a fair answer. Choosing who to ask, and when, is a skill too.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether asking for help shows strength or weakness._


#### `reader:a15-02-slate` · [main](main.md#a15-02-slate)

_The Munchkins shake their heads. There are great deserts to the east, the south and the west, they say, and nobody crosses them._  
**A Munchkin**: And to the north, where she lives, it is desert too. Nobody ever has.  
_Dorothy begins to cry. The Munchkins, to be kind, begin to cry too, and their hat bells tinkle sadly._  
_The Witch of the North takes off her pointed hat, balances it on her nose, and counts: one, two, three._  
_The hat turns into a slate. In big white chalk letters it tells Dorothy to go to the City of Emeralds._  
**The Witch of the North**: Keep to the road of yellow brick and you cannot go wrong. I cannot come with you, but I can give you my kiss.  
**The Witch of the North**: Nobody in all this land would dare hurt someone I have kissed.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): why a character acts as she does  
> _Why does the Witch of the North kiss Dorothy on the forehead?_  
> - ✅ To protect her on the road → Yes. She cannot go along, so she gives Dorothy protection. A character’s motive is the reason behind what she does.
> - To say a polite goodbye → A kiss is a kind goodbye, but listen to what she says next. The mark is meant to protect.
> - Because she is bored → Not bored at all. She is worried for Dorothy and wants her safe.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a character’s motive from what she says._

_She kisses Dorothy on the forehead. Where her lips touch there is a round, shining mark._  

**Choices**

- **Ask if she will kiss you too.**
    - **The Witch of the North**: You are only visiting, dear. Visitors bring their own luck.
    - **The Witch of the North**: Mind you share it.
- **Look closely at the mark.**
    - It shines faintly, like a coin at the bottom of a well. Dorothy cannot see it, but everyone else can.
- **Hold your Nubbin up to say goodbye.**
    - The Witch of the North taps your Nubbin once on the nose. It goes cross-eyed with pride, and stays that way some time.

#### `reader:a15-02-slate-done` · [main](main.md#a15-02-slate-done)

_Then she spins round three times on her left heel, and is gone. Toto barks at the place where she was._  
_The Munchkins wave. All their hat bells ring together, tinkle, jingle, ting._  
_You notice that the smallest bells ring the highest notes, and a bigger bell on a fence post gives a deeper one._  

> **Lesson** (music, 4-PS3-2, ask): pitch: small things make higher sounds  
> _Which makes the higher note: a tiny hat bell or a big bell on a fence post?_  
> - ✅ The tiny hat bell → Yes. Smaller things vibrate faster, and faster vibrations make a higher pitch. Big bells are slower and deeper.
> - The big bell → The other way round. The big bell vibrates slowly, so its note is deep. The tiny bell is high.
> - They sound the same → Listen again. Small and big make different notes. Small is higher, big is lower.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that smaller bells vibrate faster and sound higher._


#### `reader:a15-02-boq` · [main](main.md#a15-02-boq)

_By evening you have walked a fair way along the road of yellow brick. Dorothy has changed into her clean blue-and-white checked frock._  
_You come to a big house where a rich Munchkin called Boq is giving a feast. Everyone for miles seems to be there._  
**Boq**: Welcome, Sorceress! And the Sorceress’s friend!  
**Boq**: And the Sorceress’s friend’s… whatever that is.  
**Dorothy**: I am not a Sorceress. I am Dorothy.  
**Boq**: Only witches and sorceresses wear white, and your frock has white in it. Now: the tables!  
**Boq**: Five of them, and every plate must be blue.  
_You carry dishes. Each table gets four blue plates, and each plate gets a little iced cake._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 5 × 4  
> _Five tables, with four blue plates at each. How many plates do you carry?_  
> - ✅ 20 → Yes. Five groups of four is 5 × 4 = 20. You could also count in fours: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20.
> - 9 → That adds 5 and 4. Five tables of four plates is 5 × 4, which is 20.
> - 16 → That is four tables of four. There are five tables, so 5 × 4 = 20.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied equal groups: 5 tables of 4 plates make 20._

**Boq**: And where shall the Sorceress’s friend’s whatever-it-is sit?  

**Choices**

- **Next to Toto, at the end.**
    - Your Nubbin and Toto share the end of the bench, and a blue cake, and nobody minds at all.
- **Next to Dorothy, of course.**
    - **Boq**: Of course! A place of honour!
    - **Boq**: Fetch the tall stool!
- **Under the table, where the crumbs land.**
    - **Boq**: Ah, a practical creature. My grandmother was the same.

#### `reader:a15-02-boq-done` · [main](main.md#a15-02-boq-done)

_Three Munchkins strike up on fiddles and everyone else dances. Dorothy laughs, for the first time since the cyclone._  
_The plates are a deep, dark blue. The cups are a pale, sky blue._  
_You wonder how you would paint both with only one pot of blue._  

> **Lesson** (art, L.3.4, ask): tints and shades  
> _You have one pot of blue paint. How do you make the pale blue of the cups?_  
> - ✅ Add white → Yes. Adding white makes a tint, a paler colour. Adding black makes a shade, a darker one.
> - Add black → Black makes it darker, like the plates. For pale blue, add white.
> - Add more blue → More blue makes it stronger, not paler. To lighten a colour, add white.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that adding white makes a paler tint and adding black a darker shade._


#### `reader:a15-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a15-02-complete)

_Boq gives Dorothy his best bed, with blue sheets. She falls asleep at once, with Toto curled at her feet._  
_You sit up a while with Boq on his front step. The yellow road runs off into the dark, glowing faintly._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): welcoming a stranger  
> _Boq welcomed a girl, a dog and a creature nobody could name. What makes a stranger feel welcome?_  
> - ✅ Someone says hello and finds them a seat → Just so. A kind word and a place at the table cost very little, and mean a great deal.
> - Asking a lot of questions about them → Questions can be friendly, but too many can feel like a test. A hello and a seat come first.
> - Leaving them alone until they speak → Some quiet is fine. But a first hello makes the first step much easier.
> _For grown-ups: Talked about what makes a newcomer feel welcome._

**Boq**: It is a long way to the City of Emeralds. Many days.  
**Boq**: Mind the wild parts.  
**Boq**: But she has the Witch’s kiss, and the silver shoes. And she has you, which I suppose is something.  
_Your Nubbin falls asleep beside the silver shoes, one paw on each, as if they might walk off without it._  

### Glossary

- **Munchkin**: One of the small people of the eastern land of Oz, who wear blue.
- **feast**: A big meal with lots of people and lots of good food.

## A15-3. The Scarecrow

- `a15-03-s1`: Help lift the Scarecrow down from his pole. · _Climb the fence into the cornfield and hold the pole steady while Dorothy lifts._
- `a15-03-s2`: Help the Scarecrow find his feet. · _Walk beside him on the yellow bricks and catch him when he tips._
- `a15-03-s3`: Sit by the road and listen while Dorothy tells him about Kansas. · _Sit with them on the bank while Dorothy eats her bread._

#### `reader:a15-03-pole` · [main](main.md#a15-03-pole)

_In the morning the road runs past a big field of ripe corn._  
_High on a pole in the middle, keeping the birds off, there is a Scarecrow._  
_His head is a little sack stuffed with straw, with a face painted on it. He wears an old pointed blue hat._  
_As you look at him, he winks: slowly, with his left eye._  
**Dorothy**: Did you see that? I must have dreamed it.  
**Dorothy**: Scarecrows in Kansas never wink.  
**The Scarecrow**: Good morning. How do you do?  
**The Scarecrow**: Not very well myself, if you want the truth. It is dreadfully dull, up a pole all day.  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): materials and their properties  
> _The Scarecrow is stuffed with straw. Why is straw a good filling for him, and not iron?_  
> - ✅ Straw is light and soft, so it is easy to carry and cannot hurt anyone → Precisely. Light and soft. I am much easier to lift than a lump of iron, and I never bruise anyone I fall on.
> - Straw is stronger than iron → Hardly. Iron is much stronger. It is also far too heavy and hard for a person like me.
> - Straw is shinier → I am sorry to say that I am not shiny at all. The point of straw is that it is light and soft.
> _For grown-ups: Compared materials (straw, iron) by their properties and chose by what each is for._

**The Scarecrow**: There is a pole up the back of my coat, you see. If you could lift me off it, I would be ever so grateful.  
_Dorothy reaches up with both arms. Someone must hold the pole steady, or it will go over with him on it._  

**Choices**

- **Hold the pole steady.**
    - You hold the pole. Dorothy lifts him off, and the pole jumps in your hands as if it is glad too.
- **Stand underneath, ready to catch him.**
    - Dorothy lifts him off. He tips, and lands on you, which is like being hit by a large, polite pillow.

#### `reader:a15-03-pole-done` · [main](main.md#a15-03-pole-done)

**The Scarecrow**: Thank you. Thank you both.  
**The Scarecrow**: I feel ever so much more like a person down here.  
_He stretches, yawns, and falls flat on his face. Then he gets up, and falls over sideways._  
_You notice that he stands with his feet together, and wobbles like a skittle._  

> **Lesson** (health, 3-PS2-1, ask): balance: a wide base is steadier  
> _How could the Scarecrow stand more steadily?_  
> - ✅ With his feet wide apart → Yes. A wide base is steadier, because the middle of your weight stays over your feet. Try it yourself.
> - With his feet together → That is how he is wobbling. A narrow base tips easily. Feet apart is steadier.
> - On one foot → One foot is the hardest of all. A wide stance on two feet is steadiest.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that standing with feet apart gives a steadier base._


#### `reader:a15-03-feet` · [main](main.md#a15-03-feet)

_The Scarecrow has never walked before. His legs go wherever they like, and they do not consult each other first._  
**The Scarecrow**: I was only made the day before yesterday. Before that I was nothing at all, which is restful, but not interesting.  
**Dorothy**: I am going to the City of Emeralds, to ask the great Oz to send me back to Kansas.  
**The Scarecrow**: Does he have brains to spare, do you suppose? Straw legs I do not mind.  
**The Scarecrow**: But a straw head is another matter. People call you a fool.  
**Dorothy**: I do not know. But come anyway.  
**Dorothy**: If he has none, you will have lost nothing but the walk.  
_He takes six steps, and steps into a hole in the road and goes full length on the bricks. He is not hurt in the least._  
_By noon he has done this four times, six steps each time._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiplication word problem  
> _Say the Scarecrow manages 6 steps before each tumble, and he tumbles 4 times before noon. How many steps has he taken?_  
> - ✅ 24 → Yes. Four groups of six: 4 × 6 = 24. He is learning fast, and falling faster.
> - 10 → That adds 6 and 4. Four tumbles with six steps in each is 4 × 6 = 24.
> - 18 → That is three groups of six. He has tumbled four times, so 4 × 6 = 24.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a multiplication word problem: 4 groups of 6 steps is 24._


**Choices**

- **Offer him your arm.**
    - **The Scarecrow**: An arm! The very thing.
    - **The Scarecrow**: I shall try very hard not to take it home with me.
- **Walk ahead and call out the holes.**
    - “Hole,” you call, and he steps round it; “hole,” and he steps round; “brick,” and he falls over the brick.
- **Let your Nubbin lead him.**
    - Your Nubbin walks in front, very slowly. The Scarecrow copies every step, and falls over only twice.

#### `reader:a15-03-feet-done` · [main](main.md#a15-03-feet-done)

_By noon he can walk a straight line. By afternoon he can very nearly walk a curved one._  
_His legs, which once did not consult each other, now seem at least to be on speaking terms._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): personification  
> _The narrator says the Scarecrow’s legs “do not consult each other”. What is the author doing?_  
> - ✅ Giving legs a human habit, to be funny → Yes. This is personification: treating a thing as if it were a person. Here it makes his clumsiness funny.
