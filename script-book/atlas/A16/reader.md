# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A16: The Fox and the Rose

## A16-1. Drawing Number One

- `a16-01-s1`: Step into the desert page and find the stranger under the plane. · _Touch the little planet on the page, then walk towards the plane in the dunes._
- `a16-01-s2`: Look at the pilot’s first drawing, and say what you see. · _Sit by the pilot when he takes out his notebook at noon._

#### `reader:a16-01-desert` · [main](main.md#a16-01-desert)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page that is almost empty: one small planet, one chair, and a sunset behind it._  
_You touch the sunset. It turns warm like sand, and then it is sand, and you are standing in it._  
_Dunes in every direction, under a sky so clean it looks scrubbed. Your Nubbin sneezes at the size of it._  
_A few dunes away, a small aeroplane sits nose-down in the sand. A pair of legs sticks out from under the engine._  
**The Pilot**: Unless you’re a mechanic, keep back. If you are one, come quickly.  
_He slides out: a man with oil to his elbows and a face that hasn’t slept._  
**The Pilot**: I’m days from anywhere. You’re the last thing I expected to see.  
**The Pilot**: Where did you come from? No, don’t tell me. I’ve stopped expecting sensible answers out here.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): what a desert is, and that the Sahara is the biggest hot desert  
> **The Pilot**: Welcome to the Sahara, by the way. It’s a desert: a place that gets very little rain.  
> **The Pilot**: Less than 25 centimetres (10 inches) in a whole year. Plenty of sand, not much to drink.  
> **The Pilot**: It’s the biggest hot desert on Earth. You’ll want a hat.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a desert gets very little rain, and that the Sahara is the biggest hot desert._


**Choices**

- **Offer to hold the spanner.**
    - **The Pilot**: Hold it, and don’t hand it over unless I ask. I have a system. It’s mostly muttering.
- **Ask if he’s hurt.**
    - **The Pilot**: Only my pride. And the engine. The engine is worse.
- **Sit down in the shade of the wing.**
    - You sit, and your Nubbin sits. After a moment the man sits too, because it seems rude not to.

#### `reader:a16-01-desert-done` · [main](main.md#a16-01-desert-done)

_He slides back under the engine. Every so often a hand comes out for a tool, then disappears again._  
**The Pilot**: Sixteen litres of water, that’s all I have. I drink two a day, and I’m careful about it.  
**The Pilot**: So that’s eight days for me. But water goes faster when somebody shares it.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): dividing a total by a daily amount  
> **The Pilot**: Say one more person shares it and drinks two litres a day too. How long do sixteen litres last then?  
> - ✅ Four days. → Yes. Two people use 2 + 2 = 4 litres a day, and 16 ÷ 4 = 4. Four days. Sharing makes the water go twice as fast.
> - Six days. → Let’s count it. Two people use 4 litres a day: 4, 8, 12, 16. That’s four days, not six.
> - Eight days. → Eight days is for one person. Two people use twice as much, so the water lasts half as long. Half of eight is four days.
> _For grown-ups: Divided 16 litres by 4 litres a day to find how many days the water lasts._

**The Pilot**: When I get this running, I’ll show you something. Nobody has ever got it right. Not once.  

#### `reader:a16-01-drawing` · [main](main.md#a16-01-drawing)

_At noon the sun is too hot to work in. The pilot crawls out, wipes his hands, and takes a battered notebook from his jacket._  
**The Pilot**: When I was six, I drew this. I showed it to every grown-up I knew.  
_It is a brown shape like a hill, or a lump, or a hat with a very low crown._  
**The Pilot**: I asked if it frightened them. They all said, “Why would anyone be frightened of a hat?”  
**The Pilot**: I still show it to everyone I meet. It’s a test. Go on. What is it?  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): two people can see the same thing differently  
> _The grown-ups saw a hat, but the boy who drew it saw something else._  
> _Why might they see different things?_  
> - Each person looks with their own ideas. → That’s a fair answer. What we already know and expect changes what we see. Looking slowly, like your Nubbin, can show you more.
> - The grown-ups guessed quickly, and the boy knew what he had drawn. → That’s a fair answer. He knew the answer, and they didn’t stop to look closely.
> - It was not a very clear drawing. → That’s a fair answer. A drawing can be hard to read. But someone who looks carefully, like your Nubbin, can find much more in it.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why two people can look at the same picture and see different things._

_Your Nubbin climbs onto your knee to see. It studies the drawing for a long time, with its head on one side._  

**Choices**

- **Say it’s a hat.**
    - **The Pilot**: Of course it is. Everybody says so.
    - But your Nubbin is still looking.
- **Wait for your Nubbin.**
    - You wait, and the pilot waits. Your Nubbin is in no hurry at all.
- **Say it looks like something with something inside it.**
    - **The Pilot**: Go on…

#### `reader:a16-01-drawing-done` · [main](main.md#a16-01-drawing-done)

_Your Nubbin puts a paw on the lump and traces it: a long body, a tail, and in the middle a great round bulge._  
_Then it lies flat on its tummy and tries to look as full as possible._  
**The Pilot**: A snake. A snake that has swallowed an elephant whole.  
**The Pilot**: Twenty-odd years, and the first one to see it is… that.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): snakes swallow food whole; a real snake could not swallow an elephant  
> **The Pilot**: No real snake could swallow an elephant, mind you. It would never fit.  
> **The Pilot**: But real snakes do swallow their food whole. Their jaws stretch wide, because they can’t chew.  
> **The Pilot**: And one big meal can keep a snake going for weeks.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that snakes swallow their food whole and can go for weeks after a big meal._


#### `reader:a16-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a16-01-complete)

_He turns the page. The same snake is drawn again, opened up like a map, with a very calm elephant inside._  
**The Pilot**: I had to draw the inside for them. Grown-ups always need things explained.  

> **Lesson** (art, L.3.4, notice): a cross-section shows the inside of something  
> **The Pilot**: A drawing that shows the inside of a thing is called a cross-section. It’s as if you’d cut it open.  
> **The Pilot**: Engine makers use them all the time. So do doctors, and so do people who dig wells.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a cross-section is a drawing that shows the inside of something._

**The Pilot**: Then they told me to stop drawing and learn something useful. So I learnt to fly, which is how I got here.  
_He looks at the nose-down plane and laughs, which you didn’t expect._  
**The Pilot**: Stay, if you like. It’s a big desert. There’s room for two strangers. Three, with that one.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): welcoming someone new  
> _The pilot is tired and far from home, but he invites strangers to stay. How can you make someone new feel welcome?_  
> - ✅ Say hello, include them, and ask what they would like to do. → Yes. A friendly hello and a real invitation make a stranger feel they belong.
> - Leave them alone until they speak first. → Some people are shy, so it can feel polite to wait. A small hello from you can make it much easier for them.
> - Tell them all the rules straight away. → Rules can help later. But first, people want to feel they are wanted. A smile and an invitation come first.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how to make a newcomer feel welcome._

## A16-2. Draw Me a Sheep

- `a16-02-s1`: Spend the night by the plane, and see who comes at sunrise. · _Rest in the shade of the wing until the page’s dawn._
- `a16-02-s2`: Watch the pilot try to draw a sheep that is good enough. · _Stay by the boy while the pilot draws._
- `a16-02-s3`: Decide whether to ask for a muzzle for the sheep. · _Talk to the pilot while the boy is busy with his box._

#### `reader:a16-02-dawn` · [main](main.md#a16-02-dawn)

_That night you sleep on the sand by the wing. There are so many stars that the dark between them looks crowded._  
_At sunrise a small voice wakes the pilot. It is quite a polite voice, and quite certain._  
**The Prince**: Please. Would you draw a sheep for me?  
_A boy stands in the sand with a long scarf, a serious face, and not a speck of dust on him._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, notice): desert nights are cold because the dry, clear air lets heat escape  
> **The Pilot**: Brr. Chilly, this early. You’d never think the sand was baking yesterday.  
> **The Pilot**: In the Sahara it can be over 40 °C (104 °F) by day and near 10 °C (50 °F) by night.  
> **The Pilot**: Dry, clear air has no clouds to hold the heat in. When the Sun goes, the warmth just leaves.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned why deserts can be very hot by day and cold at night._

**The Pilot**: A what?  
**The Prince**: A sheep. Please, if you’re not too busy.  
**The Pilot**: I can’t draw. I only ever learnt the one. Here.  
_He draws the snake with the elephant inside, from the outside, and hands it over with a shrug._  
**The Prince**: No, no. Snakes are dangerous, and elephants take up far too much room. A sheep.  
_The pilot’s mouth falls open. Your Nubbin looks delighted, as if it has found a cousin._  

**Choices**

- **Sit down to watch.**
    - You sit cross-legged in the sand. The boy sits too, opposite, and waits for his sheep as if it were a bus.
- **Say hello to the boy.**
    - **The Prince**: Hello. Can you draw sheep?
    - You say sorry, you can’t draw sheep.
    - **The Prince**: That’s all right. Nobody can, it seems.

#### `reader:a16-02-drawings` · [main](main.md#a16-02-drawings)

_The pilot draws a sheep. The boy studies it with great care._  
**The Prince**: No. That one is poorly already. Another, please.  
_The second sheep is fluffier. The boy smiles kindly, the way you smile at a mistake._  
**The Prince**: That one has horns. That’s a ram. I asked for a sheep.  
_The third is small and neat, with a tidy face._  
**The Prince**: He looks tired already. Mine has to last a long time.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): being honest and gentle together  
> _The boy told the pilot the truth about every drawing, and he never was rude. How do you tell someone you don’t like their work, kindly?_  
> - ✅ Say what you need, in a friendly way, like “I need one that will last”. → Yes. Being honest and gentle at once is a skill. The pilot gets a clear clue, and nobody feels small.
> - Say you love it, even if you don’t. → That’s kind, but then he wouldn’t get the sheep the boy wanted. Telling the truth gently helps both people.
> - Say it’s awful. → That’s honest, but it hurts, and it doesn’t help him draw a better one. We can be honest without being harsh: say what you need.
> _For grown-ups: Practised giving honest feedback in a kind way._

_The pilot glances at his engine, which is waiting, and at the sun, which is climbing. He scribbles four quick lines._  
**The Pilot**: This is a box. The sheep you want is inside.  
_It is a plain box with three holes in the side. The boy’s whole face lights up._  
**The Prince**: That’s him! That’s exactly him! Will he need a lot of grass?  
**The Pilot**: Why?  
**The Prince**: Where I live, there isn’t much room for grass. Or for anything.  
_He bends close to the holes and whispers, so as not to wake anybody._  
**The Prince**: Shh. He’s already asleep.  

#### `reader:a16-02-drawings-done` · [main](main.md#a16-02-drawings-done)

_Your Nubbin climbs onto the lid of the box and sits on it gently, as if it might be somebody’s bed._  
_Neither of you can see the sheep. The boy can, and that seems to be enough for everyone._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, notice): inferring: using clues to work out what the story doesn’t say  
> _The boy sees a sheep because a box with holes and a few words give him clues, and his mind fills in the rest._  
> _Readers do this too, using clues to work out what a story doesn’t say. That is called inferring._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that inferring means using clues to work out what is not said._


#### `reader:a16-02-muzzle` · [main](main.md#a16-02-muzzle)

_The boy lies on his front with one eye to the holes. The pilot has gone back under his engine._  
**The Prince**: Sheep eat bushes, don’t they?  
_From under the plane comes a muffled “Mostly.”_  
**The Prince**: Good. That’s very useful.  
**The Prince**: And flowers? Do they eat flowers?  
_The voice under the plane says sheep eat whatever they find, even flowers with thorns._  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): sheep are herbivores; what a muzzle is for  
> **The Pilot**: Sheep are herbivores, which means they eat only plants. Mostly grass, but also leaves, shrubs and flowers.  
> **The Pilot**: A muzzle goes round an animal’s nose and mouth so it can’t nibble things it shouldn’t.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that sheep are herbivores and what a muzzle is for._

_The boy goes quiet, the way people do when they have thought of something and put it away for later._  
_You look at the box, with your Nubbin on the lid. A sheep that eats everything might need a muzzle, one day._  

**Choices**

- **Ask the pilot to draw a muzzle for the sheep now, before it’s too late.**
    - **The Pilot**: A muzzle. Fair enough. Pass me the pencil.
    - He draws one on a scrap of paper: a little muzzle, a leather strap to fasten it, and a proper buckle.
    - **The Prince**: Not now. He’s asleep. He’d hate it.
    - **The Pilot**: Then I’ll keep it till you need it. Strap and all.
    - He folds the scrap into the very back of his notebook.
- **Leave it. The sheep’s asleep.**
    - You leave it. The boy has his sheep, the sheep has its box, and nobody is thinking about muzzles yet.

#### `reader:a16-02-muzzle-done` · [main](main.md#a16-02-muzzle-done)

**The Prince**: At night I can put him in his box. Then nobody ever has to tie him up.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): what animals need to be safe and happy  
> _The boy would rather not tie his sheep up at all. What helps an animal live safely and happily?_  
> - A safe place to rest, food and water, and room to move. → That’s a fair answer. Animals need safety and freedom, and people who think about what they want.
> - Keeping it tied up, so it can’t run off. → That’s a fair answer. Tying keeps an animal close, and the boy has thought about that too. But a tied-up animal can’t move or choose, so he wants another way.
> - Food, and someone who loves it. → That’s a fair answer. Food and love matter a great deal. Water, shelter and room to move matter too.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what animals need to be safe and happy, including freedom._

**The Pilot**: Where’s that? Where do you live?  
_The boy doesn’t answer. He asks what the plane is instead, and is delighted to hear that it fell out of the sky._  
**The Prince**: You fell out of the sky? So did I, in a way. Which star is yours?  

#### `reader:a16-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a16-02-complete)

_The pilot laughs and wipes his forehead with his wrist, leaving a long black streak._  
**The Pilot**: Here. One for you as well. Anyone and anything ought to have a sheep.  
_He draws another box with three holes in it, and gives it to you. Your Nubbin climbs in and peers out of a hole._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 2 groups of 3  
> _Your box has three holes, and so does the boy’s. How many holes are there in the two boxes together?_  
> - ✅ Six. → Yes: 3 + 3 = 6. Or two groups of three: 2 × 3 = 6.
> - Five. → Close! Count again: 1, 2, 3 on one box, then 4, 5, 6 on the other. That makes six holes.
> - Three. → Three is the holes in one box. There are two boxes, so three twice: 3 + 3 = 6.
> _For grown-ups: Used equal groups to work out 2 × 3._

**The Prince**: Yours is asleep too. You can tell from how quiet it is.  

### Glossary

- **muzzle**: A strap or cover that goes over an animal’s nose and mouth, so it can’t nibble or eat things.

## A16-3. Asteroid B-612

- `a16-03-s1`: Listen to the boy tell where he comes from. · _Sit by the plane after dark, when the boy starts talking._
- `a16-03-s2`: Help with one morning’s weeding on the little planet. · _Pull the baobab shoots while they’re tiny. Leave anything that might be a rose._

#### `reader:a16-03-telling` · [main](main.md#a16-03-telling)

_It takes days to get anything out of the boy. He asks questions all the time, and hardly hears yours._  
_But at night, by the cold engine, under the stars, he begins to tell._  
**The Prince**: My planet is hardly bigger than a house. You can walk all round it before your tea gets cold.  
_The pilot murmurs that it must be an asteroid, one of the little ones with only a number for a name._  
**The Pilot**: Call it B-612, then, for now. Every little asteroid needs a number.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): asteroids are small rocky worlds; real ones have numbers and names  
> **The Pilot**: Real asteroids are small, rocky worlds left over from when the planets were made.  
> **The Pilot**: Most circle the Sun between Mars and Jupiter. Each gets a number, and many get a name too, like 4 Vesta.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that asteroids are small rocky worlds and that real ones are numbered._

**The Prince**: Grown-ups love numbers. Tell them about a friend, and they ask how much he weighs.  
**The Prince**: It’s forty-one steps round. My steps are small, about half a metre each.  
_He tells it so well that the sand under you curves. The sky comes down close, and you are standing on a planet the size of a house._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): multiplying by a half-metre step and converting units  
> **The Prince**: Forty-one steps, and every step is 50 centimetres. About how far is it all the way round?  
> - ✅ About 20 metres and 50 centimetres. → Yes. 41 × 50 cm is 2,050 cm, and 100 cm make a metre. So it’s 20 metres and 50 centimetres. A tiny planet, but a long walk for a small boy.
> - 41 metres. → That would be if each step were a whole metre. Each step is half a metre, so the answer is about half of 41: 20 and a half metres.
> - 9 metres. → Let’s check. 40 steps of 50 cm is 2,000 cm, which is 20 metres. One more step makes 20 metres and 50 centimetres.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied 41 × 50 cm and changed centimetres into metres._


**Choices**

- **Ask what colour his sunsets are.**
    - **The Prince**: Every colour, one after another. You’ll see those too.
- **Ask how many steps it is round.**
    - **The Prince**: That’s a grown-up question.
    - **The Prince**: Forty-one. Not counting going round the rose.
- **Watch your Nubbin.**
    - Your Nubbin has already set off round the planet. It comes back from the other side almost at once, astonished.

#### `reader:a16-03-weeding` · [main](main.md#a16-03-weeding)

_It is morning on the little planet. There are three volcanoes, each about knee-high, and two of them are warm._  
**The Prince**: I sweep them out every day. Then they burn slow and steady, and I can heat my breakfast on them.  
**The Prince**: That one’s asleep. I sweep it anyway. You never know with volcanoes.  
_Then he kneels and shows you the soil. Tiny green shoots are coming up everywhere, and they all look much the same._  
**The Prince**: These are baobabs. They grow as big as hills, and if you let them, their roots go right through the planet.  
**The Prince**: These others might be roses. You have to tell them apart while they’re small. And then you mustn’t wait.  
_Your Nubbin grips a shoot in both paws and looks up, waiting to be told._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): dealing with small problems early  
> **The Prince**: A baobab shoot is easy to pull when it’s small, and impossible when it’s a tree. So when should I deal with a small problem?  
> - Early, while it’s small. → That’s a fair answer. Small problems are much easier to fix early, like baobab shoots.
> - Later, when I have more time. → That’s a fair answer. Later feels easier. The boy would say that small problems grow, like baobabs, and get harder to pull.
> - It depends how big the problem is. → That’s a fair answer. Some problems do fade away, and others grow. The hard part is telling which is which. Baobabs look harmless at first.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about dealing with small problems before they grow._


**Choices**

- **Pull it. It has a baobab’s fat stem.**
    - Out it comes, root and all. The boy nods once, like a teacher who has seen a sum done right.
- **Ask him which it is first.**
    - **The Prince**: Baobab. Good. Asking is better than pulling up a rose.
    - Out it comes, root and all.
- **Leave it a day, to be sure.**
    - **The Prince**: No. By tomorrow it’ll be too big to tell you anything but trouble.
    - He pulls it himself, gently and at once.

#### `reader:a16-03-weeding-done` · [main](main.md#a16-03-weeding-done)

_By the time the sun is properly up, the planet is clean. You have a heap of baobab shoots, and not one rose in it._  
**The Prince**: I knew a planet where a lazy man left three of them. You can’t see the ground there any more.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): real baobabs live in dry lands and store water in their trunks  
> _Real baobab trees are not nearly so dreadful._  
> _They grow in Africa, Madagascar and Australia, and store water in thick trunks to get through dry months._  
> _Some live for more than a thousand years._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that real baobab trees store water in their trunks and can live for over a thousand years._


#### `reader:a16-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a16-03-complete)

**The Prince**: Every morning, first thing. If you leave it for later, later turns into trees.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, notice): a metaphor says one thing and means another  
> _The boy says “later turns into trees”. Later can’t really grow leaves, so this is a metaphor._  
> _A metaphor talks about one thing to explain another. Here it means a problem you put off keeps on growing._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a metaphor describes one thing by talking about another._

_The telling fades. The sand comes back under you, and the pilot is lying by the plane with his hands behind his head._  
**The Pilot**: Baobabs. All right. I’ll draw you one, so nobody forgets.  
_He draws a little planet with three enormous trees squeezing it. Your Nubbin gives the drawing a very hard look._  

### Glossary

- **asteroid**: A small rocky world that travels round the Sun.
- **baobab**: A real tree with a very thick trunk, which grows in dry lands.
- **metaphor**: Describing one thing by saying it is another, to help explain it.

## A16-4. The Sunsets

> **Lesson** (health, CASEL: Self-management, notice): people need water every day; they can last weeks without food but only days without water  
> _A person can live for weeks without food, but only a few days without water._  
> _More than half of your body is water, and it needs topping up every day. That is why finding a well matters so much._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the body needs water every day, and can manage only a few days without it._

## A16-15. The Well

- `a16-15-s1`: Walk through the night with the pilot, looking for a well. · _Keep up with the pilot across the dunes. Don’t stop._
- `a16-15-s2`: Find the well at daybreak, and draw up the water. · _Help the pilot haul the bucket. Steady it on the wall._
- `a16-15-s3`: Share the water. · _Hold the bucket so that everyone can drink._

#### `reader:a16-15-night` · [main](main.md#a16-15-night)

_A well, in all this desert, is a silly idea. You set off anyway, all of you together, as the sun goes down._  
_The stars come out. The sand is cold under your feet, and silver, and goes on for ever._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, ask): finding north with the Pole Star  
> **The Pilot**: Keep the Pole Star in front of us. It stays above the north all night, while the other stars wheel round it.  
> **The Pilot**: If it’s straight ahead, which way are we walking?  
> - ✅ North. → Yes. The Pole Star shows where north is, so walking towards it means walking north.
> - South. → South would be behind us. The Pole Star marks north, so if it is straight ahead, we are walking north.
> - West. → West would be on our left. The Pole Star marks north, so if it is straight ahead, we are walking north.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Pole Star shows north and used it to work out a walking direction._

**The Pilot**: Here. Your little one’s shivering.  
_He unwinds the long scarf from his own neck and winds it round and round your Nubbin, until only its eyes show._  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): wool traps air, which keeps body heat in  
> _A knitted wool scarf keeps you warm because it traps tiny pockets of air, and trapped air lets heat escape slowly._  
> _That’s why a long scarf makes a good coat for a cold desert night._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that wool keeps us warm by trapping air._

**The Prince**: There’s water somewhere. You can tell. The sand goes quiet over it.  
_After a long time the boy stumbles, and sits, and falls asleep where he sits._  
_The pilot picks him up very carefully, as if he might break, and carries him on through the night._  
**The Pilot**: He weighs nothing. I keep thinking I’ll drop him, and he weighs nothing at all.  

**Choices**

- **Carry the sheep box.**
    - You carry the box with holes. Your Nubbin rides on it in its scarf, keeping an eye on the sheep.
- **Carry the empty water tin.**
    - It is very light, and it rattles. You carry it anyway, so you’ll have something to fill.
- **Walk on the windy side of the pilot.**
    - You walk on the wind’s side. The pilot doesn’t say anything, but he walks a little closer.

#### `reader:a16-15-night-done` · [main](main.md#a16-15-night-done)

_Dawn comes up grey, then gold. The boy wakes in the pilot’s arms, and points._  
_The cold slides out of the sand. The first sunlight is pale, then yellow, then warm on your face._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.3, notice): colour words and the feelings they carry  
> _The sky goes grey, then gold. Writers use colour words to show a feeling as well as a colour._  
> _Grey feels quiet and tired, while gold feels warm and hopeful. Can you find other colour words in this story?_  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that colour words can show feelings as well as colours._


#### `reader:a16-15-well` · [main](main.md#a16-15-well)

_A well. Not a hole in the sand, like desert wells, but a real one: a stone ring, a rope, a pulley and a bucket._  
**The Pilot**: This doesn’t belong here. It’s a village well. Where’s the village?  
**The Prince**: Shh. Listen.  
_He takes hold of the rope and pulls. The pulley turns and creaks, like an old gate that hasn’t been asked to open for years._  
**The Prince**: It’s complaining. Nobody’s woken it up for ages.  
**The Pilot**: Let me. It’s too heavy for you.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, notice): a pulley changes the direction of a pull, so you can use your weight  
> **The Pilot**: A pulley is a wheel with a rope over it. Pull down on one end, and the bucket goes up.  
> **The Pilot**: It doesn’t make the bucket lighter. It changes the direction of the pull, so I can lean my weight on the rope.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a pulley changes the direction of a pull to make lifting easier._

_The pilot hauls the bucket up, hand over hand, muttering._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): a French word: puits means well  
> _In French, a well is un puits, said “pwee”._  
> _Wells like this one reach down to water hidden far below the sand._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the French word puits (well)._

**The Pilot**: Don’t ask. It’s the system.  
_You steady it on the stone, with the sun shaking in the water. Your Nubbin mutters too, to help._

_The bucket sits on the wall, full to the brim. The water in it is gold with the sunrise._

#### `reader:a16-15-water` · [main](main.md#a16-15-water)

**The Prince**: Can I have some? That’s the water I wanted. That one.  
_The pilot lifts the bucket to the boy’s lips. He drinks with his eyes shut, as if it were his birthday._  
**The Prince**: It isn’t just the water. It’s the walk.  
**The Pilot**: Your turn.  
_The bucket is heavy and cold on the outside, and the sun is in it._  
_Your Nubbin stands on tiptoe on the stone, looking in._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): fairness when sharing something everyone needs  
> _There is water for everyone, and everyone is thirsty. Who should drink first, and why?_  
> - The one who needs it most. → That’s a fair answer. Needing something is a good reason to go first.
> - Everyone takes a turn, in order. → That’s a fair answer too. Taking turns treats everybody the same.
> - The one who carried the bucket. → That’s a fair answer. Some people would say the others are just as tired, though.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about different ways to share fairly when everyone has a need._


**Choices**

- **Let your Nubbin drink first.**
    - You tip the bucket for your Nubbin. It puts its whole face in, scarf and all, and comes up dripping and happy.
- **Drink.**
    - You drink. Your Nubbin drinks after you, with its nose right in the bucket.
- **Hold the bucket for the pilot.**
    - You hold it while he drinks, both hands round it. He nods at you over the rim, and doesn’t stop for a long time.

#### `reader:a16-15-water-done` · [main](main.md#a16-15-water-done)

_It tastes of the walk: the cold sand and the stars, the creak of the pulley, and being carried._  
**The Pilot**: That’s the best thing I’ve ever drunk.  

> **Lesson** (health, CASEL: Self-awareness, notice): why water tastes so good when you are thirsty  
> _Water tastes best when you’re very thirsty. Your body keeps track of how much water it has._  
> _When it runs low, your brain makes you feel thirsty. Drinking switches that feeling off, and it feels wonderful._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that thirst is the body’s signal that it needs water._

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): a year in weeks and days  
> _A year is 365 days, and a week is 7 days. How many whole weeks are in a year, and how many days are left over?_  
> - ✅ 52 weeks and 1 day. → Yes. 52 × 7 = 364, and 365 − 364 = 1 day left over.
> - 50 weeks. → 50 weeks is only 350 days, so 15 days are left. That’s two more weeks and a day. So it’s 52 weeks and 1 day.
> - 53 weeks. → 53 × 7 = 371, which is more than 365. 52 × 7 = 364, so it’s 52 weeks and 1 day.
> _For grown-ups: Divided 365 days by 7 to find the number of whole weeks and days left over._
