# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A17: The Camphor Tree

## A17-1. The Old House

- `a17-01-s1`: Step into the summer page and find Granny at the bus stop. · _Touch the page, then walk down the country road to the bus stop._
- `a17-01-s2`: Help the Kusakabes unload the lorry at the old house. · _Carry the boxes from the lorry to the step. Take your boots off before you go in._
- `a17-01-s3`: Look at the great tree on the hill with the family. · _Stand in the back garden and look up the hill behind the house._

#### `reader:a17-01-road` · [main](main.md#a17-01-road)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a country road in summer. There is a bus stop, green rice fields, and a tree on a hill._  
_The tree is drawn so big that it runs off the top of the page._  
_The page hums under your hand. It is cicadas, thousands of them, and you are standing in the road._  
_An old woman waits at the bus stop in a straw hat. She looks you and your Nubbin up and down._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, notice): Japan is a chain of islands; rice grows in flooded fields called paddies  
> **Granny**: Look at the rice. We grow it with its feet in water, in flooded fields called paddies.  
> **Granny**: Japan is a long chain of islands, and in summer the paddies are bright green.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Japan is a chain of islands and that rice grows in flooded fields called paddies._

**Granny**: You’ll be stopping with me for the summer. Good.  
**Granny**: The girls will call me obaa-chan, and you may as well.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): words from other languages: obaa-chan means granny  
> _Granny says her name for the summer is obaa-chan. It means “granny”, and people use it for any older woman they are fond of._  
> - ✅ It is a loving name for an older woman, whether or not she is your own granny. → Yes. In Japan the word is a mark of affection as well as family.
> - It is a kind of rice. → A good guess, with all those rice fields! But no. Obaa-chan means granny, and it is used for any older woman you love.
> - It is the name of the bus. → Not quite. The bus is only a bus. Obaa-chan means granny, a loving name for an older woman.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that obaa-chan is a Japanese word for granny, used for any older woman you are fond of._

_A boy in a vest sits on the fence, pretending not to look at you. He is looking._  
**Granny**: That’s Kanta, my grandson. Say hello, Kanta.  
**Kanta**: …lo.  
_Down the road comes a small lorry piled with furniture. Two girls ride in the back, waving at everything._  

**Choices**

- **Wave back.**
    - You wave. The smaller girl waves with both arms, so hard she nearly falls out, and the bigger one grabs her dress.
- **Ask Granny who they are.**
    - **Granny**: The new family for the old house. A professor and his two girls.
    - **Granny**: Their mother’s in hospital over the hill.
- **Ask Kanta who they are.**
    - **Kanta**: Dunno.
    - He knows. He watches the lorry to the turning, and goes red when you notice.

#### `reader:a17-01-road-done` · [main](main.md#a17-01-road-done)

**Granny**: Go on, then. They’ll want hands for all that.  
**Granny**: I’ll have supper on.  
_The lorry is a small, rattling thing with three wheels, one at the front and two at the back._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 2 groups of 3 make 6  
> _This little lorry has 3 wheels. If two lorries like it came down the road together, how many wheels would that be?_  
> - ✅ 6 wheels: two groups of 3. → Yes. 3 + 3 = 6, which is 2 × 3.
> - 5 wheels. → Let’s check: one lorry has 3 wheels, and a second lorry has 3 more. 3 + 3 = 6. Two groups of 3 make 6.
> - 9 wheels. → 9 would be three lorries. Count in threes: 3, then 6. Two lorries have 6 wheels.
> _For grown-ups: Used equal groups to find that two lorries with 3 wheels each have 6 wheels._

_Your Nubbin is already trotting after it, in the dust._  
_A cloud of cicadas lifts from the hedge as it passes, and settles again behind it._  

#### `reader:a17-01-unload` · [main](main.md#a17-01-unload)

_The old house sits back from the road behind a hedge: a wooden house, a little tall, with a porch at one side._  
_A man in round glasses lifts a chest off the lorry. He hands you the other end as if he’d been expecting you._  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Oh, hello! Are you from next door?  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Wonderful. Mind the step, it bites.  
_At the step everybody slips their shoes off and lines them up. Your Nubbin has no shoes, but it waits anyway._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, notice): taking shoes off at the step keeps floors clean and shows respect  
> _In Japan, shoes come off in the entrance hall, called the genkan, and stay there._  
> _Many floors are for sitting and sleeping on, so keeping them clean is a way of being considerate._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that taking shoes off at the genkan is an everyday custom that keeps floors clean and shows consideration._

**Satsuki**: Mei! The porch post is rotten!  
**Satsuki**: Look, it wobbles!  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-ESS1-1, ask): why the porch post is rotten: fungi break down damp wood slowly  
> _The porch post is rotten and wobbly. What most likely made it so?_  
> - ✅ Years of damp, which let fungi slowly break the wood down. → Yes. Rot is slow. A damp post that nobody has mended for years is perfect for fungi.
> - The girls pushing it for a minute. → A push can make a rotten post wobble, but it was already weak. The weakness came slowly, from damp and fungi.
> - It was built wobbly. → Possibly, but a sound post of good wood does not go wobbly on its own. Slow rot from damp and fungi is the usual cause.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that wood rots slowly when it stays damp, because fungi break it down._

**Mei**: It’s rotten! It’s going to fall down!  
_They push the post together, shrieking, and run off. Something drops off the porch roof and bounces at your feet: an acorn._  
**Mei**: Acorns! Onee-chan, there are acorns!  
_There are no oaks anywhere near the house. Your Nubbin looks up at the roof, and keeps looking._  

**Choices**

- **Put the acorn in your pocket.**
    - It sits in your pocket all afternoon, small and smooth. Every so often you check it is still there.
- **Give it to Mei.**
    - **Mei**: Thank you! Now I’ve got two.
    - She puts both in her pocket and looks up at the roof, the same way your Nubbin does.
- **Leave it where it fell.**
    - You leave it on the step. When you come back with the next box, it has gone.

#### `reader:a17-01-unload-done` · [main](main.md#a17-01-unload-done)

**Mr Kusakabe**: That’s the last of it. Thank you.  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Now, Satsuki, Mei, which of you wants to find the well?  
_Both of them do, at once, and so does your Nubbin._  
_Three small people each take a step towards the back garden, and stop, and look at each other._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): a think question: what is a fair way to decide who goes first?  
> _Three of them each want to be first to the well. What would be a fair way to decide?_  
> _Think about it, and choose the one that feels right._  
> - Take turns, and draw lots for the order. → That’s a fair answer. Drawing lots means luck decides, so nobody can say it was unkind. Satsuki would nod at that.
> - The youngest goes first. → That’s a fair answer, too. It says that smaller people deserve a chance first. Mei would be very pleased with it.
> - Everyone goes together. → That’s a fair answer. Nobody is first, and nobody is left behind. It would be a noisy trip to the well, but a happy one.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about fair ways to decide who goes first and gave reasons._

_Nobody wants to be second to the well._  

#### `reader:a17-01-tree` · [main](main.md#a17-01-tree)

_Behind the house the ground rises into woods, and out of the woods rises the tree._  
_It is the biggest thing you have ever seen. Not the biggest tree: the biggest thing._  
**Satsuki**: Dad, what kind of tree is that?  
**Mr Kusakabe**: A camphor. Kusunoki.  
**Mr Kusakabe**: It must be very old. Older than the house, older than the village, I should think.  
**Mei**: It’s the biggest in the world.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): camphor trees are evergreen, and their leaves hold a strong-smelling oil  
> **Mr Kusakabe**: A camphor tree keeps its leaves all year, so it is called an evergreen.  
> **Mr Kusakabe**: Crush a leaf and you’ll smell it. The oil in the leaves is sharp and clean, and bugs don’t care for it.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that camphor trees are evergreen and that the strong-smelling oil in their leaves helps keep bugs away._

**Mr Kusakabe**: Very possibly, Mei.  
_The wind comes down the hill, and the whole tree moves at once, slowly, like something breathing._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): recognising a simile: like something breathing  
> _“The whole tree moves at once, slowly, like something breathing.” What is “like something breathing” doing here?_  
> - ✅ It compares the tree to a living thing, to show how slow and alive it feels. → Yes. That is a simile: it uses “like” to compare two things.
> - It says the tree really is breathing. → It is only an image. The tree does not really breathe like us. A simile uses “like” or “as” to compare. This one makes the tree feel alive.
> - It is a fact about camphor trees. → It is not a fact, but a picture the writer paints. A simile compares two things with “like” or “as”.
> _For grown-ups: Identified a simile and what it does: it compares a tree to something breathing._

_Your Nubbin sits down in the grass and looks at it with its mouth a little open._  

#### `reader:a17-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a17-01-complete)

_At dusk you go back through the gap in the hedge to Granny’s, and take your boots off at her step._  
_Supper is rice, pickles and a fish each. Granny sets out three plates with two pickles on each._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 3 plates with 2 pickles each  
> _Granny puts 2 pickles on each of 3 plates. How many pickles has she put out in all?_  
> - ✅ 6 pickles. → Yes. Three groups of 2 is 2 + 2 + 2 = 6, or 3 × 2.
> - 5 pickles. → Let’s count again: 2 on the first plate, 2 on the second, 2 on the third. 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 pickles.
> - 8 pickles. → 8 would need four plates. Here there are three. Three plates with 2 each makes 6.
> _For grown-ups: Used equal groups to find that 3 plates with 2 pickles each hold 6 pickles._

_The cicadas stop all at once when the frogs start._  
**Granny**: The old house has stood empty a long while. It’ll be glad of children.  
_Across the hedge, a window is lit in the old house. Your Nubbin watches the dark one above it, at the top of the stairs._

## A17-2. Soot Sprites

- `a17-02-s1`: Find the stairs up to the dark room at the top of the house. · _The stairs are behind a plain door in the kitchen. Take a lantern up._
- `a17-02-s2`: Ask Granny what the black things are. · _Granny has come over to help clean. Find her on the porch with the rags._

#### `reader:a17-02-stairs` · [main](main.md#a17-02-stairs)

**Satsuki**: There’s a window up top, but we can’t find any stairs. Will you help us look?  
_They aren’t in the hall, or behind any sliding door. Your Nubbin finds them: behind a plain wooden door in the kitchen._  
_The stairs are steep and narrow, and at the top there is only dark._  
**Mei**: You go first, Onee-chan.  
**Satsuki**: No, you.  
_In the end they go up together, side by side, which the stairs are not wide enough for. You follow with the lantern._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): going together makes it easier to be brave  
> _Both girls say “you go first”, and then climb the stairs together. What does that tell us about being brave?_  
> - ✅ It’s easier to be brave with someone beside you. → Yes. Courage grows when we’re not alone.
> - Brave people never feel scared. → Brave people often feel scared, and do it anyway. Going together is what made the stairs easier.
> - You should always go first. → Someone has to go first sometimes, but nobody has to do it alone. Sharing the fright makes it smaller.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how company makes it easier to be brave._

_At the top something rustles. Dozens of small black things scatter from the light, like soot with somewhere to be._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-2, ask): we see things when light bounces off them into our eyes  
> _You only see the small black things when the lantern shines on them. Why does the light help you see them?_  
> - ✅ Light bounces off them and into your eyes. → Yes. We see an object when light from it reaches our eyes. No light, nothing to see.
> - The lantern makes the sprites move. → They do scatter, but that’s fright, not the light’s doing. We see because light bounces off them into our eyes.
> - Your eyes make their own light. → Eyes only catch light. They don’t make any. Light bounces off the sprites and into your eyes, so you see them.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that we see things when light bounces off them and enters our eyes._

_They pour into the cracks in the boards, and are gone._  
**Mei**: I’ll catch one!  

**Choices**

- **Hold the lantern high, so she can see.**
    - You lift the lantern. Mei slaps both hands flat on the boards, very pleased with herself, and holds them up.
- **Sit still on the top stair and wait.**
    - You sit down on the top stair, and the room goes quiet. After a while, one small black thing peeps out of a crack.
    - Mei pounces. She holds her hands up, very pleased with herself.

#### `reader:a17-02-stairs-done` · [main](main.md#a17-02-stairs-done)

_Her hands are empty. Her palms are black, as if she had patted a stove._  
**Mei**: I got one! It got away.  
**Mei**: Look, I’ve got its black on me!  
**Satsuki**: Mei, your feet are black too!  
_The black comes off on the boards, on the sleeve of her dress, and on your Nubbin, which has been leaning on her._  
_A fine black powder, as light as breath, like the dust that gathers in a chimney._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): vocabulary: soot and scatter  
> _Soot is the fine black powder left behind by smoke, and to scatter is to rush off in every direction. Which sentence uses “scatter” correctly?_  
> - ✅ The pigeons scatter when the dog runs at them. → Yes. The pigeons rush off in all directions.
> - She will scatter her book on the shelf. → Not quite. You place a book on a shelf. Scatter means to spread or rush off in every direction, like startled pigeons.
> - Soot will scatter the tea. → Not quite. Soot is black powder, and tea is something you drink. Scatter means to spread or rush off in every direction.
> _For grown-ups: Learned the meanings of soot and scatter and used scatter in a sentence._


#### `reader:a17-02-granny` · [main](main.md#a17-02-granny)

_Granny has come over with rags and a bucket to help the family clean. Mei shows her the black hands and feet._  
**Mei**: Obaa-chan, there are black things in our house! Little round ones!  
**Mei**: They ran away!  
**Granny**: Soot sprites, were they? Well I never.  
**Granny**: I saw them myself, when I was your age.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): oral history: old stories are passed down by word of mouth  
> **Granny**: My own granny told me about them, and her granny told her.  
> **Granny**: Stories get handed down like that, mouth to ear. Nobody writes them down, and they last for years anyway.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that stories can be handed down by word of mouth from one generation to the next._

**Satsuki**: Really? Are they bad?  
**Granny**: Bad? No.  
**Granny**: They live in empty houses, in the dust and the dark, and they like it quiet.  
**Granny**: When people come back, they move on, one night, and nobody sees them go.  
**Granny**: So laugh a lot, and live here properly, and they’ll take the hint. They don’t care for a cheerful house.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): a think question: why does laughing make scary things feel smaller?  
> _Granny says a cheerful house makes the sprites go. Why might laughing and company make a scary thing feel smaller?_  
> _Think about it._  
> - Because when we laugh our bodies relax. → That’s a fair answer. A laugh loosens tight shoulders, and a loose body feels braver. Granny would say you’d noticed something true.
> - Because we aren’t alone with it. → That’s a fair answer. A fear shared with a friend is lighter to carry. Satsuki would agree.
> - Because the scary thing hears us and leaves. → That’s a fair answer, and a lovely one. In stories, noise and cheer do drive shadows away. Real fears often shrink the same way, though, when we aren’t alone with them.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why laughing and being together can make fear feel smaller._

**Mei**: I’ll laugh. I’m very good at it.  
_A face appears over the hedge: Kanta, holding a basket his mother has sent._  
**Kanta**: Your house is haunted!  
_He drops the basket on the step and runs, as fast as he can go._  

**Choices**

- **Call after him: “Thank you for the basket!”**
    - Kanta runs faster. Granny shakes her head at the hedge.
    - **Granny**: He has manners. He keeps them somewhere safe.
- **Laugh. It’s a lovely haunted house.**
    - **Satsuki**: It is, isn’t it? I always wanted to live in a haunted house.
    - **Mei**: Me too! I wanted it first!
- **Say nothing and pick up the basket.**
    - Under the cloth there are rice cakes, still warm. Granny looks at them and sighs.
    - **Granny**: His mother sent those. He’ll have eaten one.

#### `reader:a17-02-granny-done` · [main](main.md#a17-02-granny-done)

**Granny**: Now. Rags.  
**Granny**: That floor won’t clean itself, and the sprites won’t do it either.  
_She wrings a rag out until it is only damp, and hands it to Satsuki._  
**Granny**: Damp, not wet, and not dry. A dry rag only flings the dust back in the air.  

> **Lesson** (health, CASEL: Self-management, notice): dust is made of tiny bits; a damp cloth traps it, and we wash our hands after cleaning  
> **Granny**: Dust is only tiny bits of dirt, fluff and flakes of skin. A damp cloth traps them, and a dry one flicks them about.  
> **Granny**: That’s why we wash our hands when we’ve done, and open the windows while we work.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a damp cloth traps dust, and that washing hands after cleaning keeps us healthy._

_Soon the whole house smells of soap and wet wood._  

#### `reader:a17-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a17-02-complete)

_You all clean until dusk. The girls run the rags down the long boards in a race, bottoms in the air._  
_Your Nubbin rinses the rags in the bucket by the hand pump, mostly by sitting in it._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-4, ask): a hand pump changes the movement of an arm into the lifting of water  
> _At the hand pump, Satsuki pushes the handle up and down and water comes out of the spout. What is the pump doing?_  
> - ✅ It turns the movement of her arm into the lifting of water. → Yes. A pump is a device that changes one kind of energy, the push of an arm, into another, moving water.
> - It makes water out of air. → The water was already underground, in the well. The pump uses her arm’s movement to lift it.
> - It heats the water. → Pumped well water is usually cold. The pump changes the movement of an arm into the lifting of water.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a hand pump changes the movement of an arm into the lifting of water._

_Every so often, in a corner, something small and black watches you all. Nobody minds any more._  
**Granny**: There. A house with its windows open.  
**Granny**: That’s more like it.

## A17-3. The Bath in the Storm

- `a17-03-s1`: Help the family slide the rain shutters shut before the storm. · _Granny sent you over. Pull each wooden shutter along its groove until the house is closed._
- `a17-03-s2`: Wait in the main room while the family has their bath. · _Sit by the low table. When Mr Kusakabe calls out, join in._
- `a17-03-s3`: Watch from Granny’s step when the wind drops. · _Go home through the hedge, then sit on Granny’s step and look at the hill._

#### `reader:a17-03-shutters` · [main](main.md#a17-03-shutters)

_In the evening the wind gets up. The rice fields go silver, then dark, as the gusts run across them._  
**Granny**: Go and help them with the shutters. A professor won’t know which way they slide.  
_At the old house, Mr Kusakabe is pulling at a rain shutter that is stuck halfway along its groove._  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Ah! Reinforcements.  
**Mr Kusakabe**: I think it slides this way. Or possibly the other.  
_You lift it a little and it runs, rattling, all the way along. Then the next one, and the next, until the house is shut._  
_There are nine shutters in all. You slide five, and Mr Kusakabe does the rest._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): subtraction within 20: 9 − 5 = 4  
> _There are 9 shutters, and you slide 5 of them. How many does Mr Kusakabe slide?_  
> - ✅ 4 shutters. → Yes. 9 − 5 = 4.
> - 5 shutters. → Let’s count: 9 shutters, take away the 5 you slid. 9 − 5 = 4 for Mr Kusakabe.
> - 14 shutters. → 14 is 9 + 5, which would be adding. We are taking away. 9 − 5 = 4 shutters for him.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a take-away word problem within 20: 9 shutters, 5 slid by you, 4 by him._

_The wind finds every gap anyway. The boards creak, and the house leans and groans like a ship._  
**Mei**: Dad, the house is going to fall down.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): lifting a stuck shutter reduces friction so the push can move it  
> _The shutter was stuck until you lifted it a little, and then it slid all the way. Why did lifting help?_  
> - ✅ Lifting took weight off the groove, so there was less friction and your push could win. → Yes. Friction pushes against movement. Less friction means the same push can move it.
> - Lifting made the shutter lighter in the air for ever. → It only took some weight off the groove for a moment. That was enough to cut the friction so your push could win.
> - The wind pushed it for you. → The wind was pushing the other way, in fact. Lifting cut the friction in the groove, and your push moved it.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that friction works against movement, and that lifting a stuck shutter cuts it._

**Mr Kusakabe**: It’s only stretching, Mei. Old houses do.  

#### `reader:a17-03-shutters-done` · [main](main.md#a17-03-shutters-done)

**Mr Kusakabe**: Stay till the wind drops. Granny won’t mind.  
**Mr Kusakabe**: The bath’s nearly hot, and we’ve got plenty of tea.  
_Your Nubbin sits by the stove in the kitchen and watches the shutters jump._  
_The old house stretches and settles round you, creaking in a dozen places._  
**Mr Kusakabe**: See? It’s not falling.  
**Mr Kusakabe**: It’s only getting comfortable.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): metaphor: a house that is “stretching”  
> _Mr Kusakabe says the house is “only stretching”. Houses don’t really stretch like people._  
> _What is he doing with the word?_  
> - ✅ Using a metaphor, to describe the creaking as if the house were alive. → Yes. And there’s a true fact underneath: old wooden beams do creak as they shift with changes in damp and temperature.
> - Telling a lie to scare Mei. → No, he is trying to comfort her, not frighten her. He is using a metaphor, talking about the house as if it were a creature stretching.
> - Teaching a lesson about exercise. → Ha! Not quite. He is using a metaphor to make the creaks sound friendly.
> _For grown-ups: Recognised a metaphor (a house “stretching”) and the true idea beneath it: wood shifts as it dries and warms._


#### `reader:a17-03-bath` · [main](main.md#a17-03-bath)

_The family take their bath at the back of the house, in the bathhouse._  
_You wait in the main room with your Nubbin, by the low table._  
_A gust hits the house. Something bangs outside, and the lamp swings on its cord._  
**Mei**: Dad! Dad, I heard something!  
**Satsuki**: So did I. Is it the black things?  
**Satsuki**: Are they coming back?  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Shall I tell you the best thing to do when you’re frightened?  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Laugh. As loud as you can.  
**Mr Kusakabe**: Then anything listening will think we’re far too jolly to bother with.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): a think question: does laughing really chase fear away?  
> _Mr Kusakabe says laughing chases fright away. Does it really work?_  
> _What else helps when you are scared? Think about it._  
> - Yes, laughing makes my body feel calmer. → That’s a fair answer. A big laugh does loosen the body, and a loose body feels less afraid. Mr Kusakabe would be glad you agreed.
> - Not really, but being with people does. → That’s a fair answer. It may be the company that helps, as much as the laughing. Either way, the girls aren’t alone, and that matters.
> - It helps, but so does telling a grown-up. → That’s a fair answer. Mei told her father what she heard, and that was brave. Laughing and telling someone together make a good pair.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether laughing helps with fear and what else helps._

_Steam drifts under the door. From the bathhouse comes the biggest, silliest laugh you have ever heard from a grown man, and then two small ones._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-4, ask): steam you can see is tiny drops of water that formed when hot water vapour met cooler air  
> _Steam drifts under the door. Hot bath water gives off invisible water vapour._  
> _What turns it into the steam you can see?_  
> - ✅ The vapour meets cooler air and turns back into tiny drops of water. → Yes. Cooling turns the vapour into drops, and the drops make the white cloud. It’s a change that can go backwards, too, with heat.
> - The water burns and makes smoke. → Nothing is burning. It is not smoke. Cooler air turns the invisible vapour back into tiny drops of water.
> - The steam is made of air. → Steam is water, not air. Vapour meets cooler air and turns back into tiny water drops.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that visible steam is tiny drops of water made when hot vapour meets cooler air._


**Choices**

- **Laugh as loud as you can.**
    - You laugh as loud as you can. It sounds ridiculous, which makes you laugh properly.
- **Tickle your Nubbin until it squeaks.**
