## A23-1. The Presentation

- `a23-01-s1`: Step down into the page and find Rafiki’s baobab. · _Touch the red rock on the page, then follow the path down to the big tree._
- `a23-01-s2`: Walk with Rafiki to the great rock, through the gathering animals. · _Keep close behind Rafiki’s stick. The herds will part for him._

#### `reader:a23-01-page` · [main](main.md#a23-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page of red and gold: a great rock standing over a plain, and the sun just coming up behind it._  
_The light spills off the paper and over your hands, warm as bathwater._  
_Then there is dry grass to your knees, a smell of dust and dew, and a tree with a trunk as fat as a cottage._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, notice): what a savanna is, and where real ones are  
> _This is savanna: a warm grassland with a few trees standing alone, and a long dry season between the rains._  
> _Real savannas stretch across Kenya and Tanzania. This page is made up._  
> _It is a cousin of Hell’s Gate, a park in Kenya with red cliffs, golden grass and steam rising from cracks._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what a savanna is and that real ones lie in Kenya and Tanzania, in East Africa._

_Somebody sits in the fork of it, cracking a gourd on his knee: an old mandrill with a white beard and a long stick._  
**Rafiki**: Ah. There you are.  
**Rafiki**: The page said someone was coming. It did not say when, so I made tea for nobody, twice.  
**Rafiki**: Karibu. That means welcome.  
**Rafiki**: Come up, come up. Mind the third branch; it has opinions.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): one word can have more than one meaning  
> **Rafiki**: Karibu means welcome. It is also the Swahili word for near.  
> **Rafiki**: So why do you think people say it to a guest?  
> - ✅ It means “come near: you are welcome close to us” → Just so. A welcome is an invitation to come near. One word, two meanings, one idea.
> - It sounds like a bird → It does sound musical! But the real reason is that to welcome someone is to say “come near”. That is why karibu means both.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Swahili word karibu means both welcome and near, and why those fit together._

_Your Nubbin is up the trunk before you, hand over hand. It sits down in the fork as if it had always lived there._  
**Rafiki**: I am Rafiki. In Swahili, rafiki means friend.  
**Rafiki**: This is my tree. Today a king shows his son to the world, and I need somebody to carry gourds.  
**You**: I can carry gourds.  
**Rafiki**: Good. Everyone can.  
**Rafiki**: Not everyone does.  

**Choices**

- **Take the small gourd, carefully.**
    - **Rafiki**: Carefully. Good.
    - **Rafiki**: Either you have done this before, or you are frightened of me. Both are useful.
- **Ask what your Nubbin should carry.**
    - **Rafiki**: Its own self, today. That is plenty for something so new.
- **Say you’d better hurry.**
    - **Rafiki**: Polepole. Slowly.
    - **Rafiki**: The sun is coming up whether we run or not.
    - It is the first time he says it to you. It will not be the last.

#### `reader:a23-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a23-01-page-done)

_Rafiki swings down from the tree on his stick and sets off across the grass without looking back. You follow._  
**Rafiki**: Polepole. The sun is not going anywhere.  
**Rafiki**: Well. Up.  
**Rafiki**: But not anywhere.  
_Your feet want to run, and your head is full of fizz. Your Nubbin copies you, which does not help._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): slowing down helps us notice and think  
> _Rafiki says going polepole (slowly) lets you notice things. When might that help you?_  
> - ✅ When I am excited or in a rush and might miss something important → Exactly. Slowing down, even for a breath, gives your head time to catch up with your feet.
> - Never; fast is always better → Fast is brilliant for some things, like escaping a bee. But for noticing, listening and learning, slower wins.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how slowing down helps us notice, listen and think._

**Rafiki**: Polepole is not a rule. It is a way of walking that lets you notice things.  

#### `reader:a23-01-gathering` · [main](main.md#a23-01-gathering)

_The whole plain is walking the same way: zebra, antelope, a family of elephants, and birds in clouds over all of them._  
_Giraffes go past with their heads up in the morning, swaying like slow ships._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): a giraffe’s long neck has the same number of bones as ours  
> **Rafiki**: A giraffe’s neck has seven bones, the same number as yours. Each bone is just very, very long.  
> **Rafiki**: That long neck lets it eat leaves from the tops of trees where no other animal can reach.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a giraffe has seven neck bones, like a person, and that its long neck helps it reach leaves._

**Rafiki**: Twiga. Giraffe.  
**Rafiki**: Say it.  
**You**: Twiga.  
**Rafiki**: Good. Now you know one thing more than you did.  
**Rafiki**: Do that every day and you will be old and clever, like me.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiplication as equal groups  
> _A group of giraffes is called a tower. Rafiki counts four towers on the path, with three giraffes in each._  
> _How many giraffes is that?_  
> - ✅ 12 → Yes. Four groups of three: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12, or 4 × 3 = 12.
> - 7 → That is 4 + 3. We need four groups of three: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.
> - 9 → That is only three groups of three. With four groups it is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out 4 groups of 3 giraffes (4 × 3 = 12)._

_A blue-black hornbill flutters down onto a stone in front of you, very important, and looks you up and down._  
**Zazu**: Name? Business?  
**Zazu**: Are you on the list? There is a list.  
**Zazu**: I keep the list.  
**Rafiki**: They are with me, Zazu.  
**Zazu**: Everything strange is with you. Very well.  
**Zazu**: Go up. Mind the king’s tail; he is particular about his tail.  

**Choices**

- **Bow to Zazu.**
    - **Zazu**: Oh! Well.
    - **Zazu**: Somebody was raised properly.

- **Let your Nubbin say hello.**

#### `reader:a23-01-gathering-done` · [main](main.md#a23-01-gathering-done)

_The king comes down to meet Rafiki, and they hold each other, the big lion and the old mandrill, the way old friends do._  
**Mufasa**: Rafiki. You came.  
**Rafiki**: I always come. Now.  
**Rafiki**: Let us see him.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): what a character’s words tell us about them  
> _The king says “You came.” Rafiki says “I always come.”_  
> _What does that tell us about Rafiki?_  
> - ✅ He is loyal and can be counted on → Yes. “Always” is a strong word. Characters show who they are by what they promise, and what they do.
> - He is rude → It could sound that way, but look at the hug. The word “always” shows he is reliable, not rude.
> - He is lost → Rafiki knows exactly where he is. “I always come” tells us he is someone you can count on.
> _For grown-ups: Inferred a character trait (loyalty) from a short piece of dialogue._


#### `reader:a23-01-presentation` · [main](main.md#a23-01-presentation)

_The lioness, Sarabi, moves her paws aside. Between them is a cub the colour of new bread, blinking at all of you._  
**Sarabi**: Gently. He is three days old, and already he thinks he owns the place.  
_Rafiki shakes a rattle of gourds over the cub. The cub sneezes, and the king laughs, low, like thunder a long way off._  

> **Lesson** (music, 4-PS3-2, notice): sound comes from vibration, and shakers make rhythm  
> **Rafiki**: A dried gourd with seeds inside is a shaker. The seeds bounce, the gourd shivers, and the shiver reaches your ear as sound.  
> **Rafiki**: People all over the world make shakers. The beat they keep is older than any song I know.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a shaker makes sound by vibrating, and that people everywhere keep rhythm with shakers._

_Rafiki dips his thumb in a gourd of fruit-red and draws one line across the cub’s brow._  
_Your Nubbin climbs your back, then your shoulder, then your head, to see._  
_Rafiki walks to the very edge of the rock and holds the cub up in both hands, high, over everything._  
_Down on the plain, every animal sees him at once. Elephants trumpet and zebra stamp, and the birds go up in one great wheel._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS3-1, ask): young animals are like their parents but not the same  
> _Lion cubs are born with faint spots that fade as they grow, and a male cub has no mane until he is older._  
> _What does that tell us about cubs and adult lions?_  
> - ✅ Young animals can look different from their parents at first → Yes. Cubs are like their parents but not the same: spots, small size, no mane yet. They change as they grow.
> - Cubs look exactly like adult lions → Almost, but not quite. A cub has faint spots and a smaller body. Those spots fade as it grows into a lion.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that young animals can look different from adults and change as they grow._

_Then the whole plain kneels._  

**Choices**

- **Kneel with the plain.**
    - You kneel on the warm stone. Your Nubbin, still on your head, kneels too, which is harder than it looks.
- **Stay standing, and watch the king’s face.**
    - The king is not looking at the plain. He is looking at his son, the way you look at a fire on a cold night.

#### `reader:a23-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a23-01-complete)

_The cub goes back to his mother and falls asleep at once, worn out by being important._  
**Mufasa**: His name is Simba. In Swahili, simba means lion.  
**Mufasa**: Rafiki tells me you are a guest. Then you are welcome on this rock, guest.  
_Rafiki is quiet on the walk home. Halfway there he stops and looks back at the rock._  
**Rafiki**: There was one face missing up there. The king’s brother.  
**Rafiki**: He did not come.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): thinking about why someone stays away  
> _The king’s brother did not come to the celebration. Why might someone stay away from a happy event?_  
> _There is no one right answer._  
> - He might feel left out or cross → That’s a fair answer. Feeling left out can make a person keep away. Rafiki noticed, and that is worth noticing.
> - He might just be busy or shy → That’s a fair answer too. Sometimes people stay away for quiet reasons. Rafiki is watching, to find out which it is.
> - He might not be happy for his brother → That’s a fair answer. Mixed feelings are real. Rafiki’s “he will come or he will not” shows he is waiting to see.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why someone might stay away from a celebration, with no single right answer._

**Rafiki**: Well. He will come or he will not.  
**Rafiki**: Carry the gourds. We have painting to do.

## A23-2. Rafiki’s Tree

- `a23-02-s1`: Mix Rafiki’s paints: ochre from the riverbank, and red from the fruit. · _Bring yellow earth from the dry riverbed and ripe fruit from the tree, then grind them in the shell._
- `a23-02-s2`: Hold the shell while Rafiki paints the cub on the wall of his tree. · _Stand still beside him with the paints. Your Nubbin will try to help._

#### `reader:a23-02-paints` · [main](main.md#a23-02-paints)

_Inside, the baobab is hollow and cool, and taller than it looks. The walls are painted all the way up._  
_Lions, mostly: kings and queens and cubs in rust and white and yellow, some so old they have gone the colour of tea._  
**Rafiki**: My family. Not my family.  
**Rafiki**: The family I watch. Which is the same thing, if you watch long enough.  
**Rafiki**: Now. The cub must go up on the wall.  
**Rafiki**: I need ochre, and red. You will mix them, because my knees have retired.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): ochre is earth coloured by iron, used as paint for a very long time  
> **Rafiki**: Ochre is earth that is yellow, red or brown because of iron minerals in it. Grind it fine and it becomes paint.  
> **Rafiki**: People have painted with ochre for tens of thousands of years. It is some of the oldest paint there is.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that ochre is earth coloured by iron minerals and has been used as paint for tens of thousands of years._

_He gives you a tortoise shell for a bowl and a smooth stone for grinding._  
**Rafiki**: Ochre is the yellow earth by the dry river. Red is the fruit of this tree: the ripe ones, not the ones that make you sorry.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiplication word problem  
> _Rafiki says each shell of paint needs 3 spoonfuls of ground ochre, and he wants 4 shells for the wall._  
> _How many spoonfuls of ochre will you grind?_  
> - ✅ 12 → Yes. Four shells, three spoonfuls each: 4 × 3 = 12. Polepole, now. That is a lot of grinding.
> - 7 → That is 4 + 3. Each of the 4 shells needs 3 spoonfuls, so 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.
> - 34 → That is the digits side by side. Multiply instead: 4 × 3 = 12.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a multiplication word problem: 4 shells × 3 spoonfuls = 12._

_Your Nubbin has already found a fruit. It has red juice up to the elbows and looks thrilled._  
**Rafiki**: Hm. That one has started without you.  

**Choices**

- **Grind it slowly, the way he showed you.**
    - **Rafiki**: Polepole. Yes.
    - **Rafiki**: Slow paint stays on the wall. Fast paint ends up on the visitor.
- **Grind it fast, to get it done.**
    - A puff of yellow dust goes up your nose. You sneeze it all over the floor.
    - **Rafiki**: Polepole.
- **Wonder aloud whether the fruit is nice to eat.**
    - **Rafiki**: It is. Eat one first.
    - **Rafiki**: A hungry painter paints nothing but food.

#### `reader:a23-02-paints-done` · [main](main.md#a23-02-paints-done)

**Rafiki**: Good. Asante.  
**Rafiki**: That is thank you. You will hear it from me more than you expect.  
**Rafiki**: Asante sana is thank you very much. Sana means very.  
**Rafiki**: Try it.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): learning a new word from its parts  
> _Asante means thank you, and sana means very. What does “asante sana” mean?_  
> - ✅ Thank you very much → Yes! You added two meanings together. Words in every language are built from small pieces like this.
> - Good morning → Not quite. Asante is thank you and sana is very, so it is thank you very much.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the meaning of the Swahili phrase asante sana (thank you very much) from its two words._

_Rafiki climbs the inside of the tree with the shell hooked on his stick, up to a bare patch of bark at the height of your head._  
_He paints with his fingers, quick and sure: a round body, four fat paws, ears like two little hills._  
**Rafiki**: There. Simba.  
**Rafiki**: Not a good likeness. He is much crosser in life.

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.3-5, notice): people keep the past alive with paintings, stories and songs  
> **Rafiki**: Long before anyone wrote things down, people kept their past by painting it on walls, telling it as stories, and singing it.  
> **Rafiki**: That is called oral history when it is spoken.  
> **Rafiki**: A wall like mine, and an old story, both carry the past to people who were not there.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that people have kept the past alive with paintings and spoken stories, called oral history._

_Your Nubbin reaches into the shell and puts a red paw-print on its own forehead._  
**Rafiki**: Ha! It wants to be on the wall as well.  
**Rafiki**: Not today, small one. Painting someone is a serious thing.  
**Rafiki**: It means you mean to remember them.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): figurative language and its meaning  
> _Rafiki says “a lion is quick, and a wall is slow,” and that somebody has to hold on to things for the quick ones._  
> _What do you think he means?_  
> - ✅ A painting lasts longer than a life, so it can keep a memory safe → Just so. Lions come and go. A wall stays, and holds them for whoever comes next.
> - Walls are slower than lions at running → That is true, and it is the surface of what I said. Underneath: a wall lasts, so it can remember for the quick ones.
> _For grown-ups: Interpreted a saying about lions being quick and a wall being slow as a way of talking about memory._


**Choices**

- **Ask why he paints them.**
    - **Rafiki**: Because a lion is quick, and a wall is slow. Somebody has to hold on to things for the quick ones.
- **Look at the oldest paintings, near the top.**
    - Up near the roof, a lion is painted so faint you can only see it when you stop trying. Rafiki does not say who it was.
- **Wipe your Nubbin’s forehead.**
    - **Rafiki**: Leave it. It suits it.
    - **Rafiki**: Red, for a small brave one. It will wash off.
    - **Rafiki**: Most things do.

> _Rafiki says an empty gourd that is waiting is the best sort of gourd. What do you think he means?_

> - Being ready for something is a kind of treasure → That’s a fair answer. An empty gourd can become anything: water, seeds, a rattle. Being ready is full of possibility.
> - He means you should not hurry to fill things → That’s a fair answer. Rafiki loves polepole. Waiting well, with space left to fill, is a skill.
> - He is just being funny → That’s a fair answer too. Rafiki is funny. But notice how he often hides a true thing inside a joke.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what Rafiki means by an empty gourd that is waiting, with no single right answer._

## A23-3. Everything the Light Touches

#### `reader:a23-03-dawn` · [main](main.md#a23-03-dawn)

_The page turns. Simba is bigger now, all feet and ears, and he has been awake since a quarter to six._  
_Rafiki has sent you up the rock with a message for the king: the rains are late, and Rafiki is watching._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals  
> _Simba woke at 5:45 in the morning. The sun came up at 6:30._  
> _How many minutes was Simba awake before sunrise?_  
> - ✅ 45 minutes → Yes. From 5:45 to 6:00 is 15 minutes, and 6:00 to 6:30 is 30 more: 15 + 30 = 45.
> - 15 minutes → That is only 5:45 to 6:00. Add 30 more minutes to reach 6:30: 15 + 30 = 45 minutes.
> - 75 minutes → That adds instead of counting the gap. From 5:45 up to 6:30 is 15 + 30 = 45 minutes.
> _For grown-ups: Found a time interval: from 5:45 to 6:30 is 45 minutes._

_Before you can give it, a small lion lands on your feet._  
**Simba**: Are you the guest? Dad says you’re the guest.  
**Simba**: Do you want to see me pounce?  
_He does not wait for an answer. He pounces on your Nubbin, and the two of them roll off down the rock in one ball._  
**Mufasa**: Simba. Guests are not for pouncing.  
**Simba**: It started it!  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): noticing whether others are enjoying rough play  
> _Simba pounced on your Nubbin, and it came up delighted. How can you tell whether someone is enjoying rough play?_  
> - ✅ They smile, come back for more and play back → Exactly. Look for willing faces and a game that goes both ways. If someone stops smiling, stop too.
> - They stay quiet → Quiet can mean anything. Watch the face and body: smiling and playing back is a yes; pulling away is a no.
> _For grown-ups: Learned to tell whether a friend is enjoying rough play: smiles, playing back, and stopping if they pull away._

_It did not start it. Your Nubbin comes up out of the grass looking delighted, and dives straight back in._  
**Mufasa**: Rafiki’s message? He is always watching.  
**Mufasa**: That is a comfort, mostly. Come.  
**Mufasa**: There is something I want my son to see.  

**Choices**

#### `reader:a23-03-dawn-done` · [main](main.md#a23-03-dawn-done)

_The king walks to the very top of the rock, and Simba scrambles after him. You follow with your Nubbin under your arm._  
_Ahead of you, the sun is a thin gold edge on the far side of the plain._  
_Rafiki’s rock is tall enough to see the whole of it, from the east, where the sun is climbing, to the hills far off in the west._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, ask): compass directions and the Sun’s path  
> _The Sun rises in the east. If you stand on the rock and face the sunrise, which way are the hills behind you?_  
> - ✅ West → Yes. If you face east, west is directly behind you. The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west.
> - North → North would be on your left as you face east. Directly behind you is west.
> - East → East is where you are looking, at the sunrise. Behind you is the opposite: west.
> _For grown-ups: Used the Sun rising in the east to work out that west is behind you._


#### `reader:a23-03-light` · [main](main.md#a23-03-light)

_The sun comes up over the edge of the world, and the light runs across the plain like water spilt on a table._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): recognising a simile  
> _“The light runs across the plain like water spilt on a table.” What is this comparing, and how can you tell?_  
> - ✅ Light and water, and the word “like” shows it → Yes. A comparison that uses “like” or “as” is a simile. It helps us picture the light spreading quickly and smoothly.
> - It says the light is wet → Not quite. The writer is not saying the light is wet; the word “like” says it moves the way water does. That is a simile.
> _For grown-ups: Identified a simile and what it compares._

**Mufasa**: Look, Simba. As far as the light goes.  
**Mufasa**: One day all of it will be yours to look after.  
**Simba**: All of it? Even the bit with the river?  
**Mufasa**: Even the river.  
_Far off, past the edge of the light, there is a grey place where nothing seems to grow._  
**Mufasa**: That is beyond our borders. You must never go there.  
**Mufasa**: Everything here is joined. The grass feeds the antelope, the antelope feed us, and when we lie down at the end, we feed the grass.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): food chains: energy passes along, and decomposers return it to the soil  
> **Mufasa**: Grass makes its own food from sunlight. An antelope eats the grass.  
> **Mufasa**: A lion eats the antelope. That is a food chain.  
> **Mufasa**: And when anything dies, beetles, fungi and tiny soil creatures break it down and give it back to the ground, so the grass can grow.  
> **Mufasa**: That is why every creature on the plain matters.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned a savanna food chain (grass, antelope, lion) and that decomposers return nutrients to the soil._

**Simba**: So we eat the antelope, and then the antelope… eat us? A bit?  
**Mufasa**: A bit. Later.  
**Mufasa**: Much later.  

**Choices**

- **Ask the king what the grey place is.**
    - **Mufasa**: Somewhere I hope none of you ever learn about.

#### `reader:a23-03-pounce` · [main](main.md#a23-03-pounce)

_Zazu lands on a rock beside the king with the morning report. It is long, and he has clearly practised it._  
**Zazu**: Giraffes: tall as ever. Elephants: no complaints.  
**Zazu**: The cheetahs are hungry, and the hippos would like a word about mud.  
**Mufasa**: Simba. Come here.  
**Mufasa**: Low down in the grass. Watch his tail feathers.  
**Mufasa**: Slowly. Slower than that.  
_Simba wiggles his bottom, creeps three steps, and launches himself at the hornbill with a squeak._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): two-step measurement word problem  
> _Simba creeps three steps, each 40 centimetres long, and then pounces another 120 centimetres. How far has he moved in all, in centimetres?_  
> - ✅ 240 cm → Yes. Three steps of 40 cm is 120 cm, and 120 + 120 = 240 cm. That is 2 metres 40 centimetres.

#### `reader:a23-margin-hazel` · [main](main.md#a23-margin-hazel)

_Nova’s atlas lies open at the still pool among the roots, and a grown lion looking down into it._  
_Beside the pool, in faint pencil you have never noticed, there is a note in the margin. You know the hand._  
_“Simba found his father in the water. We carry them, you know.”_  
_“In the face. Often the chin.”_  
_And under it, one letter: H._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): mixed feelings when we find something from someone we love  
> _A note from someone you love, found when you were not expecting it, can stir up more than one feeling. What might you feel?_  
> _There is no one right answer._  
> - Happy, because it is like hearing their voice → That’s a fair answer. Familiar handwriting can feel like a visit.
> - Sad, because they are not here to talk to → That’s a fair answer. Missing someone is part of loving them.
> - Both at once → That’s a fair answer. Two feelings can sit side by side, and both are allowed.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about the mixed feelings a note from a loved one can bring, with no single right answer._

#### `reader:a23-grove-painting` · [main](main.md#a23-grove-painting)

_You look into the hollow of Hazel’s crooked tree, the way you do sometimes, for no reason. Something is in there that wasn’t before._  
_A little painting on a piece of bark: your Nubbin in yellow ochre, round and pleased, with a line of fruit-red across its brow._  
_Your Nubbin looks at it for a long time. Then it touches its own forehead, where the red mark used to be._  
_Painting someone means you mean to remember them. Somebody, somewhere, means to._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): what it means to be remembered  
> _“Painting someone means you mean to remember them.” Why might being remembered matter to a person, or a Nubbin?_  
> _There is no one right answer._  
> - It tells us we mattered to someone → That’s a fair answer. To be remembered is to be told, quietly, that you were seen and valued.
> - It keeps us connected to people we cannot see → That’s a fair answer. Memory is a bridge between people, even across distance or time.
> - It does not matter, only the living matter → That’s a fair answer, and an honest one. Many people think so. But notice how Rafiki’s wall, and this little painting, comfort those who are still here.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why being remembered matters, with no single right answer._
