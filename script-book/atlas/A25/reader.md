# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A25: The Nothing

## A25-1. The Bookshop

- `a25-01-s1`: Step through the page, behind the shop bell. · _Touch the little bell on the page. Stay behind it, where nobody in the shop can see you._
- `a25-01-s2`: Follow the boy through the rain to his school. · _Keep him in sight. He is slow on his feet, but he knows the short cuts._
- `a25-01-s3`: Sit in the attic with Bastian while he opens the book. · _Find a corner on the old gym mats and keep quiet. He thinks he is alone._

#### `reader:a25-01-shop` · [main](main.md#a25-01-shop)

_Nova’s atlas opens at a page that is mostly a glass door, with a little bell above it._  
_It is raining on the page. Real rain, running off the edge and on to your table._  
_You touch the bell. It rings, and you are standing behind it in a dim antiquarian bookshop, full of old and rare books._  
_Books climb to the ceiling. In an armchair between the towers, an old man in a black suit is reading._  
_Nobody looks at you. Your Nubbin waves at the old man, and he turns a page._  
_The door bangs open. A boy tumbles in, soaked, with a school bag on his back._  
_He is about ten, round, with wet hair in his eyes. He shuts the door and leans on it, panting._  
**Mr Coreander**: Out. I don’t sell to children.  
**Mr Coreander**: They sneeze on books and ask whether there are pictures.  
**Bastian**: I’m not buying anything. There are boys outside.  
**Bastian**: Just until they’ve gone past. Please.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): what to do when other children are unkind: go somewhere safe and tell a trusted grown-up  
> _Bastian is hiding from boys who were unkind to him._  
> _What is a good thing to do when that happens to you?_  
> - ✅ Get somewhere safe, and tell a grown-up you trust. → Yes. Being unkind to someone is never their fault. It is never yours, either. A grown-up you trust can help it stop, and you don’t have to carry it alone.
> - Hit them back. → It’s very hard to feel so cross. But hitting tends to make more trouble. Getting safe and telling a trusted grown-up can really make it stop.
> - Keep it a secret. → Keeping it secret is understandable. But there is nothing to be ashamed of. Telling a trusted person is the braver thing, and it lets help find you.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what to do when other children are unkind: get safe and tell a trusted adult._

**Mr Coreander**: Hm. What’s your name?  
**Bastian**: Bastian Balthazar Bux.  
**Mr Coreander**: That’s a lot of b’s for one name. Sit down, then.  
**Mr Coreander**: Don’t touch anything.  

**Choices**

- **Keep very still behind the bell.**
    - You keep still. The bell, which is right by your ear, decides to keep still too.
- **Take out your notebook and write down the shop’s name.**
    - You copy the letters from the glass, backwards first and then the right way round. Your pencil sounds very loud.
- **Let your Nubbin sniff the nearest pile of books.**
    - Your Nubbin sniffs the nearest pile, and sneezes on a book. That proves the old man right about something.

#### `reader:a25-01-shop-done` · [main](main.md#a25-01-shop-done)

_A telephone rings in the back room. The old man grumbles his way out and leaves his book on the armchair._  
_It is bound in copper-coloured silk. On the cover, one light snake and one dark snake bite each other’s tails._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.3-5, notice): an ancient symbol: the snake that bites its tail (the ouroboros)  
> _A note in Nova’s small writing has appeared in the margin of the page._  
> _“A snake biting its own tail is one of the oldest signs there is. Artists in ancient Egypt drew it over three thousand years ago.”_  
> _“Later, the ancient Greeks gave it the name we still use: ouroboros. It stands for something that goes round and round.”_  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the snake biting its tail is a very old symbol from ancient Egypt, with a Greek name, meaning something that goes round and round._


#### `reader:a25-01-run` · [main](main.md#a25-01-run)

_Bastian looks at the book. Then he looks at the back room, where the old man is shouting at the telephone about an invoice._  
_Then he slips the book under his coat and runs, leaving the door open and the bell still ringing._  
_You hurry after him. Your Nubbin follows, running very fast and very flat, the way it runs in thunderstorms._  
**Bastian**: I’m a thief now. I have never taken anything before in my whole life.  
_He can’t go home, he tells a lamp-post. His father doesn’t shout._  
_He hardly says anything any more._  
_The clock over the school gate says twenty-five past nine. School began at nine, and there is a maths test today._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals: from 9:00 to 9:25 is 25 minutes  
> _School began at nine o’clock. The clock over the gate says twenty-five past nine._  
> _How many minutes late is Bastian?_  
> - ✅ 25 minutes → Yes. From 9:00 to 9:25 is 25 minutes. “Twenty-five past” means 25 minutes after the hour.
> - 35 minutes → Let’s count it on the clock. From nine to half past is 30 minutes. Twenty-five past is a bit before half past, so it is 25 minutes.
> - 5 minutes → That is the 5 in “twenty-five”, but it is not the answer. “Twenty-five past nine” means 25 minutes after nine, so he is 25 minutes late.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a time interval on a clock: from 9:00 to 9:25 is 25 minutes._

**Bastian**: Can’t go in there either.  
_He goes in by the back door instead, up the back stairs and past the caretaker’s cupboard, all the way to the top._  

#### `reader:a25-01-run-done` · [main](main.md#a25-01-run-done)

_At the top of the stairs there is a door nobody uses. It isn’t locked, because it has never needed to be._  
_Bastian stops with his hand on the cold handle. His heart is going very fast._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): making things right after a mistake: honesty, saying sorry, giving back  
> _Bastian knows he took the book without asking._  
> _If you were him, what would you do next?_  
> - Take it back straight away. → That’s a fair answer. Giving something back quickly keeps a mistake small. Bastian is thinking about it too.
> - Tell the bookseller the truth and say sorry. → That is an honest and a brave answer. A sorry works best when it is said to the person.
> - Read a little first, and decide after. → That’s what Bastian says he will do. Many people feel that way when they are scared. But the longer a mistake waits, the heavier it feels.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how to make things right after taking something without asking._


#### `reader:a25-01-attic` · [main](main.md#a25-01-attic)

_The school attic is long and low, full of broken desks, rolled-up maps and a heap of old gym mats._  
_Rain drums on the roof slates. It is grey up here, and it smells of dust and chalk._  
_Bastian finds some grey army blankets, wraps himself in two, and sits on the mats like a parcel._  
_He takes out his sandwich and his apple, looks at them, and puts them away again._  
**Bastian**: Just the first page. Then I’ll take it back.  
**Bastian**: Probably.  
_He opens the book. Over his shoulder, unseen, you notice that its words are printed in two colours._  
_The words about the attic are rust-red. As he turns the page, words about a forest begin, and they are green._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.3, ask): how an author can use two colours to show two places (setting)  
> _In this book, the attic is printed in red and the forest in green. Why would an author do that?_  
> - ✅ So readers always know which world they are in. → Yes. The colour is a clue: red is the attic, green is the faraway land. On a simple press, each colour needs its own run through the machine.
> - Those were the only two inks the printer had. → A printer can use any colour. Using two was a choice, and it took extra work. The choice does a job: it shows where we are.
> - Just to look pretty. → It does look pretty. But it also tells you which world you are reading about.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why an author might print two settings in two colours._

_Your Nubbin burrows into the blankets and settles against his side, because it is warm there._  
_Bastian doesn’t notice. He has already started reading._  

**Choices**

- **Sit beside him on the mats.**
    - You sit down beside him. He shifts over without looking up, the way people do for someone they can’t see.
- **Sit by the skylight and read your own copy.**
    - Your atlas page shows exactly the same words. You read along.

#### `reader:a25-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a25-01-complete)

_The attic is drawn in rust-red ink. As Bastian reads, the edges of the page begin to turn green._  
_A forest grows in from the corners, and the rain on the slates turns into wind in leaves._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, notice): sounds are vibrations: rain on slates and wind in leaves  
> _Listen to the page. Rain pats the slates, and wind rustles the leaves._  
> _Both sounds are made the same way: by something shaking, very quickly._  
> _Raindrops make slates ring, and wind makes leaves flutter. Every sound you have ever heard began with a shake._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that every sound is made by vibrations, using rain on slates and wind in leaves._

_You are not in the attic any more. You are on a forest path at night, beside a campfire, where someone is singing badly._  
_Very faintly, if you listen, you can still hear a boy turning a page._  

### Glossary

- **antiquarian**: Having to do with old and rare things. An antiquarian bookshop sells old and rare books.
- **invoice**: A bill that says how much money is owed.

## A25-2. The Messengers

- `a25-02-s1`: Join the messengers at the campfire in the forest. · _Follow the bad singing. It comes from the clearing with the fire._
- `a25-02-s2`: Hear what the messengers have seen, and go with them to look. · _Walk with Ückück to the edge of the wood. You don’t have to go close._

#### `reader:a25-02-fire` · [main](main.md#a25-02-fire)

_In the clearing sits a will-o’-the-wisp, a little ball of light, singing to keep its courage up. It isn’t working._  
_A rockchewer comes crunching out of the trees. He is the size of a hill, and he is eating a small piece of it._  
_Then a night-hob arrives riding a bat, and a tiny, no bigger than your thumb, on a snail that has run all the way._  
**Blubb**: Strangers! Friendly ones?  
**Blubb**: Say friendly. I’m Blubb, and I’m very nervous.  
_The rockchewer says, “My name has fourteen letters. You may call me Rockchewer._  
_Sit. Pebble?”_  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): respect for other people’s customs: accepting a gift politely  
> _A rockchewer holds out a pebble. To him, it is a kind and tasty gift._  
> _What is the polite thing to do?_  
> - ✅ Take it kindly and say thank you. → Yes. People have different customs, and a gift is a gift. You don’t have to eat it. Thanking him is how you show respect.
> - Say “Yuck!” → A pebble is a strange snack to us. But to him it is a present, and “yuck” would hurt. Saying thank you shows respect for how other people do things.
> - Walk away. → That might feel safe. But it would also say that his kindness didn’t count. Saying thank you is the respectful way.
> _For grown-ups: Practised respecting a different custom by accepting a gift politely._

_The night-hob says, “We are all going the same way, it seems: to the Ivory Tower, with bad news.”_  
_The tiny one says, “My snail is the fastest in my whole country. Please don’t step on him.”_  
_Your Nubbin sits down next to the snail, very carefully, and the two of them look at each other for a long time._  

**Choices**

- **Accept the pebble, politely.**
    - You hold it as if you might eat it later. The rockchewer nods, pleased.
    - Good manners matter, whatever size you are.
- **Ask what the bad news is.**
    - **Ückück**: The same as everyone’s, I expect. That is what frightens me.

#### `reader:a25-02-fire-done` · [main](main.md#a25-02-fire-done)

_Each came from a different corner of Fantastica to the same fire, carrying the same news._  
_The rockchewer lays his pebbles on a flat stone, ready to share. There are eighteen._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): sharing equally: 18 ÷ 6 = 3  
> _There are six of you: four messengers, you, and your Nubbin._  
> _Share the eighteen pebbles fairly. How many does each one get?_  
> - ✅ 3 each → Yes. 6 groups of 3 make 18, so 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
> - 4 each → Let’s check. Six friends with 4 each would need 6 × 4 = 24 pebbles. We only have 18. Six lots of 3 is 18.
> - 2 each → Six friends with 2 each is 6 × 2 = 12. That leaves 6 pebbles over. Share those as well, and everyone has 3.
> _For grown-ups: Shared 18 things equally between 6 (18 ÷ 6 = 3)._


#### `reader:a25-02-gap` · [main](main.md#a25-02-gap)

**Blubb**: In my marsh, a piece of the bank went. Not burnt.  
**Blubb**: Not flooded. Just not there any more.  
**Pyornkrachzark**: Mountain too. Bit missing.  
**Pyornkrachzark**: Didn’t eat it. Honest.  
**Gluckuk**: At home, a lake with a hole in the middle where nothing was. Not water.  
**Gluckuk**: Not dry. Nothing.  
**Ückück**: Come. I am Ückück, and I saw it from the air.  
**Ückück**: It’s here too now.  
_At the edge of the wood, the trees just stop. It isn’t a cliff or a clearing: the picture simply isn’t there._  
_When you look at the gap, your eyes feel as if they have gone blind on that side. The singing birds fall quiet._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): the blind spot: each eye has a spot that cannot see, and the brain fills it in  
> _Here is something odd and true about your own eyes._  
> _In each eye there is one tiny spot, where the nerve leaves, that cannot see anything._  
> _You never notice. Your brain quietly fills the gap with whatever is around it._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that each eye has a blind spot that the brain fills in, so we do not notice it._

_Your Nubbin won’t go a step closer. It sits down hard in the leaves and takes hold of your boot._  
**Ückück**: It’s growing. Every land says so.  
**Ückück**: The Empress must know what it is.  

**Choices**

- **Step back with your Nubbin.**
    - You step back. The birds start singing again a little way off, as if nothing happened.
- **Look away from the gap, deliberately.**
    - You make yourself look at a real leaf instead, with a hole a caterpillar made. It helps.

#### `reader:a25-02-gap-done` · [main](main.md#a25-02-gap-done)

_In the end Blubb gives it a name, in a very small voice: “the Nothing”._  
_Everyone wishes he hadn’t. Everyone feels a little better anyway._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): naming a scary thing makes it easier to talk about  
> _Why might giving a frightening thing a name make it feel a little smaller?_  
> - ✅ Once it has a name, we can talk about it together. → Yes. A name gives you something to hold on to, and something to share. The Nothing is still there. But talking about it is a first step.
> - A name makes it go away. → If only! A name doesn’t make a thing vanish, and the Nothing is still growing. What a name does is let us talk about it, and talking helps.
> - It doesn’t help at all. → Blubb might agree with you. Everyone wished he hadn’t said it. But naming a worry is how friends begin to face it together.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why naming a fear can make it easier to face._


#### `reader:a25-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a25-02-complete)

_The four messengers set off together at dawn, at the speed of the snail, which turns out to be very fast._  
_For a moment the green fades, and you see the attic in red. Bastian has pulled the blanket round his ears._  
**Bastian**: I’d have gone with them. I think I would.  
**Bastian**: If I could.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): what a character’s words tell us about his feelings and wishes  
> _Bastian says he would have gone with the messengers, if he could. What does that tell us about him?_  
> - He wishes he were braver than he thinks he is. → That’s a fair answer. He says “if I could”, as if he doesn’t believe he can.
> - He is lonely and would like friends to travel with. → That’s a fair answer. The messengers are a gang, and he is alone in an attic.
> - He just loves adventure stories. → That’s a fair answer. He is reading as fast as he can, so he surely does. And a story can also show us what we wish for.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what Bastian’s words show about his wishes (a think question)._

_He breaks off a corner of his sandwich and goes on reading._  

### Glossary

- **messenger**: A person who carries news from one place to another.
- **customs**: Ways of doing things that a group of people has followed for a long time.

## A25-3. The Ivory Tower

- `a25-03-s1`: Go with the messengers into the Ivory Tower. · _Climb the white spiral road through the gardens to the hall of physicians._
- `a25-03-s2`: Help Cairon find the road to the Grassy Sea. · _Unroll the maps in the physicians’ library and find the long road east._

#### `reader:a25-03-tower` · [main](main.md#a25-03-tower)

_The Ivory Tower rises out of a maze of flowers, white and tall, like a spiral shell standing on its end._  
_The halls are full of messengers from everywhere. Each one carries the same news, and each thinks theirs is new._  
**Ückück**: So many. I thought I was important.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): noticing how someone else feels, and saying something kind  
> _Ückück feels small, one messenger among hundreds. What could you say to him?_  
> - ✅ Your news matters. You came all this way. → Yes. A kind word that is true can make someone feel seen. Ückück really did fly a long way, and that counts.
> - Don’t worry, you’ll feel better soon. → That is kindly meant. But it can make someone feel that their feeling doesn’t count. It helps more to say what is true about them: you came a long way.
> - You’re right, there are a lot of messengers. → That is true about the numbers. But it would make Ückück feel even smaller. A good friend finds the true thing that helps.
> _For grown-ups: Practised noticing how someone feels and choosing a kind, true thing to say._

_In a high room the Empress’s physicians are arguing. In the middle stands a black centaur with a white beard._  
**Cairon**: Enough! None of you knows what is wrong with her, and neither do I.  
**Cairon**: She is ill in just the way the land is ill. It is one illness.  
**Cairon**: If it reaches her, there is no more Fantastica.  
_The room goes silent. Your Nubbin hides behind a pillar, which is the most sensible thing anyone has done all day._  

#### `reader:a25-03-tower-done` · [main](main.md#a25-03-tower-done)

_Cairon stamps a hoof. Then he looks very old, and sits down on the floor like a tired horse._  
**Cairon**: A physician finds the cause first, and then the cure. I have the problem, at least: one illness, in two places.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-5-ETS1-2, ask): defining a problem clearly before looking for a solution  
> _Cairon says the Empress’s illness and the Nothing are one problem. Why does that help?_  
> - ✅ Then one cure might mend both. → Yes. Naming the problem clearly is the first step to solving it.
> - It doesn’t help. Two problems would be easier. → It can feel that way! But two separate cures would be twice the work. If the problem is really one, one cure might mend both.
> - It means nothing can be done. → It sounds gloomy, and Cairon feels gloomy. But a problem we understand is a problem we can try to solve.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that solving a problem begins with saying clearly what the problem is._


#### `reader:a25-03-maps` · [main](main.md#a25-03-maps)

**Cairon**: She has chosen a hero to find the cure, and I must carry this to him.  
_Round his neck hangs an amulet: two snakes, one light, one dark, each biting the other’s tail._  
_It is the same sign that you saw on the cover of the book._  
**Cairon**: AURYN. Whoever wears it acts for her.  
**Cairon**: The hero is in the Grassy Sea. His name is Atreyu.  
**Cairon**: I don’t know the way. I am a physician.  
**Cairon**: I know the way to the kitchen, and to people’s insides.  
_The maps are huge and out of date. Half of them have gaps now, where the Nothing has been._  
**Cairon**: You, traveller, with the sharp eyes. Help an old horse.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, ask): compass directions: the Sun rises in the east  
> _The Grassy Sea lies far to the east._  
> _On a clear morning, which way do you face to look at the Sun rising?_  
> - ✅ East → Yes. The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west. Face the rising Sun, and east is in front of you.
> - West → West is where the Sun sets in the evening. Face the Sun as it rises in the morning, and you are looking east.
> - South → South is at your back when you face north, and the Sun does not rise there. The rising Sun is in the east.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Sun rises in the east and used it to find a direction on a map._


**Choices**

- **Follow the river on the map.**
    - The river goes east for a thousand miles and ends in grass. Cairon puts his finger on it and grunts, pleased.
- **Ask Ückück, who has seen it from the air.**
    - **Ückück**: East. Keep going east until there’s nothing but grass, and then go on.
    - **Ückück**: You can’t miss it.

#### `reader:a25-03-maps-done` · [main](main.md#a25-03-maps-done)

**Cairon**: Good. Then I gallop.  
**Cairon**: Will you come? On long roads I talk to myself, and I’m dull.  
_Your Nubbin climbs on to his back without asking. Cairon pretends not to notice, which is kind._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): multiplying to find a distance: 4 × 100 miles  
> _Say Cairon gallops 100 miles every day._  
> _How far will he have gone after 4 days?_  
> - ✅ 400 miles → Yes. Four lots of 100 is 100 + 100 + 100 + 100 = 400 miles.
> - 104 miles → That is 100 + 4, but there are four whole days, each of 100 miles. 4 × 100 = 400.
> - 300 miles → That is three days: 3 × 100 = 300. We need one more day. 4 × 100 = 400.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied a daily distance by a number of days (4 × 100 = 400 miles)._


#### `reader:a25-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a25-03-complete)

_The road east goes on for days. Cairon gallops until his legs shake, then walks, then gallops again._  
**Cairon**: A great warrior. Strong.  
**Cairon**: Wise. Grey at the temples, I should think.  
**Cairon**: I shall know him at once.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): comparing what a character expects with what you would picture  
> _Cairon is sure he knows what the hero will look like._  
> _Is there only one way a hero can look?_  
> - Heroes can be anyone. → That’s a fair answer. Heroes in stories, and in life, come in every shape and size. Cairon’s picture is only his picture.
> - Heroes are strong, like Cairon says. → That’s a fair answer. Strength is one thing a hero might have. But bravery and kindness don’t come in any particular size.
> - I am not sure yet. Let’s see who it is. → That’s a wise answer. Cairon will find out soon, and so will we.
> _For grown-ups: Compared a character’s picture of a hero with their own (a think question)._

_In the red attic, Bastian has put an old candle in his pocket for later. It is getting dark up there._  

### Glossary

- **physician**: A doctor.
- **amulet**: A small charm that is worn or carried to protect someone.

## A25-4. Atreyu

- `a25-04-s1`: Bring Cairon to the hunters’ camp in the Grassy Sea. · _Walk ahead of him through the tall grass. The tents are by the stream._
- `a25-04-s2`: Watch Atreyu take the amulet. · _Stand at the edge of the ring and let Cairon speak. It is not your message._
- `a25-04-s3`: Get your Nubbin down off Artax’s saddle. Or don’t. · _Talk to Artax. He has opinions._

#### `reader:a25-04-camp` · [main](main.md#a25-04-camp)

_The Grassy Sea is grass as far as you can see, moving in long waves like water. In places it is purple, where the buffalo graze._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): grassland as a habitat, and its different names around the world  
> _A grassland is a habitat with lots of grass and very few trees._  
> _On our Earth, grasslands have different names in different places: prairie, steppe, savanna and pampas._  
> _Grazing animals live there, and many small creatures hide in the tall grass._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what a grassland habitat is and four of its names (prairie, steppe, savanna, pampas)._

_The hunters’ camp is a ring of hide tents by a stream. The people are green-skinned, quiet and very good at spotting visitors._  
**Greenskin Hunter**: A centaur, a stranger and a… whatever that is. You’re a long way from anywhere.  
**Cairon**: I bring a message for Atreyu, from the Empress. Take me to him.  
**Greenskin Hunter**: Atreyu? He’s out with the herd.  
**Greenskin Hunter**: He’s meant to begin learning to hunt tomorrow.  
_Cairon’s eyebrows go up. He says nothing, but his tail swishes once, impatiently._  

#### `reader:a25-04-camp-done` · [main](main.md#a25-04-camp-done)

_Someone runs to fetch him. Cairon combs his beard with his fingers, to look his best for a hero._  
_While you wait, you count the tents around the ring. There are twelve, with three people living in each._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 12 groups of 3 make 36  
> _Twelve tents, with three people living in each. How many people live in the ring?_  
> - ✅ 36 people → Yes. 12 groups of 3 make 36, because 12 × 3 = 36.
> - 15 people → That is 12 + 3. But each of the twelve tents has its own three people. 12 × 3 = 36.
> - 24 people → That is 12 × 2, which would be two people in each tent. With three in each, 12 × 3 = 36.
> _For grown-ups: Used equal groups to multiply 12 × 3 = 36._


#### `reader:a25-04-amulet` · [main](main.md#a25-04-amulet)

_A boy comes through the grass. He is about ten years old, with green skin and dark hair, in a purple-red cloak._  
**Atreyu**: You asked for me?  
**Cairon**: No. I asked for Atreyu, the hunter.  
**Atreyu**: That’s me. I’m not a hunter yet.  
**Atreyu**: I was going to be, tomorrow.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): a growth mindset: “not yet” means still learning  
> _Atreyu says he is “not a hunter yet”._  
> _What does the little word “yet” tell us?_  
> - ✅ He is still learning, and he can get there. → Yes. “Not yet” means “not so far”. It leaves the door open. Everybody starts as a beginner.
