# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Atlas, page A26: The Wall of Uruk

## A26-1. The Wall

- `a26-01-s1`: Step into the page and walk the wall of Uruk. · _Touch the sunlit wall on the page, then follow the old brickmaker along the top._
- `a26-01-s2`: Find the copper tablet box at the foot of the wall and read the lapis tablet. · _The box is set into the wall’s foundation, by the stair. The brickmaker will show you._

#### `reader:a26-01-page` · [main](main.md#a26-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page so bright it warms your face. A great brick wall stands in the sun, with date palms and a river._  
_Your Nubbin puts both paws flat on the paper, as if to test whether it is hot. It is, a little._  
_The bricks are warm under your feet, and you are standing on the rampart of a city. Two carts could pass side by side up here._  
_Below, flat roofs run down to a river. An old woman with clay to her elbows sits on a stack of bricks, eating a date._  
**Old Brickmaker**: New, are you? Everyone is new once. Walk the wall with me. It is the best way to meet a city.  
**Old Brickmaker**: This is Uruk. It stood in the land between two rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates. Today that land is part of Iraq.  
**Old Brickmaker**: The Greeks later called it Mesopotamia, which means “between the rivers”. Rivers meant water, crops and boats.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, notice): Mesopotamia means “between the rivers”; Uruk stood in what is now Iraq  
> _(Find the two rivers on Nova’s map. The Tigris and the Euphrates both run south-east through Iraq to the sea.)_  
> _For grown-ups: Learned where Mesopotamia was: between the Tigris and the Euphrates, in what is now Iraq._


**Choices**

- **Ask her how you tell a good brick.**
    - **Old Brickmaker**: You knock it. A good brick rings, and a bad one says “thud” and lets you down when it rains.
- **Ask who built the wall.**
    - **Old Brickmaker**: The king says he did. The king says a lot of things. Come down and I will show you something better.

#### `reader:a26-01-page-done` · [main](main.md#a26-01-page-done)

_Your Nubbin knocks on the parapet with one paw, and listens with its head on one side. It seems satisfied._  
**Old Brickmaker**: That is how you test a brick. Some of these were baked in a kiln, a very hot oven, and some only dried in the sun.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, ask): sound is made of vibrations  
> _A knock makes the brick ring. What is a sound made of?_  
> - ✅ Tiny back-and-forth shakes, called vibrations → Yes. The knock shakes the brick, the brick shakes the air, and your ear feels the shaking as sound.
> - Light → Not quite. Light is something we see. Sound is shaking, passed along through the air.
> - Heat → Not quite. A knock does warm a brick a tiny bit, but the ringing is shaking, called vibration.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that sound is made of vibrations._

**Old Brickmaker**: Now down the stairs. Mind the third one. It has been mended since my grandmother’s day.  

#### `reader:a26-01-tablet` · [main](main.md#a26-01-tablet)

_At the foot of the stair, set into the oldest bricks, there is a box of copper, green with years. Its lock is a bronze bolt._  
**Old Brickmaker**: Slide the bolt. Go on. It is meant to be opened. That is the whole point of it.  
_Inside lies a tablet of lapis, deep blue and flecked with gold like a night sky. Little wedge-shaped marks run across it._  
_You cannot read them. Then, the longer you look, you can._  
**Old Brickmaker**: That is cuneiform. Scribes pressed a reed into wet clay, and every press left a wedge. This one was cut into stone, to last.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): cuneiform means wedge-shaped; scribes pressed a reed into wet clay  
> **Old Brickmaker**: The word “cuneiform” comes from the Latin for “wedge”. You can see the corner of the reed in every mark.  
> **Old Brickmaker**: Groups of wedges made signs, some for words and some for sounds. People wrote like this more than four thousand years ago.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that cuneiform is wedge-shaped writing, pressed into clay, and what the word means._

**Old Brickmaker**: The tablet begins with our city and its high wall. Then it is all about a king who saw everything, and what it cost him.  
_The wedges begin to move like ants in a line. The air smells of hot dust and river water._  

**Choices**

- **Read on.**
    - You read on. The wedges become words, and the words become a street, and you are standing in it.
- **Let your Nubbin turn the tablet over.**
    - Your Nubbin turns the tablet over with great care. On the back, the story keeps going, and so do you.

#### `reader:a26-01-tablet-done` · [main](main.md#a26-01-tablet-done)

_When you look up, the brickmaker has put the tablet back in its box and slid the bolt home._  
**Old Brickmaker**: It stays here. That is what it is for. Anyone can come and open the box and read it.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): weighing where a very old thing should be kept  
> _The brickmaker puts the tablet back instead of taking it. What do you think is the right place for something so old?_  
> - Where it was found, so people can come to it → That is a fair answer. It matches what the brickmaker believes: a thing stays part of its place.
> - In a museum, where it is kept safe → That is a fair answer. A safe place matters. The brickmaker thinks a thing matters more where it belongs.
> - Anywhere that lets lots of people see it → That is a fair answer. Sharing matters. The question is how to share without taking from the people it belongs to.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about where a very old and precious thing belongs, and how to share it fairly._

**Old Brickmaker**: Go on, then. I will be on the wall when you are done.  

#### `reader:a26-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a26-01-complete)

_The street is full of people, all of them tired. Somewhere above them, a drum is beating, and it does not stop._  
_It beats four times, rests, beats four times, rests, and beats four times again. Then it starts over._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): three equal groups of four  
> _The drum beats in three groups of four. How many beats in all, before it starts over?_  
> - ✅ 12 → Yes. Three groups of four: 4, 8, 12.
> - 7 → That is 3 + 4. Three groups of four means 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.
> - 34 → Careful: three groups of four is 4 + 4 + 4, which is 12.
> _For grown-ups: Used three equal groups of four to make 12._

_Your Nubbin takes hold of your trouser leg. You are in the story now, a stranger in Uruk, and the king is awake._  

### Glossary

- **rampart**: The wide top of a city wall, where people can walk.
- **kiln**: A very hot oven for baking clay until it is hard.
- **cuneiform**: Wedge-shaped writing, pressed into wet clay with a reed.
- **lapis**: A deep blue stone with tiny flecks of gold-coloured mineral.

## A26-2. The King Who Never Rests

- `a26-02-s1`: Watch the king’s games in the great square. · _Follow the drum to the great square, and find a place by the well._
- `a26-02-s2`: Go up to the temple with the people of Uruk. · _At dusk the crowd climbs the temple steps. Go with the woman from the square._

#### `reader:a26-02-games` · [main](main.md#a26-02-games)

_In the great square, young men are running races, and carrying stones, and running races again. Nobody looks as if they want to._  
_In the middle, taller than anyone, stands a man with a curled black beard and arms like roof beams._  
**Gilgamesh**: Again! Faster! Who is tired? Nobody is tired! Uruk does not get tired!  
_That is Gilgamesh, king of Uruk. Two parts of him are god, and one part is man, and you cannot tell which part is talking._  
**Woman of Uruk**: He has been at it since the new moon. Games all day, building all night. Nobody may go home.  
_A boy in the race stumbles and sits down in the dust. He does not get up._  

> **Lesson** (health, CASEL: Self-management, ask): a tired body needs water and rest  
> _The boy is exhausted. Which is the best help for a very tired body?_  
> - ✅ Water and a proper rest → Yes. Water and rest are what a tired body needs. Children your age need about nine to twelve hours of sleep a night.
> - One more race, to toughen him up → Not quite. Pushing a worn-out body harder only makes it more tired. It needs water and rest.
> - Ignoring it until he feels better → He might, in time. But water and rest help sooner, and they are kind.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a very tired body needs water and rest._


**Choices**

- **Take the boy some water from the well.**
    - You draw a bucket and carry the boy a cup. He drinks it all, and looks at you as if you had brought him the sea.
- **Sit down in the dust beside him.**
    - You sit down beside him. After a moment your Nubbin sits too, and so, quietly, does the boy’s mother.

#### `reader:a26-02-games-done` · [main](main.md#a26-02-games-done)

_The king sees none of it. He has already started another race._  
**Woman of Uruk**: Thank you. It is the first kind thing anyone has done in this square since the new moon.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): small kindnesses matter to someone who has had none  
> _The woman says it is the first kind thing since the new moon. What does that show about small kindnesses?_  
> - ✅ They can matter a lot to someone who is tired and worried → Yes. A cup of water is small, but to someone who has had nothing kind for weeks, it is a great deal.
> - They only matter if the king sees → Not quite. The king saw nothing, and still the kindness mattered. It mattered to the boy and his mother.
> _For grown-ups: Talked about how small kindnesses matter, even when nobody important notices._


#### `reader:a26-02-clay` · [main](main.md#a26-02-clay)

_At dusk the people climb the temple steps with lamps. They do not shout, because they are too tired to shout._  
**Woman of Uruk**: Gods of the city, he is too strong for us. Give him someone his own size. Someone to wear him out.  
_The lamps flicker, though there is no wind. And for a moment, high over the temple, you see something no one else seems to._  
_A great woman, made of starlight, washes her hands in the evening sky. She pinches off a little clay and rolls it between her palms._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-1, ask): clay is soft when wet and hard when dry  
> _Aruru shapes wet clay. Why is clay such a useful material for people who want to build and write?_  
> - ✅ It can be shaped while soft, then it dries hard and keeps its shape → Yes. Soft clay becomes a brick, a pot or a tablet, and when it dries or is baked, it stays that way.
> - It never changes → It does change! Clay is soft when wet and hard when it dries. That change is why it is so useful.
> - It dissolves in water, so it is easy to wash away → Wet clay goes soft and muddy, but dry or baked clay holds firm. That is why bricks last.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that clay can be shaped while wet and hardens when it dries._

_That is the goddess Aruru, who made people in the first place. She drops the clay, gently, far away in the wild._  
_Your Nubbin watches it fall all the way, with its mouth open._  

**Choices**

- **Tell the woman what you saw.**
    - **Woman of Uruk**: Clay? In the wild? Well. I asked for help. I did not say what shape.
- **Keep it to yourself for now.**
    - You keep it to yourself. The woman pats your arm, as if you had prayed hardest of anyone.

#### `reader:a26-02-clay-done` · [main](main.md#a26-02-clay-done)

_Far off, beyond the river and the fields, something new opens its eyes in the long grass._  
_The eyes open slowly, like a door in the morning, and the light comes in._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): recognising a simile  
> _“The eyes open like a door in the morning” is a simile. Which of these is also a simile?_  
> - ✅ “He ran like the wind.” → Yes. A simile compares two things using “like” or “as”.
> - “He was the wind.” → That compares too, but without “like” or “as”. That is a metaphor.
> - “He ran very fast.” → That is plain description. A simile needs a comparison with “like” or “as”.
> _For grown-ups: Told a simile from a metaphor and a plain sentence._


#### `reader:a26-02-complete` · [main](main.md#a26-02-complete)

_Down in the square, the drum keeps going. Gilgamesh is still awake, still building, still certain nothing can stop him._  
_Your Nubbin has turned to face the wild, where the clay fell. Its ears are up._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, ask): what a fair rule about work and rest would look like  
> _The king never lets his people rest. What would a fair rule about work and rest look like in a city?_  
> - Everyone works, and everyone gets time to rest → That is a fair answer. A rule that gives everyone rest, including the king, is hard to argue with.
> - The king decides, because he is the king → That is a fair answer to think about. But the people of Uruk cried out: when one person decides alone, people can get hurt.
> - The people vote on how long to work → That is a fair answer. Many places today let people have a say in rules that affect them.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what a fair rule for work and rest might be._


## A26-3. The Wild Man

- `a26-03-s1`: Go out to the water hole on the steppe. · _Leave Uruk by the river gate and follow the gazelle tracks to the water._
- `a26-03-s2`: Find out who set the pits and snares, and what happened to them. · _Walk the edge of the steppe and look for filled-in pits and torn snares._

#### `reader:a26-03-herd` · [main](main.md#a26-03-herd)

_Three days out from the wall, the fields give way to steppe: dry grass, thorn trees, and a sky that goes on for ever._  
_At the water hole, three gazelles are drinking with their knees bent. Four more come trotting up, and two wander off again._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): add and take away within 100  
> _Three gazelles are at the water. Four more arrive, and two leave._  
> _How many gazelles are at the water hole now?_  
> - ✅ 5 → Yes. 3 + 4 = 7, and 7 − 2 = 5.
> - 9 → That is 3 + 4 + 2. Two of them left, so take 2 away: 7 − 2 = 5.
> - 7 → That is the number after the four arrived. Two then left, so 7 − 2 = 5.
> _For grown-ups: Added and took away to find five gazelles._

_In among them, on all fours, drinks a man. He is hairy all over, like a goat, and has never worn a stitch._  
_He grazes with the gazelles and drinks with them, and he looks perfectly happy. This is Enkidu, though he does not know he has a name yet._  
_Your Nubbin trots straight past you, down to the water, and drinks beside the smallest gazelle._  
**Enkidu**: Hhh?  
_Enkidu lifts his dripping face and looks at your Nubbin. Then he goes back to drinking, as if it had always been there._  

**Choices**

- **Let your Nubbin stay with the herd.**
    - You let it stay. It lies down in the cool mud with the gazelles, more content than you have ever seen it.
- **Call your Nubbin back.**
    - You call. Your Nubbin looks at you, and flicks an ear, and stays exactly where it is.

#### `reader:a26-03-herd-done` · [main](main.md#a26-03-herd-done)

_You sit under a thorn tree and watch them. Your Nubbin is in the middle of the herd, nose to nose with a fawn._  
_Enkidu grooms a gazelle’s ear with great care. There is nothing wild about the way he does it._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS2-1, ask): animals in groups help each other survive  
> _Gazelles stay together in a herd. How does a herd help each gazelle survive?_  
> - ✅ Many eyes and ears notice danger sooner → Yes. When one gazelle spots a threat, the whole herd can run.
> - Herds make the grass grow faster → Not quite. The herd does not grow the grass. What it does is spot danger sooner.
> - Gazelles in a herd never get thirsty → Not quite. Every gazelle still needs water. A herd helps by watching for danger together.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that living in a group helps animals notice danger._

#### `reader:a26-03-pits` · [main](main.md#a26-03-pits)

_At the edge of the steppe there are pits dug to catch game. Every one of them has been filled in, with care, by hand._  
_The snares have been torn up and laid in a tidy pile. Somebody has been very busy on the herd’s behalf._  
_A man with a net over his shoulder comes over the rise. He stops dead at the pits, and goes pale._  
**Trapper**: Again! Every pit, every snare. Three days I worked on those.  
**Trapper**: It is a man. A wild man, strong as a star from heaven. He runs with them. He sets them free.  
**Trapper**: I cannot feed my family on empty nets. I am going home to my father. He will know what to do.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): seeing both sides when two people need different things  
> _The trapper needs food for his family. Enkidu is freeing the animals._  
> _Who do you think is in the right?_  
> - The trapper, because his family needs to eat → That is a fair answer. A hungry family is a real problem. Enkidu does not yet understand that.
> - Enkidu, because the animals are his friends → That is a fair answer. He is protecting those he cares about. He does not yet know what the nets are for.
> - Both, and they need to understand each other → That is a fair answer. Neither is wicked. They need what the other cannot see.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about a conflict where each side has a real need._


**Choices**

- **Tell him you are sorry about his work.**
    - **Trapper**: So am I. And I am sorry I am frightened of him. I do not think he means harm. He just does not know about nets.
- **Say the herd seemed happy, at least.**
    - **Trapper**: Happy! The gazelles are happy. I am the one with the empty pot.
    - Then he laughs, once, despite himself.

#### `reader:a26-03-pits-done` · [main](main.md#a26-03-pits-done)

_He runs off towards the river, net flapping. Behind you, at the water, the wild man is filling in another pit._  
_He pats the earth flat with both hands, very gently, as if tucking someone in._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): a character’s motive  
> _Why does Enkidu fill in the pits? Think about what he knows and what he cares about._  
> - ✅ He wants to keep his animal friends safe, though he does not understand why the trapper set them → Yes. His motive is care for his friends. He sees danger to them but does not yet understand the trapper’s side.
> - He wants to annoy the trapper → Not quite. He has never met the trapper. His motive is to keep the herd safe.
> - He likes digging and filling → Look at how gently he pats the earth. His motive is care for his friends.
> _For grown-ups: Explained a character’s motive from what he knows and cares about._

_By evening the trapper is far away on the road to his father, and then on the road to Uruk._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, notice): land near rivers was green; land away from them was dry steppe  
> _(Look at Nova’s map.) The land beside the rivers was green and could be farmed, because the rivers brought water._  
> _Further out, it turned to dry steppe, where herds roamed._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that land beside Mesopotamia’s rivers was fertile, while land further away was dry steppe._

_Your Nubbin is still with the herd. It does not look round when you say its name._  

### Glossary

- **snare**: A trap made of rope or net, set to catch an animal.

## A26-4. Shamhat

- `a26-04-s1`: Walk Shamhat out to the water hole with the trapper. · _Meet the trapper at Uruk’s river gate and carry Shamhat’s bundle._
- `a26-04-s2`: Sit at the water hole with Shamhat until Enkidu understands her. · _Sit by the water. Do not chase. It takes days._
- `a26-04-s3`: Coax your Nubbin back from the herd. · _The gazelles are skittish now. Go slowly, and let your Nubbin come to you._

#### `reader:a26-04-road` · [main](main.md#a26-04-road)

_The trapper comes back from Uruk with a woman from the city. She walks quickly, and carries her own bundle until you take it._  
**Shamhat**: I am Shamhat. The king sent me. He says the wild man needs someone to talk to. I think he is right, for once.  
**Trapper**: He does not talk. He grunts. He drinks out of the pond on his knees.  
**Shamhat**: So did you, once. Somebody sat with you until you did not.  
_The bundle smells of bread and sweet oil. In it are twelve dates, a flat loaf and a clean robe folded small._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): sharing equally: division  
> _Twelve dates shared equally between three people. How many dates does each person get?_  
> - ✅ 4 → Yes. 12 ÷ 3 = 4, because 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.
> - 3 → Check it: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9, which is too few. Each person gets 4, because 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.
> - 6 → Check it: 6 + 6 + 6 = 18, which is too many. Each person gets 4.
> _For grown-ups: Divided twelve dates equally among three people._

**Shamhat**: I will share the dates between me, the trapper and Enkidu, the same number each. Most people want to tame him. I want to be the first person he understands.  

**Choices**

- **Ask her how she will do it.**
    - **Shamhat**: Patience. I will sit where he can see me, and talk, and not need anything. Then one day he will answer.
- **Tell her your Nubbin ran off with the herd.**
    - **Shamhat**: Then we have the same job, you and I. Let us see who finishes first.

#### `reader:a26-04-sit` · [main](main.md#a26-04-sit)

_Shamhat sits on a flat rock by the water hole, and you sit a little way off. The trapper hides in a bush and is no help._  
_The first day, Enkidu watches her from behind a gazelle. The second day, he comes and sniffs her bundle._  
**Shamhat**: That is bread. You do not know bread yet. You will. This is my hand. That is yours.  
_By the fourth day he sits across the rock from her, the way your Nubbin sits across from you on the hill at home._  
_She talks about the city, and the river, and the man who never sleeps. Enkidu listens with his whole face._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): why waiting patiently helps someone feel safe  
> _Shamhat never chases Enkidu. She sits, talks and waits._  
> _Why do you think that works better than chasing?_  
> - He gets to come close when he feels safe → That is a fair answer. When you do not push, the other person can choose to come closer.
> - She shows she wants nothing from him → That is a fair answer. Someone who asks for nothing is easier to trust.
> - It does not always work, but here it did → That is a fair answer. Waiting takes time, and it is not a guarantee. It gave Enkidu room to choose.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why patient waiting can build trust._

**Enkidu**: Hand.  
**Shamhat**: Hand. Yes. And what do you call yourself?  
**Enkidu**: …Enkidu.  

#### `reader:a26-04-sit-done` · [main](main.md#a26-04-sit-done)

_He says it again, louder, as if trying the word for size. Then he laughs, a big surprised human laugh._  
**Shamhat**: There you are. I knew there was someone in there.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): what an action shows about a character  
> _Shamhat asks Enkidu what he calls himself, instead of giving him a name. What does that show about her?_  
> - ✅ She respects him and lets him choose his own name → Yes. Her question treats Enkidu as a person with a say in who he is.
> - She does not know his name → She might not. But the way she asks shows respect.
> - She is in a hurry → She has waited days. The question shows patience and respect.
> _For grown-ups: Read a character’s respect from the way she asks a question._


#### `reader:a26-04-coax` · [main](main.md#a26-04-coax)

_Enkidu walks back down to the gazelles, the way he has every evening of his life._  
_They lift their heads, and look at him, and run, every one. They do not know him any more._  
**Enkidu**: Wait! It is me!  
_He tries to run after them, and cannot keep up. His legs are a man’s now._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, ask): animals use their senses to know each other

- `a26-18-s1`: Walk the wall of Uruk with Gilgamesh and Ur-Shanabi. · _Climb the wall stair. Mind the third step._
- `a26-18-s2`: Go down to the foot of the wall, where the story started. · _The old brickmaker is waiting by the copper tablet box._

#### `reader:a26-18-wall` · [main](main.md#a26-18-wall)

_You come home across the plain at evening. The wall of Uruk is gold in the low sun, and the river runs beside it._  
_Gilgamesh stops. He looks at the wall for a long while, as if he had never properly seen it._  
**Ur-Shanabi**: So that is your city. It is big.  
**Gilgamesh**: It is. Come up. I will show you.  
_You climb the wall stair together. Your Nubbin knows the way, and waits on the third step for you, which wobbles._  
_On top, the wind is warm. The whole city is laid out below, roofs and orchards and smoke from supper fires._  
**Gilgamesh**: Walk the top of this wall, Ur-Shanabi. Look at how it stands, and at its bricks. See how they were baked hard in the fire.  
**Gilgamesh**: Town, orchards and clay-pits, a square mile of each. And half a square mile for Ishtar’s temple. That is what I made.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.C.7, ask): area of a rectangle  
> _One square mile is a square one mile long on every side. How many square miles is an orchard that is 2 miles by 3 miles?_  
> - ✅ 6 square miles → Yes. Area is length times width: 2 × 3 = 6.
> - 5 square miles → That is 2 + 3, which is half the distance round (the perimeter). Area is 2 × 3 = 6.
> - 1 square mile → One square mile is a 1 by 1 square. A 2 by 3 orchard is six of them.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied length and width to find an area of 6 square miles._


**Choices**

    - You knock on a brick, and it rings. Gilgamesh hears it, and for the first time in a long time, he smiles.
- **Look at the city with them.**
    - Somebody is calling children in for supper, and somebody is singing. It all goes on, and on.

#### `reader:a26-18-wall-done` · [main](main.md#a26-18-wall-done)

**Gilgamesh**: I went to the end of the world. And here it is. It was here the whole time.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.2, ask): theme: what Gilgamesh learns  
> _“I went to the end of the world. And here it is.”_  
> _“It was here the whole time.” What idea is Gilgamesh expressing?_  
> - ✅ What we search for far away can be close to home → Yes. That is a theme found in stories all over the world: the treasure was near all along.
> - The world has no end → That is not what he says. He means the thing he wanted was here at home.
> - Walls are better than rivers → He is looking at his own city, and seeing what it is worth.
> _For grown-ups: Identified a theme: what we seek far away can be close to home._


#### `reader:a26-18-foot` · [main](main.md#a26-18-foot)

_At the foot of the wall, by the copper box, the old brickmaker is sitting on her stack of bricks, eating a date._  
**Old Brickmaker**: There you are. Long story, was it? They always are, his.  
_Below the wall, on the patch of rough grass by the river, a gazelle is grazing. It lifts its head, and looks at you, and does not run._  
**Gilgamesh**: He asked you to look after them. You did.  
**Old Brickmaker**: Now then. Every brick in this wall has someone’s hand in it. Want yours in?  
_She lays a fresh clay brick in front of you. You press your hand into it, and your Nubbin, after some thought, presses a paw._  
**Old Brickmaker**: Kiln-fired, this one. Sun-dried bricks crumble in the rain. Kiln-fired, they last for thousands of years.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-ESS2-1, ask): weathering: why kiln-fired brick lasts  
> _Sun-dried bricks crumble in the rain, but kiln-fired bricks last for thousands of years. Why?_  
> - ✅ Firing changes the clay for good, so water cannot soften it → Yes. Baking clay in a kiln changes it so it will not turn back to mud. Rain slowly wears sun-dried brick away: that is called weathering.
> - Kiln bricks are painted to keep the rain off → There is no paint. The firing itself changes the clay.
> - Sun-dried bricks are made of different mud → The mud is the same. The difference is the fire in the kiln.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that firing changes clay for good, so kiln-fired bricks resist weathering far longer than sun-dried bricks._


**Choices**

    - **Old Brickmaker**: It is. Always will be, if people leave it be. That one, nobody takes.
- **Ask if she will put her own mark on it too.**
    - **Old Brickmaker**: Me? No. I am on all the others.

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, ask): fairness about things that belong to a people  
> _Many tablets were taken from the places they belonged, and some have come home. What is a fair way to treat a people’s heritage?_  
> - ✅ Protect it, and return it to where it belongs when we can → Yes. Heritage belongs to the people it comes from, and to the whole world to learn from, but taking it without asking is unfair.
> - Keep it, because it is safer in a faraway museum → Safety matters, but that does not make taking it fair. Many countries and museums now work to return what was taken.
> - Sell it to the highest bidder → Selling a people’s treasures would take them away from the people they belong to. That is why many countries have laws against it.
> _For grown-ups: Talked about treating a people’s heritage fairly: protect it and return it where possible._
