## A28-1. We Don’t Really Mean It

#### `reader:a28-01-courtyard` · [main](main.md#a28-01-courtyard)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page the colour of a warm night: a courtyard, a carved stool, and a ring of faces round a fire._  
_You touch the stool. The paper is warm, and then the air is, and you are standing at the edge of the ring._  
_This is an Asante village in Ghana. Crickets are singing, and somewhere a pot is being scraped._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.3-5, ask): finding Ghana on a map of Africa  
> _Nova’s atlas says this village is in Ghana. Where is Ghana?_  
> - ✅ On the west coast of Africa, beside the Gulf of Guinea. → Yes. Ghana lies in West Africa, with a warm coast on the Gulf of Guinea. The Asante, who live here, are part of the Akan.
> - In the far north of Europe. → Not quite. Europe is a different continent, and a long way north. Ghana is in West Africa, on the coast of the Gulf of Guinea.
> - On an island in the Pacific Ocean. → The Pacific is on the other side of the world. Ghana is a country in West Africa, beside the Atlantic Ocean.
> _For grown-ups: Located Ghana in West Africa, and met the Asante and the Akan._

_Children sit on mats round the fire, elbowing for the front. Grown-ups lean in the doorways with their arms folded._  
**The Children**: Nana’s coming! Nana!  
_An old woman crosses the courtyard, slowly, because she can. She has a cloth over one shoulder and all the time in the world._  
_Your Nubbin looks at the one empty mat, right at the front, and then at you._  

**Choices**

- **Sit on the front mat.**
    - A small girl shuffles over to make room, without once taking her eyes off the stool.
- **Sit at the back, by a doorway.**
    - A man in the doorway nods to you, as if latecomers are expected and nearly forgiven.
    - Your Nubbin goes to the front anyway, and sits on the end of the girl’s mat.

#### `reader:a28-01-courtyard-done` · [main](main.md#a28-01-courtyard-done)

_The old woman lowers herself on to the stool. The fire pops._  
_Everybody goes quiet, even the grown-ups, even the man still scraping the pot._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): listening as a way of showing respect  
> _Why does everybody, even the grown-ups, go quiet when the storyteller sits down?_  
> - ✅ Listening closely shows respect for the teller and the tale. → Yes. A story is a gift from the teller, and listening well is how you say thank you.
> - They are all too tired to talk. → Perhaps a few are tired. But the quiet is a choice. People go quiet because listening closely shows respect.
> - They are afraid of her. → Nobody looks afraid. They look eager. The quiet is respect: it makes room for the story.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why listening quietly is a way to show respect._


#### `reader:a28-01-stool` · [main](main.md#a28-01-stool)

**Nana Ama**: Before I begin, you must all understand something.  
**Nana Ama**: None of what I am about to tell you is really true. Not truly.  
**Nana Ama**: Not at all.  
_She says it twice, the way you knock twice on a door you fully intend to open._  
_At once the children shout back at her, all together, loud enough to lift the moths off the wall._  
**The Children**: Tell it! Tell it anyway!  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): why a storyteller might open this way: a motive  
> _The storyteller says her tale is not true, and the children shout, “Tell it anyway!” Why do you think she begins like this?_  
> - It is a game between teller and listeners: everybody knows it is a story. → That is a fair answer. A shared game makes the listeners part of the telling. Nana would say the children are doing half the work.
> - It reminds listeners that a tale is not news, so nobody takes it too literally. → That is a fair answer. A story can be true in what it means without being true in fact.
> - It is polite, like asking permission to begin. → That is a fair answer. The shout back is their permission. Good manners and good storytelling often travel together.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why a storyteller might open a tale by saying it is not true, and why listeners answer._

_The girl beside you nudges you: you are meant to shout too. Everybody is._  

**Choices**

- **Shout it with them.**
    - **You**: Tell it anyway!
    - Your Nubbin shouts too. It comes out as a squeak, and the old woman points at it, delighted.
- **Mouth it, a bit shyly.**
    - The girl shouts it again, for both of you. Your Nubbin bounces in time, which seems to count.

#### `reader:a28-01-stool-done` · [main](main.md#a28-01-stool-done)

**Nana Ama**: Good. Now that we all agree none of it happened, I can tell you exactly how it happened.  
_The children laugh. You laugh too, a beat behind them._  
_You count six mats round the fire, with four children squeezed on to each._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 6 groups of 4  
> _Six mats, with four children on each. How many children are sitting round the fire?_  
> - ✅ 24 → Yes. Six groups of four is 6 × 4 = 24. You can check by adding: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24.
> - 10 → That is 6 + 4. We need six groups of four, which is multiplication. Count by fours: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24. So 24 children.
> - 20 → Close. That is only five mats. Count by fours: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20… One more mat makes 24.
> _For grown-ups: Found the total of six equal groups of four by multiplying and by skip counting._


#### `reader:a28-01-fall` · [main](main.md#a28-01-fall)

**Nana Ama**: This is a story about Kwaku Ananse, the spider. Who else would it be about?  
**The Children**: Ananse!  
**Nana Ama**: Once there were no stories on the earth at all. Not one.  
**Nana Ama**: Imagine that.  
_You try. It is like trying to imagine a room with no corners._  
**Nana Ama**: Every story there was belonged to the Sky-god, Nyame, and he kept them by him.  
_Her voice goes low, and the courtyard goes with it. Under her words the crickets chirp on, and the stars come down close._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, ask): sound is made by vibration  
> _A cricket makes its chirp by rubbing its wings together. What is really making the sound?_  
> - ✅ Parts of the wings vibrate, and the shaking travels through the air. → Yes. Rubbing makes the wing parts vibrate, which means shake very fast. The shaking passes through the air to our ears, and we hear it as sound.
> - Air whistling through the cricket’s throat. → Crickets do not sing from the throat. They use their wings. The wings vibrate, and that shaking is the sound.
> - Its legs tapping on the ground. → Tapping makes a different sound. A cricket’s song comes from its wings. They rub together and vibrate, and the vibration is what we hear.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that crickets make sound by rubbing their wings, which vibrate, and that vibrations are sound._

_Your Nubbin grips your sleeve. Then the mat under you is red earth, there are trees, and it is morning._  
_Something is sitting on a fallen log beside you, swinging a great many legs._  

#### `reader:a28-01-fall-done` · [main](main.md#a28-01-fall-done)

**Kwaku Ananse**: You have two arms. And a bag.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Excellent.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: A spider has eight legs and nowhere at all to put a python.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: And eight legs means eight socks. My wife Aso has eight legs too.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Ruinous.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 2 spiders with 8 legs each  
> _Ananse needs a sock for every leg, and so does Aso. How many socks do the two of them need?_  
> - ✅ 16 → Yes. Two spiders with eight legs each is 2 × 8 = 16 socks.
> - 10 → That is 8 + 2. Each spider has eight legs, and there are two spiders. 8 + 8 = 16 socks.
> - 8 → That is only Ananse’s socks. Aso has eight legs too. 8 + 8 = 16 socks.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied two equal groups of eight to find how many socks two spiders need._

_Your Nubbin reaches for a silver thread on the log, and sticks to it._  
_The spider unpicks it without looking, like somebody who has done this many times before._  

#### `reader:a28-01-complete` · [main](main.md#a28-01-complete)

**Kwaku Ananse**: I am Kwaku Ananse. You will carry things for me, and in return you will see the cleverest thing ever done.  
_Behind you, very faint, you can still hear the fire, and the old woman’s voice going on._  
**Kwaku Ananse**: That is her, telling us. Do not worry about it.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: She gets me right. Mostly right.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Come. We are going to see the Sky-god.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Walk tall. Not too tall.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: He is taller.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): describing a character from what he says and does  
> _Ananse has said a lot about himself already. Which words describe him best so far?_  
> - ✅ Clever, boastful and a little sly. → Yes. He boasts about his cleverness, and he is already planning a trick. Good readers notice what a character says and does, not just what he claims.
> - Shy, honest and tired. → Look at what he says: “the cleverest thing ever done”. That is not shy. He is clever, boastful and a little sly.
> - Slow, gentle and sleepy. → Ananse is quick and full of plans. Nothing sleepy about him. Clever, boastful and a little sly fits better.
> _For grown-ups: Described a character’s traits using evidence from his words and actions._

_Your Nubbin falls in behind him, stepping where he steps, which is hard work with eight legs to copy._

#### `reader:a28-02-climb` · [main](main.md#a28-02-climb)

_Ananse spins a thread straight up into the morning and climbs it, and you climb after him. Nobody explains how._  
_Your Nubbin rides on your shoulders, sticks to your hair, and stays stuck all the way up._  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Don’t look down. Down is where all the people who didn’t think of this are.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, ask): how spiders make silk  
> _Real spiders spin silk too. Where on a spider’s body does the silk come from?_  
> - ✅ Tiny spouts called spinnerets at the back, where liquid silk hardens into thread. → Yes. Silk is made inside the spider as a liquid. It leaves through the spinnerets and turns into a strong thread as it is pulled out.
> - From its mouth, like a long string of spit. → Some creatures do make threads from their mouths, but not spiders. A spider’s silk comes from spinnerets at the back of its body.
> - From the leaves it eats. → A spider does not make silk out of leaves. Most spiders eat insects. Its body makes the silk, and it comes out of the spinnerets.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that spiders make silk inside their bodies and spin it out of spinnerets._

_At the top is a court of white cloud._  
_Elders sit on carved stools, and at the far end is a light too bright to look at._  
_Rich men are leaving as you arrive, their servants carrying out gifts that were all refused._  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Kings. Big towns.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: They came to buy the Sky-god’s stories. Couldn’t pay.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: I, on the other hand, have no money at all. It is a great advantage.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Nobody watches a poor spider.  

**Choices**

- **Ask what he means to pay with, then.**
    - **Kwaku Ananse**: With whatever he asks for. That is the trick.
    - **Kwaku Ananse**: Agree first. Work out how afterwards.
- **Unstick your Nubbin from your hair first.**
    - It takes a while. An elder on a stool watches the whole business with deep interest, and nods when it is done.

#### `reader:a28-02-climb-done` · [main](main.md#a28-02-climb-done)

_Ananse straightens his hat, takes three steps forward, and bows so low that the brim touches the cloud._  
_The light at the end of the court is Nyame, the Sky-god, the Supreme Being of the Akan, whom many people still honour today._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): respect for someone with great age or wisdom  
> _Why does Ananse bow so low at the Sky-god’s court?_  
> - ✅ To show deep respect for someone great and wise. → Yes. Bowing is a way of showing respect without a word. In many places, people honour elders and leaders with gestures like this.
> - Because he dropped something. → That would be funny, but Ananse is bowing on purpose. He bows to show respect to the Sky-god.
> - To hide his face from the elders. → He is not hiding. A bow is a greeting that says, “I honour you.”
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how a bow shows respect for a leader or an elder._


#### `reader:a28-02-price` · [main](main.md#a28-02-price)

**Nyame**: Kwaku Ananse. You have come a long way up, for a spider.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Great one. I have come to buy your stories.  
_A murmur goes round the elders, who sit in three rows of four on their carved stools. One of them stops chewing._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 3 rows of 4  
> _The elders sit in three rows of four stools. How many elders are in the court?_  
> - ✅ 12 → Yes. Three rows of four is 3 × 4 = 12 elders.
> - 7 → That is 3 + 4. There are three rows, and four elders in each row. 3 × 4 = 12.
> - 34 → That is the numbers 3 and 4 side by side. We need three groups of four. 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied the rows by the stools in each row to find how many elders sit in the court._

**Nyame**: Great towns have come to buy them. Kings have come.  
**Nyame**: They have all gone home again with their gold.  
**Nyame**: What makes you think you can pay what they could not?

> - Kofi → Kofi is for Friday, one day too early. A Saturday boy is Kwame.
> - Kwesi → Kwesi is for Sunday, one day too late. A Saturday boy is Kwame.

#### `reader:a28-02-road-done` · [main](main.md#a28-02-road-done)

_He gets up, dusts off his hat, and points into the trees with three legs at once._  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Python first. He is the vainest.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Vain ones are easy.  
_A python is a snake with no venom. It catches its food and squeezes it, and it finds its way partly by flicking its tongue._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, ask): a snake’s tongue collects scent  
> _A python flicks its tongue in and out. What is the tongue doing?_  
> - ✅ Collecting tiny scent particles from the air, so it can smell. → Yes. The tongue picks up scent from the air and ground. The snake then presses it to special organs in its mouth that sense the smells.
> - Cooling itself down, like a dog panting. → Snakes do not cool themselves that way. They lie in shade instead. Flicking the tongue is how they collect scents.
> - Making a hissing noise. → A hiss comes from breathing out, not from the tongue. The tongue flicks to collect scents.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a snake flicks its tongue to collect scent, one of its senses._

_The forest is enormous, green and noisy. Somewhere in it is a python, and somewhere a fairy._  
**Kwaku Ananse**: One thing at a time. That is how you eat a whole yam.

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): making promises: think before you agree  
> _Ananse said yes first and worked out how afterwards. Is that a good way to make a promise?_  
> - No. Only promise what you know you can do. → That is a fair answer. A promise is something other people rely on. Ananse promised Nyame his own mother, and never asked her first. She has views on that.
> - Sometimes. A big promise can push you to find a way. → That is a fair answer. Some great things only get done because someone said yes first. The risk is who gets hurt if you cannot do it.
> - It depends who gets hurt if you fail. → That is a fair answer. A promise that risks only yourself is different from one that risks others. Notice how many creatures Ananse’s promise puts at risk.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about when it is wise to make a big promise, and who is affected if it is broken._

## A28-3. Onini the Python

- `a28-03-s1`: Find a long palm branch and some creeper for Ananse. · _Look along the forest floor for a fallen branch; the creeper is where your Nubbin walks into it._
- `a28-03-s2`: Watch Ananse and Aso’s argument from close by, with a straight face. · _Stand by the branch under the python’s tree._

#### `reader:a28-03-branch` · [main](main.md#a28-03-branch)

**Kwaku Ananse**: We need a palm branch. A long one.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: And creeper, for tying. Plenty of creeper.  
_You find a fallen palm branch longer than a canoe. Your Nubbin finds the creeper by walking into it._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): choosing a material for its properties  
> _Ananse wants creeper for tying, not a plank. Why is creeper better?_  
> - ✅ It is flexible and strong, so it bends round things and holds a knot. → Yes. Materials are chosen for their properties. Creeper is bendy and strong. A plank is strong but stiff.
> - It is heavy, so it holds things down. → Weight is not what matters for tying. Creeper is quite light. It is useful because it is flexible and strong.
> - It is shiny. → Shininess does not help with tying. Creeper works because it is flexible and strong.
> _For grown-ups: Chose a material for tying by comparing flexible and stiff properties._

**Kwaku Ananse**: Now. My wife Aso is coming.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: When she gets here we will have a terrible argument, and you will look shocked.  
_A spider in a headcloth comes along the path with a basket, already looking tired of him._  
**Aso**: What are you doing with that branch, Kwaku?  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Not now, Aso. Argue with me.  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Loudly. By the python’s tree.  
**Aso**: I always argue with you loudly. I don’t need a reason.  

#### `reader:a28-03-branch-done` · [main](main.md#a28-03-branch-done)

_The three of you drag the branch to a great tree with roots like knuckles._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): recognising a simile  
> _The tree has “roots like knuckles”. What kind of language is that?_  
> - ✅ A simile: it compares two things using “like”. → Yes. A simile says one thing is like another, to help you picture it. Knuckles are bony and knobbly, so you can see the roots.
> - A rhyme. → Nothing here rhymes. It is a comparison. Using “like” to compare two things is a simile.
> - A fact about trees. → Roots are not really knuckles. It is a picture in words. Comparing two things with “like” is a simile.
> _For grown-ups: Recognised a simile (roots like knuckles) as a comparison using “like”._

_The roots are fat, brown and gnarled, and they grip the ground tightly._  
_Something long is asleep up in the branches, and you can hear it breathing._  

#### `reader:a28-03-argue` · [main](main.md#a28-03-argue)

**Kwaku Ananse**: He IS longer than this branch!  
**Aso**: He is NOT! He is shorter!  
**Aso**: Anybody can see it!  
**Kwaku Ananse**: Longer!  
**Aso**: Shorter! Ask anybody you like!  
_They are both very good at this. You start to suspect they have had a great deal of practice._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): settling a disagreement with evidence, not volume  
> _Ananse and Aso shout at each other, and neither gives way. What would settle it fairly?_
