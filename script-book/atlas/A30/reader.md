#### `reader:a30-01-page` · [main](main.md#a30-01-page)

_Nova’s atlas falls open at a page of brown ink: a steep-roofed synagogue, a lane of small houses, a river._  
**Ori**: That is Prague, in the Czech lands. The wavy line is the Vltava, the river that runs through the city.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, ask): reading a river on a map  
> **Ori**: Maps use signs. How is a river like the Vltava usually drawn on a map?  
> - ✅ As a wavy blue line → Yes. Rivers are usually wavy blue lines, and they often join the sea or another river.
> - As a straight red line → Red lines are often roads or borders. A river is wavy, and blue like water.
> - As a black square → Small squares often mean buildings. The river is the long, wavy blue line.
> _For grown-ups: Read a map: a river is drawn as a wavy blue line. Placed Prague, in the Czech lands, beside the Vltava._

**Ori**: This page is a legend. Rabbi Judah Loew was a real teacher, from more than four hundred years ago. The clay man came into stories long after.  
_The paper goes cool and gritty under your hand, like a doorstep. Then it is a doorstep, and you are sitting on it._  
_A narrow lane, wet cobbles, the smell of bread. A man with a ring of keys looks down at you both._  
**Avram**: You’ll be the lodger. Good. I’m Avram. I look after the synagogue, which mostly means I look after its keys.  
_Your Nubbin holds up both arms, to show that it will fit anywhere at all._  

**Choices**

> - Being given food and a bed → That’s a fair answer. Being looked after tells you that you are wanted.
> - Being asked your name → That’s a fair answer. Being noticed as a person, not just a guest, matters a great deal.

#### `reader:a30-01-water` · [main](main.md#a30-01-water)

_Rivka is about nine, and walks backwards the whole way so she can talk to you._  
**Rivka**: That’s the study house. That’s the bathhouse. That’s Hannah’s bakery. Don’t look at it, you’ll want everything.  
_Your Nubbin looks at it._  
**Rivka**: Nobody goes in the attic. It’s where the worn-out books go. You don’t throw away a holy book; you put it to rest.  
_At the well two women are talking low, the way people talk about weather that hasn’t arrived yet._  
**Rivka**: Mama says not to listen at wells. Mama listens at wells all the time.  
_Dvora needs thirty litres of water for the big pot. Your buckets hold six litres each, and you carry two._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.D.8, ask): a two-step word problem with litres  
> _You carry two full buckets of six litres. Dvora needs thirty litres._  
> _How many more bucketfuls?_  
> - ✅ 3 → Yes. Two buckets is 12 litres, and 30 − 12 = 18. Then 18 ÷ 6 = 3 more bucketfuls.
> - 4 → Let’s check: 12 litres carried, so 18 to go. 18 ÷ 6 = 3 bucketfuls, not 4.
> - 5 → 12 litres carried, 18 to go. Each trip is 6 litres, so 3 trips.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a two-step problem: 30 − (2 × 6) = 18, then 18 ÷ 6 = 3._

_Your Nubbin carries a cup, spills about half of it, and is very proud of the half._  

#### `reader:a30-01-water-done` · [main](main.md#a30-01-water-done)

**Dvora**: Good. Now you’re a guest. Rivka, take them to Hannah’s before she shuts, and don’t eat the change.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): the meaning of lodger  
> _Avram called you the lodger. From the way Dvora treats you, what is a lodger?_  
> - ✅ Someone who stays in another family’s house → Yes. A lodger lodges, or stays, in someone else’s home, often paying or helping in return.
> - Someone who sells water → A lodger doesn’t sell anything. To lodge means to stay in someone else’s home.
> - Someone who owns the house → The owner is the householder. A lodger is a guest who stays.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the meaning of the word lodger from context._

#### `reader:a30-01-bakery` · [main](main.md#a30-01-bakery)

**Hannah**: Is this yours? It’s been there since you came round the corner. It hasn’t moved.  
_Hannah is flour to the elbows. Behind her, plaited loaves stand in rows, shining, waiting for Friday._  
**Hannah**: Those are for the Sabbath. Nobody touches those. This, on the other hand, is a crust I burnt.  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-4, ask): some changes cannot be undone  
> _Hannah’s soft dough goes into the hot oven and comes out as bread. Can baked bread be turned back into dough?_  
> - ✅ No, the heat changed it for good → Right. Some changes, like baking, can’t be undone. Others, like melting butter, can.
> - Yes, if you add water → Water won’t help. The heat has changed the dough into something new.
> - Yes, if you let it cool → Cooling won’t help either. Bread stays bread.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that baking is a change that cannot be reversed._

_She holds it out. Your Nubbin takes it in both paws as if it were a medal._  
**Rivka**: She burns one every day. On purpose. Everyone knows.

## A30-2. The Rabbi

- `a30-02-s1`: Go to the study house with Avram and sit at the back. · _The bench by the door. Sit still, and listen._
- `a30-02-s2`: Answer the rabbi’s question, if you can. · _He is looking at the back bench. There is no wrong answer._

#### `reader:a30-02-study` · [main](main.md#a30-02-study)

_The study house is one long room full of books and noise. Students sit in pairs, arguing over a page, both talking at once._  
**Avram**: It’s meant to be loud. A quiet study house is one where nobody’s thinking.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, ask): how people studied in the past, compared with today  
> _Students here study in pairs and argue over a page. Why might that be a good way to learn?_  
> - ✅ Your partner spots things you missed → Yes. Two minds catch more than one, and explaining your idea helps you understand it.
> - It keeps the room quiet → The room is anything but quiet! Arguing in pairs is how they test their ideas.
> - There are no books → There are books everywhere. The pairs are for thinking, not for sharing.
> _For grown-ups: Compared learning in pairs in a study house long ago with learning at school today._

**Avram**: Sit at the back. That is Rabbi Judah Loew. People call him the Maharal, from the first letters of “our teacher, the rabbi Loew”.  
_At the front sits an old man with a plain grey beard and ink on his fingers. The room goes quiet for him without being asked._  
**Rabbi Loew**: I’m going to tell you a story from the Talmud. Rava made a man, and sent him to Rabbi Zeira.  
**Rabbi Loew**: Rabbi Zeira spoke to him, and the man did not answer.  
**Rabbi Loew**: So Rabbi Zeira said to him: then go back to your dust.  
_Your Nubbin, on the bench beside you, has gone very still._  

#### `reader:a30-02-study-done` · [main](main.md#a30-02-study-done)

_The rabbi lets the story sit in the room a while, the way you let bread rest before you cut it._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): recognising a simile  
> _That line compares a story with bread. Which of these is a simile, a comparison using “as” or “like”?_  
> - ✅ The room was as quiet as snow → Yes. “As quiet as snow” compares two things using “as”. That is a simile.
> - The room was a snowfield → That compares without “as” or “like”. It is a metaphor, a close cousin of the simile.
> - The room was silent → That says it plainly, without any comparison.
> _For grown-ups: Told a simile from a metaphor and a plain statement._


#### `reader:a30-02-question` · [main](main.md#a30-02-question)

**Rabbi Loew**: Now. What is a person, if not that? Someone tell me.  
**A Student**: Someone who answers, Rabbi.  
**Another Student**: Someone who can be spoken to. Which is not quite the same.  
**Rabbi Loew**: There’s a psalm in the Bible, a kind of prayer, that says: before I was even made, you saw me.  
**Rabbi Loew**: The Hebrew word there is golmi, “my golem”: me, a lump not yet shaped. So every one of us was clay once, and somebody saw it.  
**Rabbi Loew**: What makes the difference?  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): what makes a person a person  
> _The rabbi asks what makes a person different from a clay figure. What do you think?_  
> - Having feelings → That’s a fair answer. Something that can be glad or frightened has an inside, not just an outside.
> - Being able to choose → That’s a fair answer. A person can say no, or change their mind. A clay man only waits.
> - Being loved by other people → That’s a fair answer. We become who we are among people who know us.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what makes a person different from a clay figure. No single right answer._

_He is looking past the students, at the back bench. At you._  

**Choices**

- **“Someone who can answer.”**
    - **Rabbi Loew**: The same as the student said, but braver, from the back of the room. Good.
- **“Someone who can ask.”**
    - **Rabbi Loew**: Then this is a room full of people, and I have been worrying for nothing.
- **Look at your Nubbin, who can’t say a word.**
    - **Rabbi Loew**: Yes. It doesn’t speak. And it answered me just now: it looked up when I said clay.
    - **Rabbi Loew**: Answering is more than words. Remember that. I may need reminding.

> - He is joking and means nothing → There is a joke in it, but it also tells us what he cares about.

## A30-3. Fear

- `a30-03-s1`: Find out why Avram has come home early. · _He is in the courtyard, sitting on the woodpile, which he never does._
- `a30-03-s2`: Get through the night. · _Choose where to be. Nothing will happen to you. It is only a long night._

#### `reader:a30-03-rumour` · [main](main.md#a30-03-rumour)

_Avram comes home at noon, which he never does, and sits on the woodpile, which he never does either._  
**Avram**: There’s a story going round outside the walls. About us. It isn’t true.  
**Avram**: I won’t tell you what it is. Saying it again is how it travels.  
**Dvora**: It’s been told before. It’s always been a lie. It always finds people who want it.  
_Dvora puts her hand on his shoulder, and leaves it there._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): why untrue stories travel  
> _Avram won’t repeat the lie, because repeating is how it travels. Why do you think untrue stories spread so easily?_  
> - People like exciting news → That’s a fair answer. A thrilling story gets passed on before anyone checks it.
> - People are frightened and want someone to blame → That’s a fair answer. Fear looks for a reason, and a lie can offer a false one.
> - Nobody stops to check → That’s a fair answer. A question like “how do we know?” can stop a lie in its tracks.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why untrue stories spread, and how asking “how do we know?” can slow them. No single right answer._

**Avram**: There were men at the gate last night, talking. Only talking. Tonight there may be more of them.  
**Rivka**: Will they come in?  
**Dvora**: The gates are shut at night. And there are a great many of us, and we’re all very good at shouting.  

#### `reader:a30-03-rumour-done` · [main](main.md#a30-03-rumour-done)

**Avram**: Bar the shutters at dusk. That’s all. Then we sleep, if we can.  
_There are four shutters on the house, and each one has two bars, one high and one low._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): equal groups: 4 × 2  
> _Four shutters, two bars on each. How many bars does Avram bar in all?_  
> - ✅ 8 → Yes. Four groups of two is 4 × 2 = 8.
> - 6 → Try it as four twos: 2, 4, 6, 8. Eight bars.
> - 4 → Four is the number of shutters. Each has two bars, so 4 × 2 = 8.
> _For grown-ups: Used equal groups to multiply: 4 shutters with 2 bars each make 8._


#### `reader:a30-03-night` · [main](main.md#a30-03-night)

_After dark there is lantern light at the far end of the street, beyond the gate. Voices, low, and then louder._  
_You can’t hear the words. You don’t want to._  
_The house goes very quiet. Shmuel gets into his sister’s bed without asking, and she lets him._  
_Across the lane, in the rabbi’s window, the lamp is lit. It stays lit._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-2, ask): we see a lamp because its light reaches our eyes  
> _The lane is dark, but you can see the lamp across it. How can you see it?_  
> - ✅ Light from the lamp travels to your eyes → Yes. We see a glowing thing when its light reaches our eyes, and in a dark lane there is nothing to hide it.
> - Your eyes send light to the lamp → Eyes don’t send out light. They catch light that arrives from the lamp.
> - The lamp is warm → Warmth isn’t how we see. It is the light that reaches our eyes.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that we see a lamp because its light travels to our eyes._

**Shmuel**: Is the rabbi scared?  
**Rivka**: The rabbi is thinking. It looks the same from outside.  

**Choices**

- **Sit on the floor by the children’s bed.**
    - You sit on the floor with your back to the bed. After a while a small hand comes down and holds your collar.
    - Your Nubbin climbs up between them, and Shmuel goes to sleep with his face in its fur.
- **Keep watch at the shutter with Avram.**
    - **Avram**: There’s nothing to see. I know. I look anyway.
    - You look anyway, together, through the crack in the shutter, until the lights at the gate go out one by one.
- **Watch the rabbi’s window.**
    - Once, very late, a shadow crosses the rabbi’s lamp, back and forth, back and forth. Somebody walking, and not sleeping.

#### `reader:a30-03-complete` · [main](main.md#a30-03-complete)

> - ✅ Asking deep questions about life → Yes. A philosophical person wonders about big things, like good mornings and how long they last.
> - Very hungry → Not hungry. Look at what he said: he is wondering about mornings and time.
> - Very cheerful → He sounds thoughtful, not cheerful. Philosophical means wondering about big questions.
> _For grown-ups: Used context to work out that philosophical means thinking about big questions._

_Your Nubbin comes out of the house wearing the rabbi’s coat from yesterday. Most of it trails behind._  
**Dvora**: He leaves coats on pegs the way a hen leaves eggs. That’s the third this month.  
**Rabbi Loew**: Ah. I wondered where I had got to.  

### Glossary

## A30-4. The Answer

- `a30-04-s1`: Take the rabbi his supper in the study house. · _Dvora has wrapped it in a cloth. He will forget to eat it._
- `a30-04-s2`: Listen to what the rabbi asks of you. · _He is at the study house door with two men._

#### `reader:a30-04-ask` · [main](main.md#a30-04-ask)

_Dvora sends you across the lane with bread and a bowl of soup under a cloth. The study house is empty but for him._  
_He is not reading. He is sitting with his hand over his eyes, very still._

**Rabbi Loew**: It’s a very old question. Usually the answer is: be patient, be careful, be kind to your neighbours, and wait.  
**Rabbi Loew**: I have been all four. I’d like a fifth.

> _The rabbi has been patient, careful and kind, and has waited. What might the fifth thing be?_  
> - Courage → That’s a fair answer. Courage can mean doing the hard thing, even when you are afraid.
> - A good plan → That’s a fair answer. Kindness and patience are not always enough without a plan.
> - Help from other people → That’s a fair answer. Nobody keeps a whole town safe alone.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what else might help keep people safe. No single right answer._

_Your Nubbin climbs up on the bench and pushes the soup an inch closer to him, as a suggestion._

> **Ori**: A book makes a hard pillow. Sleep is when our bodies and brains do their mending.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that children need about nine to twelve hours of sleep a night._

#### `reader:a30-04-lantern` · [main](main.md#a30-04-lantern)

_Dvora lights the lantern, and you carry it up to the loft window. The flame stands up straight in the still air._  
_The lantern has a thin window of horn on each side, to keep the wind off the flame._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, ask): a lantern window lets light out and keeps wind off  
> _Why does a lantern have thin windows of horn or glass?_  
> - ✅ They let light out and keep the wind off the flame → Yes. Light passes through the thin window, but moving air can’t get at the flame to blow it out.
> - They make the flame hotter → The window doesn’t add heat. Its job is to protect the flame and let the light through.
> - They stop people seeing the light → They do the opposite. Light shines out through them.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a lantern window lets light through and shields a flame from wind._

**Rivka**: Why is it lit? Who’s it for?  
_You tell her it’s for the rabbi. She takes this very seriously._  
**Rivka**: Then I’ll mind it too. I’m good at minding things. I minded Shmuel for a whole year and he’s still alive.  
_She sits cross-legged beside the lantern, chin in her hands. Your Nubbin sits beside her, and copies her exactly._  

#### `reader:a30-04-complete` · [main](main.md#a30-04-complete)

_The lantern was lit at six in the evening. At midnight the study house door opens and shuts softly._  
_Three shapes go down the lane towards the river. Rivka is still awake, and looks at you, and looks at the lantern._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals: 6 pm to midnight  
> _The lantern was lit at six in the evening, and it is now midnight. For how many hours has it burned?_  
> - ✅ 6 hours → Yes. From 6 pm to midnight is six hours: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.
> - 5 hours → Count the hours on a clock: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. That is six.
> - 12 hours → Twelve is the clock number at midnight. From six to twelve is six hours.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a time interval from 6 pm to midnight._
