# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Fun quests: Mr. Bean (Q21–Q30)

## Q21. Bear Comes Too

- `q21-s1`: Mr. Tweed has lost his bear. Follow his pointing down to the pond and look for a clue. · _Search the reeds on the near side of the pond. Clues sparkle faintly when you are close._
- `q21-s2`: Follow the little wild Nubbin up the western hill and find where it took the bear. · _Wild Nubbins nest in the long grass near the top. Walk slowly: if you run, it will hide._
- `q21-s3`: Carry Bear back down to Mr. Tweed. · _He is still sulking on his rock by the pond. Hand him the bear with Give._

#### `reader:q21-intro` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q21-intro)

_A man in a tweed jacket is standing at his gate, patting every pocket he owns. Some of them twice._  
_He looks inside his hat. He looks under a stone._  
_He lifts a sleeping duck, peers underneath, and puts it back with a sorry little pat._  
_This is Mr. Tweed. He lives at the end of the lane, and nobody has ever heard him finish a sentence._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Hnn.  
_He holds his hands apart, about as far as a school ruler is long. Then he hugs the empty air, very tenderly._  
_He points at the empty space in his arms, then at you, then at the empty space again._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): reading body language  
> _Mr. Tweed hasn’t said a single proper word. But his body is saying a lot._  
> _What is he telling you?_  
> - ✅ He has lost something he loves. → Yes. The patting, the searching, the hug with nothing in it: he’s showing you the shape of what’s missing.
> - He wants a hug. → Close! But the hug is for something that isn’t there. He’s showing you the shape of something he has lost.
> - He’s doing a silly dance. → It does look a bit like one! But look again: pockets, hat, under a stone. He’s searching for something he loves.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out what someone was feeling and asking for from their actions alone, with no words._

**You**: Something’s missing?  
**Mr. Tweed**: Bear.  
_He makes two little ears on top of his head with his fingers, in case you were unsure about the bear._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): choosing sensible units of length  
> _His hands were about a school ruler apart. A school ruler is 30 centimetres long._  
> _So about how long is the bear?_  
> - ✅ About 30 centimetres. → Yes: about as long as your ruler. Small enough to hug, big enough to miss.
> - About 30 metres. → 30 metres is longer than a swimming pool! That bear wouldn’t fit in any pocket. Metres are for big things like rooms. Centimetres are for small things, like bears. About 30 centimetres.
> - About 30 millimetres. → 30 millimetres is only 3 centimetres: about as long as a grape. Far too small to hug! The bear is about 30 centimetres.
> _For grown-ups: Chose centimetres, not metres or millimetres, as the sensible unit for a small object._

_Then he points down the lane towards the pond, very firmly, and nods as if that settles everything._  

**Choices**

- **I’ll help you look.**
    - **Mr. Tweed**: Mm!
    - He shakes your hand with both of his, for slightly too long.
- **What does the bear look like?**
    - He mimes it: small, brown, one chewed ear, and a look of enormous dignity.
    - You are not sure how he mimed the dignity. But he did.
- **Ask your Nubbin to sniff it out.**
    - Your Nubbin sniffs Mr. Tweed’s sleeve, sneezes, and trots off towards the pond.
    - **Mr. Tweed**: Ah!

#### `reader:q21-scarf` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q21-scarf)

_In the reeds at the edge of the pond, something catches your eye._  
_It’s a tiny knitted scarf. It is exactly bear-sized, and slightly damp._  
_Mr. Tweed arrives at a jog, sees the scarf, and clutches it to his chest._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Ohh.  
_He looks at the pond. He looks under a lily pad. He looks at the sky, in case bears can fly._  
_There is no bear._  
_He sulks so hard that his whole face folds in, like a letter going back into its envelope._  
**You**: The scarf is here, but the bear isn’t. So where did it go?  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): using clues to make an inference  
> _Be a detective. The scarf is here. The bear is not. What do the clues tell you?_  
> - ✅ Something carried the bear away, and the scarf fell off. → Good detective work. A bear can’t walk off by itself, so something must have carried it. The scarf slipped off on the way.
> - The bear fell in the pond. → It’s a fair worry! But Mr. Tweed checked the pond. And a bear that fell in would probably float. More likely, something carried it away and the scarf slipped off.
> - The bear went for a walk. → Ha! In a story, maybe. But this bear can’t walk. Something must have carried it, and the scarf fell off on the way.
> _For grown-ups: Used two clues in the story to work out something the story doesn’t say straight out._


#### `reader:q21-sulk` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q21-sulk)

_Mr. Tweed sits on a rock and sulks. It is a big, proper sulk, with the bottom lip and everything._  
_Every so often he glances up the western hill. Then away. Then up again._  
**You**: Is the bear up there?  
_He shrugs. It is a very small shrug, about the size of a bear._  
_Then he points. A wild Nubbin is waddling uphill with something brown in its arms._  

> **Lesson** (science, K-ESS2-2, notice): animals line their nests with soft things  
> _Lots of animals carry soft things home to line their nests._  
> _Birds use moss, grass and feathers, and even tufts of sheep’s wool. A soft nest keeps eggs and babies warm._  
> _To a wild Nubbin, a small brown bear would make a very fine nest indeed._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that many animals collect soft materials to build and line their nests, changing the place around them._

**Mr. Tweed**: Hm!  
_He stands, marches three steps after it, remembers he is sulking, and sits back down._  
_He folds his arms. The sulk resumes, a bit louder, for anyone who might be watching._  
_He waves you on. This is your job now. He has a sulk to finish._  

#### `reader:q21-nest` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q21-nest)

_Near the top of the western hill, the long grass has been pressed into a round, cosy nest._  
_In the middle, a wild Nubbin is fast asleep. It is cuddling a small brown bear._  
_The bear has one chewed ear and, somehow, a look of great dignity._  
_The Nubbin’s tummy goes up and down. It gives a tiny snore._  
_It doesn’t know the bear belongs to anyone. It just found something soft to love._  
_You need the bear back. But the little Nubbin looks so happy._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): being gentle and fair with animals  
> _How could you get the bear back without upsetting the little Nubbin?_  
> - ✅ Slowly and gently, and leave it something else to cuddle. → That’s kind and fair. Mr. Tweed gets his bear, and the little one still has something to hold.
> - Grab it fast, before it wakes up. → That might work. But waking up to a grab is a horrible shock, and wild creatures can learn to be scared of people. Slow and gentle is better, and it’s fair to leave it something else.
> - Shout “Give it back!” → It’s fair to want the bear back! But a shout would frighten it, and it didn’t mean any harm. Slow and gentle works better, and you could leave it something to cuddle instead.
> _For grown-ups: Chose a way to solve a problem that was fair to a small animal as well as to the owner._


**Choices**

- **Ease the bear out gently.**
    - The Nubbin stirs, sighs, and lets go. You leave it a piece of fruit to cuddle instead.
    - It hugs the fruit and goes straight back to sleep.
- **Offer the little scarf in trade.**
    - The Nubbin sniffs the scarf and wraps it round its own neck. It looks very smart. Fair trade.
    - Mr. Tweed may have opinions about the scarf.

#### `reader:q21-carry` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q21-carry)

_The bear is lighter than it looks, and it looks very light._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): choosing units of mass: grams and kilograms  
> _If you wanted to weigh this light little bear, which unit would you use?_  
> - ✅ Grams → Yes. Grams are for light things, like apples, letters and small bears.
> - Kilograms → A kilogram is 1,000 grams. That’s as heavy as a big bag of sugar: far too heavy for this little bear. Grams are better for light things.
> - Metres → Metres tell you how long something is, not how heavy. For a light bear, use grams.
> _For grown-ups: Chose grams as the right unit for weighing a light object, and heard that 1 kilogram is 1,000 grams._

_Your Nubbin wants to carry it. It holds the bear the way Mr. Tweed did: very seriously, with both arms._  
_Down by the pond, a tweed shape is still sulking on its rock._  
_He hasn’t seen you yet. He is trying very hard not to look up the hill._  
_Your Nubbin hides the bear behind its back. The bear is wider than its back._  
_A chewed ear sticks out on one side. A paw sticks out on the other._  
**You**: Ready? Let’s surprise him.  

#### `reader:q21-complete` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q21-complete)

_Mr. Tweed sees the bear. His face unfolds all at once, like a deckchair snapping open._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): understanding a simile  
> _“His face unfolds like a deckchair snapping open.” Comparing two things using “like” or “as” is called a simile._  
> _What is this simile telling you?_  
> - ✅ His sad, folded-up face opened into a big happy one, all at once. → Exactly. A deckchair goes from flat and folded to wide open in one snap. So did his face.
> - He looks like a piece of furniture. → Ha! Not quite. A simile borrows one picture to describe another. A deckchair snaps from folded to wide open in one go. His face went from sad to happy just as fast.
> _For grown-ups: Explained what a simile (a comparison using “like”) tells us about a character’s face._

**Mr. Tweed**: Bear!  
_He hugs the bear. He checks its chewed ear, then its good ear, then the chewed one again._  
_He tucks it into his top pocket, so only its head pokes out._  
_Then he notices your Nubbin, gazing at the bear with enormous longing._  
_Mr. Tweed holds up one finger. He rummages in his jacket, then his trousers, then, briefly, his sock._  
_Out comes a second bear, no bigger than your thumb. He places it on your shoulder, very solemnly._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Bear.  
_It stays there. It seems to like the view._  
_That evening you pass his window. He is laying the table: two plates, one of them very small._  
_He pulls out the small chair first._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): thinking about loneliness and company  
> _Mr. Tweed lives on his own. Tonight he has set a place at the table for his bear._  
> _Why do you think he does that?_  
> - Bear is his friend, and friends eat together. → That’s a lovely answer. To Mr. Tweed, Bear isn’t just a toy. Bear is company.
> - He might be lonely. → That’s a thoughtful answer. Living alone can feel lonely, and a bear across the table helps a little. Maybe a neighbour could help a little more.
> - It’s just a funny joke. → It is funny! And lots of funny things have a little bit of sad tucked inside them. Both can be true at once.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why someone who lives alone might set a place at the table for a toy._


### Glossary

- **tenderly**: In a gentle, loving way.
- **dignity**: A calm, proud, serious way of being that makes others respect you.
- **clutches**: Holds something tightly, as if afraid to lose it.
- **sulk**: To be quiet and grumpy because you feel upset or let down.
- **resumes**: Starts again after a pause.
- **grams**: Small units for measuring how heavy something is. 1,000 grams make 1 kilogram.
- **simile**: A way of describing something by comparing it to something else, using “like” or “as”.
- **solemnly**: In a very serious way, as if something is important.

**Notes:** Lessons: body language (sel), centimetres vs metres, inference from clues, nest building, being fair to a sleeping animal, grams vs kilograms, a simile, and a think question on loneliness. The approved source quote at the reunion is replaced by “Bear!”, Mr. Tweed’s own word.

## Q22. The Armchair Drive

- `q22-s1`: Help Mr. Tweed tie the armchair on top of his hand cart. · _Use rope from your bag on the cart, then tap each corner of the chair to tie it down. Four corners, four knots._
- `q22-s2`: Clear the path to Mr. Tweed’s house before the cart gets there, so nothing falls off. · _Lift the branch, carry the sleeping Nubbin to the grass at the side, and unhook the washing line. The cart won’t stop for you._

#### `reader:q22-shop` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q22-shop)

_At the market, Mr. Tweed has bought an armchair. It is green, deep and enormous._  
_His hand cart stands beside it. The whole cart is about as big as the chair’s cushion._  
_He lifts one end of the chair and looks at the cart. Then he lifts the other end, in case that end is smaller._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Hnn.  
_He tries the chair upside down. He tries it on its side._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): finding a difference in length (will it fit?)  
> _Will it fit? Let’s measure. The armchair is 95 centimetres wide. The cart is 60 centimetres wide._  
> _How much wider is the chair than the cart?_  
> - ✅ 35 centimetres → Yes: 95 take away 60 is 35. The chair is 35 centimetres too wide to go in the cart. And turning it upside down doesn’t make it one bit smaller!
> - 45 centimetres → Nearly! Let’s check by counting on: 60 add 35 makes 95. So the chair is 35 centimetres wider.
> - 155 centimetres → That’s the two added together. To find how much wider, take away instead: 95 take away 60 is 35.
> _For grown-ups: Subtracted two measurements in centimetres to find how much too big something is._

_He sits in it and tries to push the cart with his feet. The cart is too far away._  
_Then a light comes on behind his eyes. He points at the chair, then at the top of the cart, then mimes a knot._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Rope!  
_He looks at you the way people look at someone who definitely has rope._  

**Choices**

- **Hand over some rope.**
    - He takes it with a little bow and starts a knot that goes under, over, and somehow through himself.
- **Is this a good idea?**
    - **Mr. Tweed**: Mm.
    - He nods firmly, which answers a different question. You fetch some rope.
- **We could just carry it.**
    - He looks at you, at the chair, and at the long road home. Then he gives a tiny, pitying shake of the head.

#### `reader:q22-tied` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q22-tied)

_The chair sits on top of the cart, tied down at all four corners._  
_It is also tied, by accident, to a lamp post._  
_You untie the lamp post. Mr. Tweed says sorry to it with a small bow._  
_He climbs up into the armchair and settles in, very pleased with himself._  
_The cart creaks. Up there, the chair and Mr. Tweed are a long way off the ground._  
_He wobbles left. He wobbles right. He grips the armrests and stays very, very still._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): top-heavy loads tip easily  
> _When a load has lots of weight high up, we call it top-heavy. Top-heavy things tip over easily._  
> _Where should the heavy part of a load go, to keep a cart steady?_  
> - ✅ Low down and in the middle → Yes. The lower and more central the weight, the harder it is to tip. That’s why lorries carry their heaviest loads near the floor, not stacked on the roof.
> - High up on top → That’s exactly where Mr. Tweed is sitting! Weight up high makes a cart wobbly and tippy. Low down and in the middle is steadiest.
> - Hanging off one side → Then the cart would lean that way and might tip over. Low down and in the middle is steadiest.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a load is steadiest when its weight is low down and in the middle._

_Then he takes a long stick, pokes the ground behind him, and the cart begins to roll._  

#### `reader:q22-road` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q22-road)

_The cart wobbles off down the lane. Mr. Tweed steers by leaning, and now and then by stick._  
_Up in the armchair, he can’t see the ground. He can see the sky, the trees and the birds. He waves at them._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Ooh.  
_Ahead you spot three obstacles: a fallen branch, a Nubbin asleep in the path, and a rather low washing line._  
_You’ll have to run ahead and clear the way. Anything that hits the cart falls off the cart._  
_Including, probably, Mr. Tweed._  
_Then the lane tips downhill. The cart notices at once and speeds up._  
_Mr. Tweed notices much later. He grips the armrests and lifts both feet off the ground, as if that will help._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): gravity speeds things up on a slope  
> _Mr. Tweed has stopped pushing with his stick. So why is the cart going faster?_  
> - ✅ The slope: gravity is pulling it downhill. → Exactly. Gravity always pulls things down. On a slope, that pull rolls the cart forward, faster and faster. Lifting his feet doesn’t help at all. But it does look very brave.
> - Because he lifted his feet. → He’d like to think so! But his feet weren’t touching anything. The real pull is gravity, dragging the cart down the slope.
> - The wind is pushing it. → Wind can push things, but there isn’t much today. The cart sped up when the lane tipped downhill. That’s gravity.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that a cart speeds up downhill because gravity pulls it, an unbalanced force._


**Choices**

- **Sprint ahead.**
    - You sprint. Behind you, Mr. Tweed hums a little driving tune that he has just made up.
- **Send your Nubbin to wake the sleeper.**
    - Your Nubbin trots ahead and pokes the sleeping one. It rolls over, which is not the same as moving.

#### `reader:q22-arrive` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q22-arrive)

_The cart rolls up to Mr. Tweed’s gate and stops against it with a gentle bonk._  
_His hat flies off. He catches it with one foot and flicks it back onto his head._  
_He looks round to see if anyone saw. You saw. You give him a thumbs up._  
_Nothing has fallen off, technically._  
_Mr. Tweed checks anyway: himself, the chair and the stick, in that order._  
_The chair has slid sideways. One rope is holding on by a single, very tired knot._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): working out a word from context  
> _“Nothing has fallen off, technically.” What does “technically” mean here?_  
> - ✅ It’s true by the exact rules, but only just. → Yes. Nothing fell all the way off, so it’s true. But one tired knot is all that saved the chair!
> - Using lots of machines. → Good thinking: “technical” can be about machines and gadgets. But here “technically” means “by the exact rules”. It’s true, but only just.
> - Very carefully. → Mr. Tweed was not very careful! Here “technically” means “by the exact rules”. Nothing fell off, but only just.
> _For grown-ups: Used the story around a word to work out what “technically” means._

**Mr. Tweed**: Ha!  

#### `reader:q22-complete` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q22-complete)

_Mr. Tweed climbs down. He looks at the chair. He looks at his small front door. He goes very quiet._  
_The chair will not fit through the door._  
_He measures both with his hands, three times, hoping. He gets a different answer every time._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.MD.A.1, ask): why we measure with standard units  
> _Mr. Tweed measured with his hands and got a different answer each time. Why?_  
> - ✅ Hands aren’t a fixed size, and they move about. → Right. That’s why we use rulers and tape measures. A centimetre is the same size every time, everywhere. With a tape measure: the door is 80 centimetres wide and the chair is 95. No amount of hoping changes that.
> - The door keeps changing size. → Doors stay the same, luckily! It’s his hands that wobble and stretch. A tape measure gives the same answer every time: door 80 centimetres, chair 95. It won’t fit.
> - He can’t count. → He probably can! The trouble is his hands. They aren’t a fixed size, and they move about. A tape measure says: door 80 centimetres, chair 95. It won’t fit.
> _For grown-ups: Explained why rulers and tape measures give better answers than hands, then compared two lengths._

**Mr. Tweed**: Hnn.  
_Then he unties the chair, pushes it towards you and pats it twice. It is yours. He has no room._  
_He pats the cart too. He mimes walking, then riding, then points at your legs: riding is much faster._  
_The cart is yours as well. He has, it seems, gone off carts._  
_As you leave, he carries a small kitchen chair out to his front garden and sits in the sun, perfectly happy._  
_He pats the space next to him. There is no space next to him._  
_So you sit on the wall, and for a while the two of you watch the lane together._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): quiet company as a kindness  
> _Mr. Tweed sits on his own a lot. You chose to sit with him, without saying anything at all._  
> _Why might just sitting with someone be a kind thing to do?_  
> - So they don’t feel lonely. → That’s a kind answer. Company doesn’t always need words. Sometimes just being there is the whole present.
> - Because he gave me a chair and a cart. → That’s fair: he was generous, and it’s good to give something back. Your time is a gift too.
> - I’m not sure it does anything. → That’s an honest answer. Watch his face, though. Sometimes the smallest things matter most to the person who gets them.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why simply sitting with someone can be a kind thing to do._


### Glossary

- **pitying**: Feeling a bit sorry for someone, because you think they don’t understand.
- **mimes**: Acts something out without words, using only the face and body.
- **top-heavy**: Heavier at the top than the bottom, so it tips over easily.
- **obstacle**: Something in your way that you have to get past or move.
- **technically**: True by the exact rules, even if it doesn’t feel quite true.

**Notes:** Lessons: will it fit? (95 − 60 cm), top-heavy loads, gravity downhill (3-PS2-1), “technically” in context, why standard units beat hand spans, and a think question on quiet company. Mr. Tweed’s armchair is 95 cm; his door is 80 cm; the cart is 60 cm.

## Q23. Pumpkin Head

- `q23-s1`: Calm the wandering pumpkin so it stops bumping into things. · _Tap the pumpkin and choose Pet. A calm Nubbin sits still and listens._
- `q23-s2`: Free the Nubbin with three gentle wiggles, in time with the beat. · _Tap when the pumpkin glows: tap, tap, tap. Pulling only makes it squeak, and there is no rush._

#### `reader:q23-patch` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q23-patch)

_It is harvest time, and the pumpkin patch is fat and orange._  
_Long green vines curl along the ground between the pumpkins, under big, floppy leaves._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, do): the life cycle of a pumpkin  
> _Every pumpkin in this patch started as one flat, pale seed._  
> _Put the pumpkin’s life in order: seed, seedling, long vine, yellow flower, pumpkin._  
> _And what’s inside every pumpkin? More seeds, ready to start again. That’s why we call it a life cycle._  
> Check: `edu:order:pumpkin-life-cycle:5`
> _For grown-ups: Put the stages of a pumpkin plant’s life cycle in order, from seed back to seed._

_One pumpkin is moving. Left, then right, then gently into a fence post. It goes “mff”._  
_Mr. Tweed is already crouched beside it, studying it with enormous concentration._  
_He knocks on the pumpkin. The pumpkin knocks back._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Oh!  
_Two little feet stick out of the bottom._  
_A curious Nubbin climbed in to eat the middle. Now it can’t find the way out._  
_Mr. Tweed grabs the stalk and braces one foot against the pumpkin, ready to pull with all his might._  

**Choices**

- **Stop! Not like that.**
    - He freezes mid-heave, one foot in the air, and slowly lets go of the stalk.
    - **Mr. Tweed**: Mm.
- **Help him pull.**
    - You both pull, and the pumpkin squeaks. The Nubbin inside squeaks louder.
    - You both let go at once, and pretend that didn’t happen. Pulling a scared creature only scares it more.
- **Watch which way it wriggles.**
    - The pumpkin rocks left, right, left. There is a rhythm to it. The Nubbin is trying to wiggle its way out.

#### `reader:q23-calm` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q23-calm)

_You kneel down beside the pumpkin and rest one hand on it, very gently._  
_You pat it slowly. Pat. Pat. Pat._  
_The pumpkin stops wandering and sits down with a soft thump._  
_From inside comes a small, contented hum._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): helping someone who is frightened  
> _The Nubbin was scared and bumping about. What helped it calm down?_  
> - ✅ Slow pats and a gentle hand. → Yes. When someone is frightened, slow and gentle helps. Fast and loud makes it worse. It works for people too: a calm voice, a slow breath, a quiet hand on the shoulder.
> - Pulling it out quickly. → Pulling only made it squeak! When someone is frightened, slow and gentle helps much more.
> - Knocking loudly to say hello. → A loud knock from outside would sound like thunder in there! Slow, gentle pats are what calmed it.
> _For grown-ups: Picked out what helps a frightened creature calm down: slow, gentle and quiet._

_Mr. Tweed puts his ear to the pumpkin and listens._  
_He nods, like a doctor who has heard exactly what he expected to hear._  
**Mr. Tweed**: Mm.  

#### `reader:q23-wiggle` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q23-wiggle)

_Mr. Tweed holds up three fingers. He mimes a tiny wiggle, wiggle, wiggle, and then a pop._  
_He looks very proud of this plan. It is, to be fair, a good plan._  
_You put both hands on the pumpkin. Inside, the Nubbin goes still and listens._  
**Mr. Tweed**: One…  
_He beats time in the air with one finger, like the conductor of a very small orchestra._  
_Then he gets carried away. Both arms. Bigger and bigger, grander and grander._  
_Inside the pumpkin, the Nubbin starts to hum along, louder and louder._  

> **Lesson** (music, notice): tempo and crescendo  
> _When music grows louder bit by bit, musicians call it a crescendo. The Nubbin’s hum is doing one now._  
> _The speed of the beat is called the tempo. For a good wiggle, the tempo has to stay steady._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned two music words: tempo (the speed of the beat) and crescendo (getting louder bit by bit)._

**You**: Gently. On the beat.  

#### `reader:q23-pop` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q23-pop)

_Wiggle. Wiggle. Wiggle._  
_On the third wiggle there is a soft pop, and the Nubbin slides out in a shower of seeds._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): halving within 100  
> _The pumpkin glows 90 times every minute. Musicians say the tempo is 90 beats per minute._  
> _How many beats happen in half a minute?_  
> - ✅ 45 → Yes: half of 90 is 45. That’s a lot of glows. You only need three of them.
> - 30 → Let’s check: 30 and 30 makes 60, not 90. Half of 90 is 45, because 45 and 45 makes 90.
> - 180 → 180 is double 90: that would be two whole minutes. Half a minute is half of 90, which is 45.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out half of 90 to find how many beats happen in half a minute._

> - ✅ H → Yes. An H has a line of symmetry straight across the middle. Flip it top to bottom and it matches itself. Nobody would ever know. Except Mr. Tweed.
> - T → That’s the one he tried! A T’s only line of symmetry runs up and down, so upside down it turns into a lamp post. An H has a line of symmetry straight across the middle, so it matches itself upside down.
> - R → An R has no line of symmetry at all, so upside down it looks very odd indeed. An H has a line of symmetry straight across the middle, so it matches itself upside down.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that an H looks the same flipped top to bottom because it has a line of symmetry straight across the middle, and a T does not._

**Odette Pennant**: Keep the cosy, it suits your eggs. And keep that box. Two eggs side by side, on wheels? That’s a pram, that is.  
_She slips the cosy off and hands it back to you. Mr. Tweed passes her the egg last of all._  
_He holds on a moment longer than he needs to, pats it twice, and lets go. She tucks it into her basket._  
**Odette Pennant**: So. Was it good?  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): honesty when someone trusts you  
> _Odette asks if it was good. The egg did go rolling off, but it’s safe now. What would you tell her?_  
> - Tell her it went for a roll. → That’s an honest answer. Odette trusted you, and the truth helps her know her egg better.
> - Say it was perfect. It’s safe now, after all. → That’s a kind thought: you don’t want to worry her, and the egg is safe. But she might like to know her egg is a roller. Something to think about.
> - Tell her, and tell her the funny bits too. → That’s a great answer. The truth can be told kindly, and even with a laugh.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how to answer honestly and kindly when someone who trusted you asks how it went._


**Choices**

    - **Odette Pennant**: They always do. That’s why I asked a good Keeper and not the waiting room.
- **A perfect angel.**

## Q30. The Three-Wheeler

- `q30-s1`: Meet Rufus at the start line and enter the ridge race in your hand cart. · _Ride your hand cart to the start line on the ridge track, and talk to Rufus._
- `q30-s2`: Win the race. Each time you draw level with the Bluebell on a bend, it tips over: make that happen three times. · _Draw level with the Bluebell on a bend and Mr. Tweed will wave. Do it three times, then cross the line first._

#### `reader:q30-meet` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q30-meet)

_A bright blue three-wheeled cart rattles up the lane. It has one wheel at the front and a great deal of confidence._  
**Rufus Crankshaw**: Afternoon! Rufus Crankshaw. And this is the Bluebell. Fastest three wheels this side of the ridge.  
_Mr. Tweed is riding in the back of your hand cart, the one he gave you. He looks at the Bluebell’s single front wheel._  
_He smiles a very small smile._  
**Rufus Crankshaw**: Is that a race face? That’s a race face. One lap of the ridge track. Winner gets bragging rights.  
_Mr. Tweed points forward like a ship’s captain. Then he holds up three fingers and tips his flat hand gently onto its side._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): why a narrow base tips more easily  
> _Mr. Tweed thinks the Bluebell will tip over three times. Why might a cart with one front wheel tip on a bend?_  
> - ✅ Its wheels make a narrow triangle, so its weight can lean out past the edge. → Yes. Join up the three wheels and you get a triangle, narrow at the front. On a bend, the weight swings outwards. If it leans past the edge of that triangle, over it goes.
> - Blue carts are tippy. → Ha! Colour makes no difference. It’s the narrow triangle of wheels: on a bend, the weight can lean past its edge.
> - Three is an unlucky number. → Numbers can’t be lucky or unlucky! It’s the narrow triangle of wheels. On a bend, the weight can lean out past its edge.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that a three-wheeled cart’s narrow triangle of wheels lets its weight lean past the edge on a bend._

**Mr. Tweed**: Three.  

**Choices**

- **You’re on.**
    - **Rufus Crankshaw**: Splendid! Mind your wheels. All four of them, show-off.
- **Is the Bluebell… stable?**
    - **Rufus Crankshaw**: Stable as a table! A three-legged table, which is the steadiest kind. Ask anyone.
    - Behind him, the Bluebell tips gently onto its side, all by itself.
    - **Rufus Crankshaw**: …It does that.
    - Rufus is half right. Three legs never wobble on bumpy ground. But a narrow base can still tip over sideways.

#### `reader:q30-grid` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q30-grid)

_Two carts on the start line._  
_Rufus revs the Bluebell by saying “brrm” quite loudly._  
_Mr. Tweed climbs out and polishes the front of your cart with his sleeve._  
_Then he walks over and polishes the front of the Bluebell too, just as carefully._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, ask): fairness: treating both sides the same  
> _Why does Mr. Tweed polish the Bluebell too?_  
> - ✅ To be fair: both carts get the same. → Yes. A fair race starts with both sides treated the same. Then whoever wins, wins properly.
> - He just likes polishing. → He probably does! But he did it just as carefully as yours. He wants both carts treated the same, and that’s fair.
> - To trick Rufus. → Good suspicious thinking! But a shiny front doesn’t slow anyone down. He’s treating both carts the same, to be fair.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that a fair contest starts with both sides treated the same._

**Rufus Crankshaw**: Very sporting of you!  
_Mr. Tweed climbs back in, folds his hands, and nods. Now it’s a race._  

#### `reader:q30-race` · [main](../../../fun/bean.md#q30-race)

_The flag drops. Rufus shoots off the line, waving._  
_In the back of your cart, Mr. Tweed points at the Bluebell, then at a gap on the inside of the bend._  
_Every time you draw level, he leans out and gives Rufus a cheerful little wave._  
_Every time, Rufus waves back. The Bluebell leans, and leans, and gently tips over._  
**Rufus Crankshaw**: Not to worry! It does that!  
_He rights it with one hand and is off again. He is very, very good at righting it._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): adding time across a minute  
> _Each tip costs Rufus 2 seconds. Without tipping, his lap would take 3 minutes 58 seconds._  
> _After three tips, what is his time?_  
> - ✅ 4 minutes 4 seconds → Yes. Three tips cost 6 seconds. 3 minutes 58 seconds and 2 more makes 4 minutes. Then 4 more: 4 minutes 4 seconds.
> - 3 minutes 64 seconds → Good adding! But 60 seconds make a minute, so 3 minutes 64 seconds is really 4 minutes 4 seconds.
> - 4 minutes 0 seconds → That’s just one tip: 2 seconds. Three tips is 6 seconds, which takes him to 4 minutes 4 seconds.
> _For grown-ups: Added seconds to a lap time and crossed into the next minute, knowing 60 seconds make 1 minute._

**Mr. Tweed**: Ha!  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): what makes a win fair  
> _Is it fair to win because the Bluebell keeps tipping over whenever Rufus waves?_  
> - No. We should win fairly. → That’s a fair-minded answer. A win feels best when you know you earned it.
> - Yes. Rufus chooses to wave. → That’s a thoughtful point. Nobody is tricking him: he waves at everyone. Maybe the real problem is his cart.
> - It’s fine, because everyone is having fun. → That’s a lovely way to see it. Rufus is laughing more than anybody. Sometimes how you play matters more than who wins.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether a win is fair when the other side keeps having bad luck, and why it matters._


**Choices**

- **Tell Mr. Tweed to stop waving. Win it fair.**
    - Mr. Tweed looks wounded, and sits on his hands.
    - On the next bend Rufus draws level, sees you, and waves anyway. The Bluebell tips over.
    - **Rufus Crankshaw**: Not to worry! I just like waving!
    - Mr. Tweed slowly takes his hands out from under him. It was never his fault. He looks enormously relieved.
- **Let him wave. It’s only friendly.**
    - Mr. Tweed waves so warmly that a Nubbin on the verge waves back, and also tips over.
