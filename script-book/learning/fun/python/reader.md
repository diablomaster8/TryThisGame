## Q1. Two Shrubberies

- `q01-s1`: Bring the hooded three a shrubbery. · _Buy a flower bed in the Decorate shop and place it. The hoods will call it a shrubbery._
- `q01-s2`: Make a second shrubbery, a little higher, with a beetle-sized path, and place it by the first. · _Place a second flower bed and a path stone beside the first one._

#### `reader:q01-intro` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q01-intro)

_The waterfall path is blocked. Three Nubbins in long brown hoods stand shoulder to shoulder, which for Nubbins means elbow to elbow._  
_The middle one is taller than the others. Look closer: it is standing on a log._  
**The Hooded Three**: GRONK!  
_Your Nubbin flinches. A small bird leaves a nearby tree and does not come back._  
**You**: Could we just get past, please?  
**The Tall Hood**: You may not. Not until you bring us the one thing our hearts are set on.  
_There is a long pause, full of suspense. Everyone waits to hear what comes next. The hoods sway._  
**The Tall Hood**: It is green. It is leafy. It is… a bush. A tidy little bush. A shrubbery.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): how writers build suspense  
> _Look at the Tall Hood’s words: “It is… a bush.”_  
> _Why do you think the writer put “…” before the big reveal?_  
> - ✅ To make us wait and wonder: suspense. → Yes. The dots are a pause. A pause makes the reader lean in and wonder what is coming. Then the answer lands: a bush. The bigger the pause, the sillier the answer feels.
> - Because the Tall Hood forgot the word. → Ha! It might have. But here the dots are on purpose. They make a pause, so we wait and wonder. That waiting feeling is called suspense. Writers use it right before a surprise.
> - It doesn’t mean anything. → Read it aloud and stop at the dots. Hear the wait? That pause builds suspense. Writers use “…” to make us lean in before a surprise. Here, the surprise is a bush.
> _For grown-ups: Noticed how a pause (written “…”) builds suspense before a big reveal._

_The other two nod gravely. One of them was not expecting it and nods a moment late._  

**Choices**

- **What sort of shrubbery?**
    - **The Tall Hood**: A tidy one. About knee-high, on a Nubbin. With leaves. Leaves are important.
    - The hood on the left whispers something. The Tall Hood adds, firmly: “Green.”
- **Chant back at them.**
    - You make the loudest, strangest noise you can manage.
    - The hooded three look at one another. The one on the right writes something down.
- **One shrubbery. Coming up.**
    - **The Tall Hood**: Good. We shall wait here. Swaying.

#### `reader:q01-first` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q01-first)

_You set the shrubbery down by the falls. It is a neat little bush of reeds and twigs, with fresh leaves._  
_The hooded three lean over it and inspect it from every side, including underneath._  
_To inspect something is to look at it very carefully. They take this seriously. One uses a magnifying leaf._  
**The Tall Hood**: This shrubbery pleases us. It has leaves. We approve of the leaves.  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-LS2-1, notice): what a shrub is, and what leaves do  
> **The Tall Hood**: Do you know what makes a shrub a shrub? Woody stems. Lots of them, all from near the ground.  
> **The Tall Hood**: A tree usually has one big trunk and grows much taller. A shrub stays low and bushy.  
> **The Tall Hood**: And the leaves are the kitchen. They use sunlight, air and water to make the plant’s food.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a shrub is a woody plant with many stems, and that leaves use sunlight to make a plant’s food._

**The Tall Hood**: A shrubbery without leaves is just a pile of sticks with ideas.  
_They chant once more, softly, as a kind of thank-you. The bird does not come back for that either._  

#### `reader:q01-second` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q01-second)

_You step towards the path. The Tall Hood raises one paw._  
**The Tall Hood**: Wait. There is a second thing.  
**You**: You said one shrubbery.  
**The Tall Hood**: We did. We have grown since then. As people.  
**The Tall Hood**: Bring us another. It goes next to this one, but up a step, so the two look terraced, like stairs up a hill.  
**The Tall Hood**: And a small path between them. Not a big path. The sort of path a beetle would enjoy.  
_The other two hoods nod. One holds its paws a beetle-width apart, to help._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.MD.A.1, do): measure length in centimetres  
> **The Tall Hood**: Measure the gap between my colleague’s paws. Use the ruler from your bag. Line up the zero.  
> _The gap is 2 centimetres: about as wide as a beetle walking along with its legs out._  
> **The Tall Hood**: Excellent. A metre is 100 of those. That would be a path for a horse. We are not catering for horses.

> _For grown-ups: Measured a beetle-sized path with a ruler (2 cm) and compared centimetres with metres._

**You**: And then we can pass?  
**The Tall Hood**: Then we shall discuss it.  
**The Tall Hood**: Also, we are no longer the Hooded Three. We have a new name. It goes like this.  
_It makes a noise like a tea tray falling down a staircase full of spoons. The other two join in, slightly behind._  
**You**: I can’t say that.  
**The Tall Hood**: Nobody can. That is what makes it so frightening.  

**Choices**

- **Fine. A little higher, with a path.**
    - **The Tall Hood**: Good. Use gravel. We have strong feelings about gravel.
- **Can the path be imaginary?**
    - **The Tall Hood**: No. We tried that once. Somebody fell off it.

#### `reader:q01-terraced` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q01-terraced)

_Two shrubberies, one a little higher than the other, with a tiny gravel path wandering between them._  
_It is, you have to admit, rather lovely._  
**The Tall Hood**: Oh. Oh, that is nice.  
_Just then a rain cloud drifts over the falls and lets go. Everyone huddles under the hoods’ hoods._  
_The rain runs down the hill. On the terrace it slows, soaks in, and stays where the roots are._  
**The Tall Hood**: Look at that. The water is staying put. Our steps are doing a job.  
_Below the path, on the bare slope, a little stream of muddy water carries soil away._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-ESS2-1, notice): how terraces and roots slow erosion  
> _When rain rushes down a bare slope, it carries soil with it. That slow wearing away of land is called erosion._  
> _Flat steps called terraces slow the water down, and plant roots hold the soil together._  
> _Farmers in hilly places have built terraces for thousands of years, for exactly this reason._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that terraces and plant roots slow rainwater so it does not wash soil away (erosion)._


#### `reader:q01-complete` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q01-complete)

_The hooded three step aside. Then they step back again, because they want to keep looking at the shrubbery._  
**The Tall Hood**: You may pass. You may pass every day, in fact. We shall be here.  
**You**: Here? For good?  
**The Tall Hood**: There is a terrace, and a path. Where else would we go?  
**The Tall Hood**: We guarded this path for years, you know. We never once asked what was at the other end of it.  
**The Tall Hood**: A shrubbery is a much better reason to stand somewhere.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): think question: doing things just because we always have  
> **The Tall Hood**: We stood here for years, guarding a path, and never asked why.  
> **The Tall Hood**: Why do you think someone keeps doing a thing for years without asking what it is for?  
> - Because it’s a habit, and habits are comfy. → Yes. A habit is like a well-worn path. Your feet follow it without asking your head. That is a fair answer. We were very comfy. We just weren’t happy.
> - Because nobody told them to stop. → That is a fair answer. Nobody did tell us. And we never told ourselves. Sometimes you have to be your own somebody.
> - Maybe they were scared of what was at the other end. → Oh. That is a thoughtful answer. Perhaps we were. Guarding a path is easier than walking it. A shrubbery gave us a braver reason to stay.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why people keep doing something out of habit, and what it means to find a better reason._

_They settle on a flat rock by the falls. One produces a tiny rake and starts tidying the gravel._  
_The terraced shrubbery is yours to move anywhere you like. They hope you won’t. They will forgive you if you do._  
_At dusk you can hear them practising their new name. They have still not got all the way through it._  

### Glossary

- **suspense**: The excited, waiting feeling when you want to know what happens next.
- **inspect**: To look at something very carefully, checking every part.
- **terraced**: Built in flat steps, one a little higher than the next, like stairs up a hill.
- **erosion**: The slow wearing away of land by water, wind or ice.

## Q2. The Plank Knight

- `q02-s1`: Beat Sir Dentwhistle at karate on the plank bridge. · _Choose Karate on the bridge to start a bout. Any of your Nubbins can take part._
- `q02-s2`: Win a second bout against Sir Dentwhistle. · _Same bridge. Swap in a fresher Nubbin if yours is tired; Sir Dentwhistle does not mind._
- `q02-s3`: Win the third and final bout. · _If you keep losing, rest your Nubbin first. Tired Nubbins move slowly._

#### `reader:q02-intro` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-intro)

_A plank bridge crosses the stream. In the middle of it stands a Power Nubbin in a dented tin helmet, perfectly still._  
_It has stood there so long that a snail has started up its leg. It has not noticed, or will not admit it._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): how a snail moves  
> _How does a snail climb a knight? Slowly, and on one big foot._  
> _A snail’s body is mostly one long, muscly foot. Tiny waves ripple along it and push the snail forward._  
> _It also makes slime, which helps it grip and glide. Even up a leg that would rather it didn’t._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a snail glides on one muscly foot, over a layer of slime it makes itself._

**You**: Excuse me. We’d like to cross.  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Halt! This plank is closed to the public, and you are extremely public.  
_Its voice echoes inside the helmet. It sounds like someone talking into a bucket._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: I am Sir Dentwhistle. I have guarded this plank for three whole days.  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Before that it was unguarded. Chaos. People simply walked across it.  
_It raises its little fists. If you want to cross, you will have to beat it at karate._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: I warn you, I am extremely courteous. I shall bow first, and then defeat you.  

**Choices**

- **Bow politely, then fight.**
    - **Sir Dentwhistle**: A bow! Now we are both courteous. This will be the politest defeat in history.
    - In real karate, people bow before and after a bout. It means: I respect you, and I will play fair.
- **Can’t we just go round?**
    - You look downstream. There is a perfectly good row of stepping stones about ten steps away.
    - **Sir Dentwhistle**: Nobody uses those. They are beneath us. Literally. They are lower.

#### `reader:q02-round1` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-round1)

_Your Nubbin’s best move sends the dented helmet spinning off into the grass. Bonk. Roll. Stop._  
_Underneath, Sir Dentwhistle has very flat hair._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Barely a bump! I hardly felt a thing!  
**You**: It was your hat.  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: A hat is a sort of bump. A bump on the head. Again!  
_Its ears have gone pink. The snail stops climbing, out of tact._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): character traits from what a character says and does  
> _Sir Dentwhistle just lost its helmet. It says it was “barely a bump”._  
> _What trait does that show? What is it like?_  
> - ✅ It is proud, and hates admitting it lost. → Exactly. Its words say “fine”. Its pink ears say “embarrassed”. When words and actions don’t match, the actions often tell the truth.
> - It really didn’t feel anything. → Its head is fine, true. But look at its ears: pink. It feels something. Embarrassment. It is proud and won’t admit it lost. That’s a character trait: something it does again and again.
> - It is very brave. → It is brave, in a way! But calling it “barely a bump” shows something else too. It is proud and hates admitting a loss. Watch for it: it will do it again.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a character’s trait (proud, won’t admit losing) from its words and its pink ears._


#### `reader:q02-scarf` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-scarf)

_Sir Dentwhistle wraps a long woollen scarf round its neck three times, for armour._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: The scarf is my second line of defence. Nobody has ever got past the scarf.  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: My mother knitted it. She said nothing would ever get through it. She meant the cold, but the principle stands.  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-1, ask): insulation: wool traps warmth  
> **Sir Dentwhistle**: My mother called it insulation. Tell me, Keeper: where does a scarf’s warmth actually come from?  
> - ✅ From you. The wool keeps your own warmth in. → Correct! Wool is full of tiny pockets of trapped air. Heat struggles to get through them. So my warmth stays with me. That is called insulation. My mother was an expert.
> - The wool makes heat by itself. → A popular idea! But no. Put a scarf on a cold stone, and the stone stays cold. Wool traps air, and air keeps your own body’s warmth in. That is called insulation.
> - From the sun. → The sun warms many things. But a scarf works in the dark too. Its wool traps air, which keeps in the warmth my body makes. Insulation!
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a scarf doesn’t make heat; wool traps air and keeps in the body’s own warmth._

**You**: Has anybody tried?  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: That is not the point.  
_It takes up a fighting stance. The stance is mostly scarf._  
_The snail has reached its shoulder and is enjoying the view._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.D.8, ask): two-step length problem in centimetres  
> _Each wrap round Sir Dentwhistle’s small neck uses 20 centimetres of scarf. It wraps three times._  
> _Then 40 centimetres dangle down at the end. How long is the whole scarf?_  
> - ✅ 100 cm, which is 1 metre. → Yes. Three wraps of 20 is 60. Add the 40 hanging down: 100 centimetres. And 100 centimetres is exactly one metre. A metre of pure defence.
> - 60 cm → Good start: three wraps of 20 is 60. But don’t forget the bit dangling down. 60 add 40 is 100 centimetres. That’s one whole metre.
> - 63 cm → Careful: it’s three wraps of 20, so 20 + 20 + 20, which is 60. Then add the 40 that hangs down. 60 + 40 = 100 centimetres, or one metre.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a two-step problem: 3 wraps of 20 cm plus 40 cm left over makes 100 cm, or 1 metre._


#### `reader:q02-round2` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-round2)

_Your Nubbin grabs the end of the scarf and steps back. And back. And back._  
_The scarf unwinds in one long, satisfying pull and drifts off downstream like a woolly eel._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, notice): similes  
> _“Like a woolly eel” is a simile: a comparison that uses “like” or “as”._  
> _An eel is a long, wriggly fish. Picture a wriggly fish made of wool. That’s the scarf._  
> _For grown-ups: Spotted a simile (“like a woolly eel”) and explained how comparing two things paints a picture._

**Sir Dentwhistle**: Barely bothered! I was getting warm anyway.  
_It puffs out three hot, cross breaths._  
_Its tiny round glasses fog up completely. It looks like a very small, very angry cloud._  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-4, notice): condensation on cool glass  
> _Why did its glasses fog up? Breath is warm, and it carries water as an invisible gas: water vapour._  
> _When that warm, damp breath touches cooler glass, the vapour turns back into tiny drops of water._  
> _That change is called condensation. It’s the same thing that mists a bathroom mirror after a bath._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned why glasses fog: water vapour in warm breath turns back into tiny drops on cooler glass._

**Sir Dentwhistle**: I can’t see a thing. This is not a disadvantage. It is a strategy.  

#### `reader:q02-glasses` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-glasses)

_Sir Dentwhistle wipes its glasses on its own tummy and puts them back on, crooked._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Final round. I have one item left, and it is my best item.  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Without these, I cannot see you. Which means you cannot see me either. Logically.  
**You**: That isn’t how seeing works.  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: It is how it works on my bridge.  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-2, ask): we see things when light from them reaches our eyes  
> **Sir Dentwhistle**: Prove it, then. If I take off my glasses, can you still see me?  
> - ✅ Yes. Light bounces off you into my eyes. → Exactly. Light hits Sir Dentwhistle, bounces off, and travels into your eyes. That’s how you see it. What it can see doesn’t change that at all. Its logic is wonky. Its plank is not exempt from science.
> - No, it’s right. → Try it! Close your own eyes. Can your Nubbin still see you? Of course it can. We see things when light bounces off them into our eyes. What the other person sees doesn’t matter.
> - Only if it can see me first. → Seeing isn’t a swap. It only needs light: light bounces off Sir Dentwhistle and into your eyes. So you can see it whether its eyes are open, shut or fogged up.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that you see an object when light bounces off it into your eyes, whatever the object can see._

_Your Nubbin looks up at you. It would like to win quickly and then have a snack._  

**Choices**

- **Go easy on it.**
    - Your Nubbin nods and loosens its shoulders in a merciful way.
- **Give it everything.**
    - Your Nubbin cracks its knuckles. It has no knuckles. It cracks them anyway.

#### `reader:q02-round3` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-round3)

_The glasses fly off and land neatly in the snail’s path. The snail goes round them._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: I am completely fine. Never better. Which one of you said that? Both of you?  
_It is talking to a fence post._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Return at once! I have not finished being brave! I have barely started!  
_The fence post, to its credit, does not move an inch._  
_You pick up the glasses and gently put them back on Sir Dentwhistle’s nose. It blinks at you._  

> **Lesson** (health, notice): glasses help eyes focus; eye tests  
> _Lots of people wear glasses, from small children to grandparents. They help eyes focus, so things look sharp._  
> _If words or faces look blurry to you, tell a grown-up. An eye test is quick, and it doesn’t hurt._  
> **Sir Dentwhistle**: Mine were prescribed by a professional. I simply prefer to fight without them. Occasionally. By accident.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that glasses help eyes focus, and that blurry seeing is something to tell a grown-up about._

**Sir Dentwhistle**: Ah. There you are. You have moved. Sneaky.  

#### `reader:q02-complete` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q02-complete)

_Sir Dentwhistle sits down on the plank, which is the closest it will ever come to stepping aside._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Very well. You may cross. Not because you won. Because I am gracious, and I have decided to have a sit.  
_You pick the dented helmet out of the grass and offer it back. It pushes it towards you._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: Keep it. I have several others. At home. In a box.  
_It does not have several others. You can tell from the way it watches the helmet._  
**Sir Dentwhistle**: And tell your Nubbins this: losing is only losing if you mind. I never mind. I am famous for not minding.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): think question: plain truth or saving face  
> _Sir Dentwhistle lost three times, but won’t quite say so._  
> _Is it kinder to tell someone the plain truth, or to let them keep their pride?_  
> - The plain truth. Pretending doesn’t help anyone. → That’s a fair answer. Honest words, said gently, can be a gift. People often trust you more for them.
> - Let them keep their pride. Everyone needs that sometimes. → That’s a kind answer. Sometimes people need a soft landing first, and the truth later.
> - It depends on the person, and how you say it. → That’s a wise answer. A truth said kindly is very different from a truth said to win.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about when it is kinder to tell someone a hard truth and when to let them keep their pride._

_You cross the plank in two steps. Downstream, the stepping stones look on, unused and faintly hurt._  
_From now on your Nubbins take karate losses the way Sir Dentwhistle does: cheerfully, and slightly wrongly._  

**Choices**

- **Shall we call it honours even?**
    - **Sir Dentwhistle**: Honours even. Yes. I was about to suggest it myself. I was being gracious.
    - It shakes your hand, and then the fence post’s, to be thorough.
- **You lost, you know. Three times.**
    - Sir Dentwhistle is quiet for a long moment. The snail, now on top of its head, is quiet too.
    - **Sir Dentwhistle**: …I know. I was there.
    - **Sir Dentwhistle**: Thank you for saying it plainly. Nobody ever does. It is oddly restful.

### Glossary

- **courteous**: Polite and respectful, even to someone you are competing against.
- **trait**: A quality that is part of someone’s character, like being proud, kind or stubborn.
- **insulation**: Material that stops heat getting in or out, like wool in a scarf.
- **simile**: A comparison that uses “like” or “as”, such as “drifts off like a woolly eel”.
- **condensation**: When water vapour (water as a gas) cools and turns back into drops of liquid water.
- **logically**: Following sensible steps of reasoning. Sir Dentwhistle uses the word without doing it.
- **gracious**: Kind and polite, especially when things haven’t gone your way.

## Q3. The Bridge of Three Questions

- `q03-s1`: Count every Nubbin in your Grove, then go back to the gorge. · _Open the counter and tap each Nubbin once. Use tally marks in fives. Don’t forget the ones in the pond._
- `q03-s2`: Find out how fast a Fly Nubbin can carry a coconut. · _Take a coconut from the palm on the coast, hand it to a Fly Nubbin and time its flight across the pond._

#### `reader:q03-intro` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q03-intro)

_At the edge of the gorge, beside a rope bridge nobody has ever been allowed on, sits a very old Keeper in a very old hood._  
_The hood has a fringe of moss. So, possibly, does the Keeper._  
**Old Gorrel**: Halt. Nobody sets foot on my bridge without answering three questions first.  
**Old Gorrel**: Answer rightly, and the bridge is yours. Answer wrongly, and… well. It is a long way down. To the stream.  
_You look down. The stream is knee-deep and full of contented ducks._  
**Old Gorrel**: First question. How many Nubbins live in your Grove? Exactly. Not roughly. I will know.  
**You**: I… think I know? Let me check.  
**Old Gorrel**: Go and count them. I shall wait. I am extremely good at waiting.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, do): tally marks in groups of five  
> **Old Gorrel**: A tip from an old counter. Use a tally. One line for each Nubbin.  
> **Old Gorrel**: Four lines standing up, then the fifth goes across them, like a little gate. That makes a group of five.  
> **Old Gorrel**: At the end, count the gates in fives, five, ten, fifteen, then add the loose lines. Much harder to lose your place.  
> Check: `edu:tally:nubbins:fives`
> _For grown-ups: Counted with tally marks in fives, then counted the groups (5, 10, 15…) to find the total quickly._


**Choices**

- **Make a confident guess.**
    - **Old Gorrel**: Wrong. Probably. A guess is not an answer. Go and count.
- **Go and count properly.**
    - **Old Gorrel**: Wise. The last one who guessed is still in the stream. He likes it there now.

#### `reader:q03-count` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q03-count)

_You come back with the number, tally and all. Old Gorrel checks it against something written on his sleeve._  
**Old Gorrel**: Correct. Annoyingly.  
**Old Gorrel**: Second question. What did your newest Nubbin eat for breakfast?  
**You**: A run fruit. Half of one. It dropped the other half in the pond.  
**Old Gorrel**: Correct, and also tragic.  
_Down in the stream, a duck finds the floating half, eats it, and then spends a long time tidying its feathers._  
**Old Gorrel**: Ducks. Always preening. Vain creatures. Dry, though. Always dry.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): how ducks keep their feathers waterproof  
> **Old Gorrel**: It isn’t vanity, really. Near its tail a duck has a small gland that makes oil.  
> **Old Gorrel**: When it preens, it rubs that oil all over its feathers with its beak. Water rolls straight off.  
> **Old Gorrel**: That is why a duck can sit in a stream all day and still be dry underneath. I envy it.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that ducks spread oil from a gland near the tail over their feathers to keep them waterproof._


#### `reader:q03-third` · [main](../../../fun/python.md#q03-third)

**Old Gorrel**: Third question.  
_He leans forward. The hood leans further. The ducks below go quiet, sensing an occasion._  
**Old Gorrel**: Read this card. Out loud. It is very old.  
_The card says: “How quickly can a small bird fly, with nothing at all in its claws?”_  
**You**: Birds aren’t really my area.  
**Old Gorrel**: Nor mine. I have never actually seen the bird on the card.  
**Old Gorrel**: Then a Fly Nubbin. Carrying a coconut. How fast? Go and find out.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): speed needs distance and time  
> **Old Gorrel**: Before you go, Keeper. To find the speed of something, how fast it goes, what must you measure?  
> - ✅ How far it goes, and how long it takes. → Yes. Distance and time. Say the pond is 20 metres across and it takes 10 seconds. Then it flies 2 metres every second. That’s its speed. Off you go.
> - How heavy the coconut is. → The coconut might slow it down, true. But you can’t get speed from weight alone. Speed needs distance and time: how far, and how long. Measure the pond, then time the flight.
> - Just how long it takes. → Half right! But 10 seconds to cross a puddle is slow, and 10 seconds to cross a lake is fast. You need both: how far it goes, and how long it takes.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out that to measure speed you need two measurements: how far something goes, and how long it takes._
