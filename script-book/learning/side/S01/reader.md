# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S1: The Wardens’ Walk

## S1. The Wardens’ Walk

- `s1-step1`: Walk beside Moss to the stream lantern, at his speed. · _Moss walks very slowly. Stay next to him. If you get ahead, he stops and waits for you to notice._
- `s1-step-boats`: Play Briar’s game: float a lit leaf-boat from the stream lantern all the way to the pond. · _Tap the water behind the boat to push it along. Keep it off the reeds. Leaves are free, so try as often as you like._
- `s1-step2`: Walk with Moss to the lantern by the old wall. · _Hush’s post is on the north side of the Grove, by the tumbled stone wall. Go on a quiet evening._
- `s1-step3`: Walk with Moss to his own lantern, by the pond. · _Go at dusk, just after sunset. Moss likes the light then._
- `s1-step4`: Moss won’t visit the Long-Grass post while it stands empty. He’ll tell you when. · _This story finishes in chapter 9. Carry on with the main story until then._

#### `reader:s1-ask` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-ask)

_The Keepers wander home with their empty cups. Three lanterns glow. The fourth post, by the long grass, is dark._  
_Moss hasn’t gone back up the hill. He sits by your boot, staring at the dark post._  
**Moss**: Twenty-two years, that post has been dark.  
**You**: That’s a long time.  
**Moss**: Not tonight. Ask me something else. Anything else.  
**Moss**: I told you the tale. A tale is only the part that fits in a song. The rest doesn’t fit anywhere.  
**Moss**: At each post I keep one small memory that isn’t in the tale. Four posts. Four small things.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): oral history: how spoken stories carry the past  
> **Moss**: Nobody wrote the old days down. They were kept like this: one of us telling the next one.  
> _People do this too. When someone tells what they remember and others pass it on, it’s called oral history._  
> _Oral means spoken. Long before there were books, most people kept their past this way._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what oral history is: memories passed on by telling, before and alongside writing._

**Moss**: Hazel used to walk round them with me every Lantern Night. Slowly. I’d like to do it again.  
**Moss**: At my pace. I am older than the trees, and the trees are not in a hurry either.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): a quarter of the speed takes four times as long  
> _Moss walks at a quarter of your speed. That means he covers one step for every four of yours._  
> _On your own, you could reach the stream lantern in 3 minutes. How long will it take beside Moss?_  
> - ✅ 12 minutes → Twelve. Four times as slow means four times as long. Three, four times over. You’ve been listening.
> - 7 minutes → You added four. Slowness doesn’t add, I’m afraid. It multiplies. Four lots of three minutes: 3, 6, 9, 12. Twelve minutes. Bring patience.
> - 3 minutes → If I walked at your speed, yes. I don’t. I never have. A quarter of the speed takes four times as long. Four lots of three: twelve minutes.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out that a quarter of the speed means four times the time (4 × 3 = 12)._


**Choices**

- **Walk beside him.**
    - You walk. It takes a long time to cross the grass. Halfway, you realise that is the point.
    - **Moss**: Good. You didn’t sigh. Hazel sighed for the first ten years.
- **Offer to carry him.**
    - **Moss**: Certainly not. I have legs. They are very short and very old, but they are mine.
    - **Moss**: Ask me again another year. I may surprise us both.
- **Ask which post comes first.**
    - **Moss**: Briar’s, by the stream. She hated waiting more than anything, except being sad.
    - **Moss**: She was very good at not being sad. It was most of what she did.

#### `reader:s1-briar` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-briar)

_The stream lantern hisses softly in the damp air. Moss looks at it for a long while before he speaks._  
**Moss**: Briar. The songs call her Joy. That’s a big word. Mostly, she laughed.  
**Moss**: She laughed at frogs. At weather. At me, constantly. When she was scared, she laughed loudest of all.  
**Moss**: Fading is how an Eternal dies. When it was Briar’s time, she walked up the hill laughing.  
**Moss**: She said she’d thought of a joke and wanted to tell it to the view.  
_For a moment the lantern light turns old and gold, and you hear a voice that isn’t his._  
**Briar**: If I’m not down by supper, eat mine. If I’m not down by spring, plant something nice.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): reading between the lines: what a character really means  
> _Briar says, “If I’m not down by spring, plant something nice.” What did she really mean?_  
> - ✅ She knew she wasn’t coming back, and wanted to be remembered with something lovely. → Yes. She knew. She just said it in a way that let us smile. That was Briar all over.
> - She wanted someone to tidy the garden. → Ha. She’d have liked that answer. But no. She knew she wasn’t coming down that hill. She asked for a flower so we’d have something lovely to remember her by.
> - She was hungry and wanted her supper saved. → She was always hungry, to be fair. But listen to the second half. “If I’m not down by spring.” She knew she wouldn’t be. The flower was her way of saying goodbye gently.
> _For grown-ups: Inferred a character’s real meaning from what she said, not just the words themselves._

**Moss**: Nobody had to plant anything. Something yellow came up where she sat. The first memorial flower.  
**Moss**: Typical. She always got the last word.  
**Moss**: I never found out what the joke was. Some evenings I sit up there and try to guess.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, notice): remembering someone who has died  
> **Moss**: When someone you love dies, the missing doesn’t stop. It changes shape.  
> **Moss**: Telling their stories helps. So does a flower. So does guessing at their jokes, on a quiet evening.  
> _For grown-ups: Heard that sadness after a death is normal, and that remembering together helps._


#### `reader:s1-boats-ask` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-boats-ask)

**Moss**: She had a game. Of course she had a game. Leaf-boats, each with a candle stub a grown-up lit, from her post down to mine.  
**Moss**: If yours reached the pond still lit, you had to laugh out loud. Those were her rules.  
**Moss**: There were eleven rules. Nine of them were about laughing.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 1.OA.A.1, ask): subtraction word problem within 20  
> **Moss**: Eleven rules. Nine about laughing. So how many were about anything else at all?  
> - ✅ 2 → Two. One said only a grown-up lights the candles. One was about not pushing Moss in. She needed that one.
> - 20 → That’s eleven and nine added together. We want what’s left over. Start at nine and count up to eleven: ten, eleven. Two rules. One was about not pushing me in.
> - 3 → Close. Count up from nine: ten, eleven. That’s two steps, so two rules. One said only a grown-up lights the candles, and one was about not pushing me in.
> _For grown-ups: Solved 11 − 9 = 2 by counting on from the smaller number._

_He brings out a dock leaf from somewhere, folded very neatly, with a stub of candle standing in it._  
**Moss**: I have never once got one to the pond. Not in all the years. She said I watched it too hard.  
**Moss**: I’ve been watching it less hard, on purpose, for a very long time. It hasn’t helped.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-5-ETS1-2, ask): why a wide, flat boat is steadier  
> **Moss**: Before you fold yours. Which boat is less likely to tip over: wide and flat, or tall and narrow?  
> - ✅ Wide and flat → Right. A wide boat spreads its weight over lots of water, and its low middle is hard to tip. Tall and narrow wobbles at the first ripple. Ask me how I know.
> - Tall and narrow → That’s how I fold mine. Every year. Watch what happens to it. Wide and flat is steadier. The weight is spread out and low down, so a ripple can’t tip it.
> _For grown-ups: Compared two boat designs and explained why a wide, low boat is more stable._


**Choices**

- **Fold yours wide and flat.**
    - **Moss**: Briar’s way. Flat as a pancake and twice as smug.
- **Give yours a sail.**
    - **Moss**: A sail. Nobody has ever thought of a sail.
    - **Moss**: …I don’t know if that’s allowed. She’d have allowed it. She’d have been furious she didn’t think of it first.

#### `reader:s1-boats` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-boats)

_The stream takes the boats as if it has been waiting all year. Moss’s tips over at once, with great dignity._  
**Moss**: Every year.  
_Yours wobbles past the stepping stones, spins once at the reed bend, and slides out onto the pond._  
_The candle is still lit._  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): what a flame needs to keep burning  
> **Moss**: Briar always said a flame needs three things: something to burn, air, and heat.  
> **Moss**: Splash it, and the water cools it and shuts out the air. Keep it dry, and it carries on. Like yours.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that fire needs fuel, air and heat, and that water puts it out by cooling it and blocking air._

_Your Nubbin, who has run beside it the whole way along the bank, sits down hard in amazement._  
**Moss**: Well? Rules are rules.  
_You laugh. It comes out louder than you meant, and it rolls right across the water._  
**Moss**: …There. That’s the sound. I haven’t heard it at this post in a very long time.  
_Behind you, Moss’s boat drifts back to the bank upside down, as if it would quite like another go._  
**Moss**: I have a theory about Briar’s joke, you know. That my boat never reaches the pond.  
**Moss**: She folded my first one. I’ve used her fold ever since. I’m beginning to suspect the fold.  
_He looks at the upside-down boat with deep suspicion._  
**Moss**: Next Lantern Night. And the one after. Somebody ought to. It’s a tradition now.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): traditions: how a group remembers together  
> _Something a family or a town does again every year, the same way, is called a tradition._  
> **Moss**: Traditions are how a valley remembers without anybody having to write it down. Boats included.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what a tradition is and how it helps a community keep its memories._


#### `reader:s1-wall-ask` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-wall-ask)

_A few evenings later, Moss is waiting at the gate. He has clearly waited a while, and would like you to know it._  
**Moss**: The old wall next. Hush’s. Feed them first. I’ll wait. I am, you’ll have noticed, good at it.  
_Byte is leaning on the wall with a notebook, trying to look casual. It clearly took a lot of planning._  
**Byte**: Is he talking? Right now? Ask him something only he would know. For proof.  
**Moss**: Tell the pale one it’s the other shoe this time.  
_Byte looks down at both shoes. Byte goes very quiet, and leaves._  
**Moss**: Hush was the quiet one. People think quiet means resting. Hush wasn’t resting. She was listening.  
**You**: Listening to what?  
**Moss**: Whatever you weren’t saying. Come on. North side of the Grove. Mind the step.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, ask): compass directions: north, east, south, west  
> **Moss**: Hush’s wall is on the north side. Face north with me. Now: which way is east?  
> - ✅ To my right → Right. Facing north, east is on your right hand, west on your left, and south behind you. Going round clockwise: north, east, south, west. Hush’s post never moves, which helps.
> - To my left → That’s west. Easy to swap them. Face north and hold out your right arm. That points east. Clockwise: north, east, south, west.
> - Behind me → Behind you is south, the opposite of north. East is on your right. Go round clockwise: north, east, south, west.
> _For grown-ups: Practised the four compass directions and where east and west are when facing north._


#### `reader:s1-hush` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-hush)

_The lantern by the old wall burns lower than the others, steady and quiet, like a candle in an empty room._  
**Moss**: After the White Year, some of the stilled ones never moved again. They sat where they were put down.  
**Moss**: The rest of us were busy. Mending. Hatching. Getting the valley warm. Hush sat with the still ones.  
**Moss**: Every one. Every day. She didn’t try to wake them. She kept them company, so they weren’t alone.  
_Moss stops. When he goes on, his voice is smaller._  
**Moss**: The last of them faded on a winter morning, under this wall. Faded means it died. Hush was beside it.  
_The memory rises slowly, like breath on cold glass._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, notice): similes  
> _“Like breath on cold glass” is a simile. A simile compares two things using “like” or “as”._  
> _It tells you how the memory appears: slowly, softly, and a little bit misty._  
> _For grown-ups: Spotted a simile and explained what the comparison shows._

**Hush**: That’s the last of them, Moss. Somebody kept them. I think I’d like to be kept now.  
**Moss**: She called grief a kind of keeping. You hold it, so it doesn’t get dropped all over the valley.  
**Moss**: She held it a very long time. Then she put it down. I’ve never thought that was weakness.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): think question: what grief is for  
> **Moss**: Hush called grief a kind of keeping. I’ve wondered about that for a long time. What do you think she meant?  
> - Being sad about someone is a way of still loving them. → That’s a fair answer. I think so too. The sad part is the love, with nowhere to go.
> - Somebody has to remember them, so they’re not forgotten. → That’s a fair answer. Hush would have nodded at that. Keeping them in mind is keeping them.
> - I’m not sure. Grief just feels heavy. → That’s an honest answer, and honest is always fair. It is heavy. Maybe that’s why she called it keeping. You carry it, and one day you’re allowed to put it down.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what grief means; there was no wrong answer._


#### `reader:s1-pond-ask` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-pond-ask)

_Moss is on the hill when you look for him, and by the pond when you stop looking. That seems to be how it works._  
**Moss**: Mine. I’ve been putting it off. Not because it’s sad. Because it’s mine, and I talk about myself too much.  
**Moss**: Sit. No, closer. There. That’s where she sat.  
**You**: Hazel?  
**Moss**: Hazel. Before she was anybody’s gran. Before she was anybody’s anything.  
_The pond lantern hums. Above it, the sky has gone the deep blue that comes just after sunset. It is dusk._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-ESS1-1, notice): dusk: why the sky stays light after sunset  
> **Moss**: Dusk. My favourite light. The Sun has already gone below the edge of the world.  
> **Moss**: But its light still glows in the sky for a while after. That’s why it isn’t dark straight away.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that dusk is the time after sunset when sunlight still lights the sky._


**Choices**

- **Sit where she sat.**
    - The grass there is worn, very slightly, in the shape of someone who came back to the same spot for forty years.
    - **Moss**: She’d say you were sitting in it wrong. You aren’t.
- **Ask him why he chose her.**
    - **Moss**: Patience. I’m getting there. Rush me and I’ll start from the Spring Age, and we’ll be here till May.

#### `reader:s1-hazel` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-hazel)

_The pond lantern burns clean and warm. Moss looks at the water, not at you._  
**Moss**: She came here young. Clever, cross, and in a terrible hurry. She wanted the Nubbins to love her by Tuesday.  
**Moss**: They didn’t. So she sat by this pond every day and grumbled to the nearest thing that would listen. Me.  
**Moss**: I didn’t say a word, all winter. She wriggled and fidgeted the whole time, and never once asked me to talk.  
_The light turns old and gold. Snow, in the memory, and a young woman on a frozen bank, in a coat too big for her._  
**Young Hazel**: I haven’t the faintest idea what I’m doing. I don’t suppose you have either.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): two points of view on the same event  
> _To young Hazel, it looks as if Moss is just sitting there, doing nothing. What was he really doing all winter?_  
> - ✅ Waiting to see if she’d keep coming back. → Exactly. From where she sat, nothing was happening. From where I sat, everything was.
> - Ignoring her. → That’s how it looked to her. It’s a fair guess from her side of the pond. From my side, I was watching to see if she’d keep coming back. Same winter, two very different stories.
> - Sleeping. → I have been accused of that. Often. No. I was watching to see if she’d keep coming back. She thought nothing was happening. Something was.
> _For grown-ups: Compared two characters’ points of view on the same events._

**Moss**: Then, at the first thaw, I looked up at her and said, “You’ll do.”  
**Moss**: She laughed so hard she fell in the pond. It was only ankle-deep. She has never let me forget it.  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-ESS2-3, notice): ice floats, so ponds freeze from the top  
> **Moss**: Ice floats. So a pond freezes from the top down, like a lid.  
> **Moss**: Under the lid the water stays liquid, and the fish carry on all winter. Hazel landed on a thin bit of lid.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that ice floats, so ponds freeze from the top and life survives underneath._

**Young Hazel**: You sat with me all winter. Why?  
**Moss**: Because you kept coming back. That’s all it ever takes. Most people don’t.  
_The memory fades. The pond is a pond again, with your face in it, and his._  
**Moss**: Her third rule. Sit down next to them and wait. She thinks she invented it. I’ve never had the heart to say.  

#### `reader:s1-wait` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-wait)

_You look across the Grove at the fourth post, by the long grass. Moss doesn’t._  
**Moss**: Not that one. Not while it’s empty.  
_Over the lane, some children are skipping and chanting, in the sing-song voice children keep for old rhymes._  
_“Cinder held them close and tight, / kept them warm one winter night;”_  
_“when the morning came to call, / they did not move at all.”_  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.2.4, ask): rhyme and rhythm help words last  
> _That rhyme is older than anyone in the valley. What helps children remember it for so long?_  
> - ✅ The rhyme and the steady beat. → Yes. Tight and night, call and all. Rhymes and a beat are easy to remember, especially when you skip to them. That’s why so many old stories were kept as songs and rhymes.
> - It’s written in a famous book. → Nobody wrote it down. The children learn it from older children, by ear. What makes it stick is the rhyme and the beat: tight, night, call, all. You can skip to it.
> - It’s very long. → It’s actually very short, which helps. But the real glue is the rhyme and the beat. Tight, night, call, all. Words that rhyme and bounce are easy to remember.
> _For grown-ups: Noticed how rhyme and rhythm make a verse easy to remember and pass on._

**Moss**: They’ve got the words right. They’ve got her wrong.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, ask): think question: can a story be true and still unfair?  
> **Moss**: The words are right. She did hold them too tight. So can a story be true, and still be unfair to someone?  
> - Yes. If it only tells the worst part. → That’s a fair answer. A true story with the rest left out can still mislead.
> - No. If it’s true, it’s fair. → That’s a fair answer too. Truth matters most. I’d only say: a true part isn’t always the whole. That’s why I want you to hear the rest.
> - It depends who’s telling it. → That’s a fair answer. Historians say the same: always ask who told it, and what they left out.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether a true story can still be one-sided; there was no wrong answer._

**Moss**: Everybody remembers her winter. Somebody should remember the rest of her. I’ll tell you. When it’s home.  
**You**: When what’s home?  
**Moss**: Feed them. Go to bed. Ask me again when I ask you.  

#### `reader:s1-cinder` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-cinder)

_The evening after you buried the tin. Frost reaches out of the long grass in thin white fingers._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, notice): metaphors  
> _Frost doesn’t really have fingers. “Thin white fingers” is a metaphor: it says one thing is another._  
> _It makes the frost feel as if it is reaching for something. Which it is._  
> _For grown-ups: Recognised a metaphor and what feeling it creates._

_Moss is waiting at your door. He is so faint you can see the doorstep through him._  
**Moss**: All four home. I said I’d tell you. You may carry me. I’m told it’s an honour. Don’t make a face.  
_You carry him to the fourth post. He weighs almost nothing, like a bird’s nest with opinions._  
**Moss**: Everyone remembers Cinder in the snow. I’m going to tell you about the day before.  
**Moss**: Hot. The hottest day anyone could remember. The Nubbins lay flat in the long grass like dropped gloves.  
**Moss**: Cinder spent the whole day carrying them into the shade, one at a time. They kept wandering back out.  
_The light goes old and gold, then red at the edges. You smell hot grass and hear a voice, fierce and fond._  
**Cinder**: Out of the sun, you silly things. You’ll burn. Come here. Come here. I’ve got you.  

> **Lesson** (science, K-PS3-1, ask): why shade is cooler than sunshine  
> **Moss**: She knew what she was doing, carrying them into the shade. Why is shade cooler?  
> - ✅ Something blocks the sunlight, so less of it reaches the ground to warm it. → Yes. Sunlight warms whatever it lands on. Block it, and that patch stays cooler.
> - Shade is made of cold air. → It feels that way. But shade is just a place the sunlight can’t reach. Sunlight warms whatever it lands on. Under a tree, less sun lands, so it stays cooler.
> - Trees blow cool wind. → A nice idea. Trees do rustle. But the cool comes from blocking the sunlight. Sunlight warms the ground it lands on. Shade is where it can’t land.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that shade is cooler because it blocks sunlight that warms the ground._

**Moss**: She was never gentle. But she was never once tired of them. Not once, in all the years.  
**Moss**: That night the first snow came. In summer. And it didn’t stop.  
_The memory goes white, and is gone. Frost glitters at the foot of the post._  
**Moss**: Cinder-love, they call it now. A love that holds too tight. It’s fair. It isn’t all of her.  

#### `reader:s1-complete` · [main](../../../side/S01.md#s1-complete)

**Moss**: There. Now two of us remember her warm. That’s twice as many as yesterday.  
_He nudges something across the frost to your boot: an acorn on a loop of faded string, worn smooth as a pebble._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, notice): the life cycle of an oak  
> _An acorn is an oak tree’s seed. Planted, it sprouts into a seedling, then a young sapling, then an oak._  
> _An oak can live for hundreds of years and drop acorns of its own. Moss would approve of the pace._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the life cycle of an oak tree, from acorn to seedling to sapling to tree._

**Moss**: Hazel tied that on me, the spring after our first winter. The first gift anyone had given me in a very long time.  
**Moss**: Wear it. Then something of hers is walking about the Grove, and something of mine.  
_Hanging on the post is a soft hat with a little lantern stitched into the brim, and a note in big loud letters._  
_“SAW YOU TWO DOING THE ROUNDS IN THE DARK, SO I MADE YOU THIS. IT PROBABLY DOESN’T EXPLODE. —SPARKS”_  
_That night you write it all down: four posts, four small things. You call the pages The Tale of the Four._  
_Now there is a written record, as well as Moss’s memory._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, ask): spoken memories and written records  
> _Moss remembers. Your journal records. Why might a valley want both?_  
> - ✅ Writing lasts even when the teller is gone, and telling keeps the feeling in it. → Yes. Historians use both: what people wrote, and what people remember and tell.
> - It doesn’t. Writing is always better. → Writing does last. But a written page can’t answer questions or do Briar’s laugh. Historians use both: what people wrote down, and what people remember and tell.
> - It doesn’t. Telling is always better. → Telling is wonderful. But tellers grow old, and memories slip. Writing it down keeps it safe. Historians use both, side by side.
> _For grown-ups: Compared oral history with written records and why both matter._

_Moss falls asleep on your windowsill before you finish, which he has never done before._  

### Glossary

- **oral history**: The past as people remember it and tell it out loud, passed from one person to the next.
- **memorial**: Something that helps people remember someone who has died, like a flower or a stone.
- **dignity**: Staying calm and proud, even when something silly or hard is happening.
- **tradition**: Something a group does again and again, often every year, in the same way.
- **casual**: Relaxed and not bothered, or pretending to be.
- **grief**: The deep sadness you feel when someone you love has died.
- **simile**: A comparison that uses “like” or “as”, such as “quiet as a mouse”.
- **dusk**: The time just after sunset, when the sky is still glowing but getting dark.
- **grumbled**: Complained in a low, cross voice.
- **thaw**: When ice and snow melt because the weather is warming up.
- **metaphor**: Saying one thing is another to describe it, such as “the frost’s white fingers”.
- **record**: Something written or saved so that people can find out about it later.
