# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S4: The Wellspring

## S4. The Wellspring

- `s4-step1`: Check the water line Hazel painted on the rock by the falls. · _The falls are past the pond, on the Hollow side. Tap the white mark on the rock beside the pool._
- `s4-step2`: Send a swimmer up the outflow to see what’s above the falls. · _Choose Swim at the top of the pool. If none of your Nubbins can swim yet, Marina gives one a lesson first._
- `s4-step3`: Walk the ledge path behind the falls with Marina. · _Meet Marina at the pool in the evening. Stay close behind her on the ledge, and don’t stop to look down._
- `s4-step4`: Open the sluice gate on the Circuit’s dam, after dark. · _Go at night with Marina and a strong Nubbin. Move on the snore, freeze on the whistle. The sluice sticks._

#### `reader:s4-pools` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-pools)

**Marina**: You won. Good. Come and see something that isn’t winning.  
_Past the ridge, Marina’s tide pools step down the rock like a row of bowls. Half of them are dry._  
_In the rest, the anemones have shut up tight, like fists._  
**Marina**: They get what the stream gives them. This summer it’s giving them less. And what does come down is cold.  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-ESS1-1, notice): tides and the Moon’s pull  
> **Marina**: Real tide pools sit on the seashore. The sea fills them up, then drains away, about twice a day.  
> **Marina**: That rise and fall is called the tide. The Moon’s pull causes it.  
> **Marina**: Mine are too high up the rock for any tide. They only get the stream. So when the stream fails, so do they.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the Moon’s pull causes the tides, which rise and fall about twice a day on most coasts._

**Marina**: Cold. In July.  
**Marina**: Hazel painted a line on the rock by the falls, years back. Where the water ought to come to. Go and look.  
**You**: Why not look yourself?  
**Marina**: I’ve looked forty times. I want someone else to tell me I’m wrong.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): when worry wants a second opinion  
> _Marina has checked forty times. Why do you think she wants someone else to look?_  
> - ✅ When you’re worried, a second pair of eyes helps you know if it’s real. → That’s it. After forty looks you stop trusting your own eyes. Worry does that.
> - She’s too lazy to walk to the falls. → Forty times. To the falls. Lazy. She’s worried. When you’re very worried, asking someone else to check helps you know what’s real.
> - She wants to win an argument. → I’d love to lose this one. She’s worried. Asking someone else to look is a good way to check whether a worry is real.
> _For grown-ups: Recognised that asking someone else to check can help when we are worried._

**Marina**: Take a Nubbin. They like the falls. Mind the moss on the rocks. Not that Moss. The slippery kind.  

#### `reader:s4-line` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-line)

_The falls are loud and white. On the dark rock beside the pool, someone has painted a thick white line._  
_The water sits a hand’s width below it. Above the water the rock is pale and dry, where it should be dark and wet._  
_Your Nubbin dips a paw in, pulls it out, and looks at you as if you did this._  
**Marina**: A hand. A whole hand.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.MD.A.1, do): measuring length in centimetres  
> **Marina**: Mind you, a hand isn’t a proper measure. Everybody’s hand is a different size.  
> **Marina**: Here. Hazel’s measuring stick. Put the zero at the water and read the mark at the white line.  
> _(Line the stick up and read the number at the white line.)_  
> **Marina**: Ten centimetres. Write it down. Numbers don’t argue, Hazel used to say.  
> Check: `edu:measure:water-line:10cm`
> _For grown-ups: Measured a length in centimetres with a ruler, starting from zero._

_Marina has followed you after all. She is standing at the edge of the pool with her shoes in her hand._  
**Marina**: Something upstream is taking it. Something big enough to take a hand of water off the falls.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): reading signs that water levels have changed  
> _Wet rock looks darker than dry rock. The pale band is where the water used to reach and doesn’t any more._  
> _People who study rivers look for bands like this. The rock remembers where the water was._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned to read marks on rock as evidence of a change in water level._


#### `reader:s4-lesson` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-lesson)

**Marina**: Hazel traded me fruit for swimming lessons. For her Nubbins, not her. She swam like a dropped teapot.  
**Marina**: Fossil swam sideways, on purpose. Never once the way I taught her. I still check that corner of the pool.  
**Marina**: Lesson one: they float better than you’d think. Lesson two: they don’t think, so let them.  
_She wades in to her knees and holds your Nubbin under the chin, the way you’d steady a cup._  
_It kicks once, amazed, and doesn’t sink._  
**Marina**: There. Now up the outflow, where the water leaves the pool. Not far. Just far enough to see what’s up there.  
**Marina**: And stay where I can see you. That’s the rule for Nubbins and Keepers alike.  

> **Lesson** (health, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, notice): water safety  
> **Marina**: Real rule, for real water: never swim without a grown-up watching. Not once. Not even a good swimmer.  
> **Marina**: Rivers and pools can be colder, deeper and faster than they look. Cold water steals your strength quickly.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the water-safety rule: only swim with a grown-up watching, as water can be colder and deeper than it looks._


**Choices**

- **Wade in after them.**
    - You get in up to your waist and stop, gasping. It is very, very cold.
    - **Marina**: Lesson three: Keepers sink. Out you get.
- **Stay on the bank and trust them.**
    - **Marina**: Good. That’s the hardest stroke there is: staying on the bank.

#### `reader:s4-dam` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-dam)

_Your swimmer comes back down the outflow and shakes itself all over you, the way Nubbins do when water has been rude._  
_Caught in its fur: a grey thread, and a flake of fresh mortar._  
_Round the bend upstream you find it. A wall of new grey stone across the stream, with a sluice gate in it, shut._  
_Behind the wall the water lies flat and still and very blue, all the way up to the Circuit’s new swimming lanes._  

> **Lesson** (science, K-ESS2-2, ask): how a dam changes the water downstream  
> **Marina**: So. Why are my pools drying up?  
> - ✅ The dam holds the water back, so less flows down to the falls and the pools. → Yes. A wall that holds back a river is a dam. The lake it makes behind it is a reservoir. Everything downstream gets whatever the dam lets through. Right now, that’s not much.
> - The sun has dried it all up. → The sun takes a little, every summer. Not a whole hand. The dam holds the water back, so less flows down to us. The lake behind it is called a reservoir.
> - The Nubbins have been drinking it. → They do drink a lot. Not that much. The dam holds the water back, so less flows down to us. The lake behind it is a reservoir.
> _For grown-ups: Explained how a dam holds back water and changes the land and life downstream._

_Each lane has a rope, a number, and a neat sign: NO SPLASHING._  
**Marina**: No splashing. It’s water. Splashing is what it’s for.  
**Marina**: A swimming course. My pools are dying for a swimming course.  
**Marina**: I’ve written to the Circuit three times. I got three forms back. Received. Filed. Very tidy.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): temperature difference (subtraction within 100)  
> _Marina dips her old thermometer in the stream. It reads 8°C (46°F)._  
> **Marina**: Hazel wrote down the July water every year. Last July: 17°C (63°F). How many degrees Celsius colder is it now?  
> - ✅ 9 degrees → Nine. 17 take away 8. That’s not summer water. That’s spring water.
> - 25 degrees → That’s 17 and 8 added together. We want the difference. Count up from 8 to 17: two to get to 10, then seven more. Nine degrees colder.
> - 11 degrees → Close. Count up from 8: two to get to 10, then seven more to 17. Two and seven is nine. Nine degrees colder.
> _For grown-ups: Found the difference between two temperatures (17 − 8 = 9) by counting on._


#### `reader:s4-ledge-ask` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-ledge-ask)

**Marina**: Before we do anything about that wall, I want to show you something. Not because it helps.  
**Marina**: Because somebody besides me ought to know the way. Hazel knew it. Hazel’s away.  
_Beside the falls, half hidden by ferns, a narrow ledge runs straight into the roar._  
_Behind the curtain of water, the rock has been worn back into a long, dark hollow._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-ESS2-1, notice): erosion: how water carves rock  
> **Marina**: Falling water wears rock away. That’s called erosion. Soft rock wears away faster than hard rock.  
> **Marina**: Here the top is hard and the rock beneath is soft. Over a very long time the soft part washed out, and left a hollow.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how erosion by falling water carves soft rock faster than hard rock, making hollows behind waterfalls._

**Marina**: The ledge path. Back to the rock, eyes on my heels.  
**Marina**: Rule five. Don’t be me at your age. Rule four is “Don’t be Sparks”, and at your age I was Sparks, with longer hair.  
**Marina**: Don’t look at the water. It doesn’t like being stared at.  

#### `reader:s4-ledge` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-ledge)

_Behind the falls the world turns to noise and spray. The rock under your hand is wet and soft with moss._  
_Halfway along, the ledge bends into the dark. From round the bend comes a breath of air: warm, and smelling of stone._  
**Marina**: That’s the spring breathing. Where the first ones came up, if you believe Moss. I do, mostly.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): warm springs: heat from under the ground  
> **Marina**: There are warm springs in the real world too. Rain soaks down deep, where the rock is hot.  
> **Marina**: The water warms up down there and rises back to the surface. Where warm water meets cold air, you see a mist.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that warm springs are heated by hot rock deep underground._

_Marina stops at the bend. She doesn’t go on._  
**Marina**: Further is for when it’s needed. Hazel’s rule. I’ve kept it twenty years.  
**Marina**: But now you know the way this far. That’s all a path is, really. Somebody else knowing it.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, ask): passing on knowledge of places: walking and maps  
> **Marina**: So. What’s the best way to make sure a path like this is never forgotten?  
> - ✅ Walk it with someone, and draw a map as well. → Both. Walking teaches your feet. A map keeps it when there’s nobody left to walk it with you.
> - Walk it with someone. → That’s how Hazel taught me. It’s the best way to learn it. But add a map as well. A map remembers the way when there’s nobody left to walk it with you.
> - Draw a map. → A map is a good keeper. It doesn’t forget. But walk it too. A map can’t tell you which rock wobbles. Both together is best.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how knowledge of a place is passed on, by showing someone and by maps._


#### `reader:s4-sluice` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-sluice)

**Marina**: Three letters. Three forms. I’m done writing.  
**Marina**: We’re not breaking anything. Breaking is loud, and it gets you talked about. We’re opening a gate that ought to be open.  
_At night the dam is just a dark wall and the sound of held water. The sluice is a great wooden door in new stone._  
_Beside it, in a tidy hut, the Circuit’s night steward sits bolt upright in his chair, fast asleep, clipboard on his chest._  
_He snores in a perfect rhythm. In, out, and a thin little whistle. The clipboard rises and falls._  
**Marina**: I’ve watched him three nights. Move on the snore. Freeze on the whistle. The whistle’s when he’s nearest to waking.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.OA.C.5, ask): a repeating pattern and its rule  
> **Marina**: In, out, whistle. In, out, whistle. If we count every sound, what will the 9th sound be?  
> - ✅ Whistle → Whistle. Every third sound is a whistle: 3rd, 6th, 9th. So on the 9th, we freeze.
> - In → Let’s count it: in 1, out 2, whistle 3, in 4, out 5, whistle 6, in 7, out 8, whistle 9. Every third sound is a whistle. 3, 6, 9. Freeze on 9.
> - Out → Count it with me: in, out, whistle is 1, 2, 3. Then 4, 5, 6. Then 7, 8, 9. The whistle lands on every multiple of 3. The 9th is a whistle. Freeze.
> _For grown-ups: Found the rule of a repeating pattern (every third sound) and used it to predict the 9th._

**You**: Has he ever woken?  
**Marina**: Not once in three nights. I’m still not taking chances.  
_You creep to the sluice on a snore. Your Nubbin creeps beside you, one foot raised, and freezes on the whistle like a garden ornament._  
_It holds the pose through three more whistles. It is still holding it when the snore comes back._  
**Marina**: You can put your foot down now, pet.  
_It puts its foot down. It looks as if it would have preferred to win._  
_You try the sluice on the next snore. It doesn’t move. It is, without any doubt, stuck._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, ask): think question: is it right to open the gate without asking?  
> _Marina asked three times, politely, and nobody listened. The gate isn’t hers._  
> _Is it right to open it without permission? What do you think?_  
> - Yes. The pools are dying, and the gate should never have been shut. → That’s a fair answer, and it’s my answer too. Living things come before tidy swimming lanes.
> - Only if we leave a note saying what we did and why. → That’s a fair answer. Doing it openly, with your name on it, is a kind of honesty.
> - No. We should keep asking, louder, and get more people to ask too. → That’s a fair answer. Lots of things change that way. I’m just not sure the pools can wait.
> _For grown-ups: Weighed rules, fairness and urgency in a hard choice; there was no wrong answer._


**Choices**

- **Lift, then push.**
    - You lift, and push, the way the Grove gate taught you. The sluice groans, gives, and swings.
    - **Marina**: Where did you learn that?
    - **You**: Home.
- **Let your strongest Nubbin have a go.**
    - Your Nubbin puts its back into it and shoves, and nothing happens. Then it slips, lifts by accident, and the whole thing swings.
    - **Marina**: Hazel’s trick. Even the Nubbins know it.
- **Pin a polite note to the gate first.**
    - You write: “Sluice opened for the tide pools, with thanks, from Hazel’s Grove.” Then you lift, and push, and it swings.
    - **Marina**: Polite. He’ll hate that more than anything.
    - In the hut, the steward’s hand rises in his sleep, pats the air where a note might be, and puts it in a tray that isn’t there.

#### `reader:s4-water` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-water)

_Water pours through the gap, dark and loud._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): water flows downhill  
> _Which way does the water go, now the gate is open?_  
> - ✅ Downhill, towards the falls. → Yes. Gravity pulls water down, so it always flows to the lowest place it can reach. Downhill means the falls.
> - Uphill, back to the swimming lanes. → Water can’t flow uphill on its own. Gravity pulls it down. It always runs to the lowest place it can reach. Here, that’s the falls and the pools.
> - It stays where it is. → It stayed put while the gate held it. Now nothing does. Gravity pulls water downhill, to the lowest place it can reach: the falls and the pools.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that gravity makes water flow downhill to the lowest place it can reach._

_It goes tumbling off towards the falls as if it’s late for something._  
**Marina**: Listen.  
_Below you, the falls change their sound: fuller and louder, the way the rock remembers them._  
_In the hut, the steward stirs, says “Received,” quite clearly, and goes back to sleep._  
**Marina**: Filed, I expect.  
**Marina**: Somebody will shut it again, probably. Then somebody will open it again. That’s most of keeping.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.2, notice): theme: what “keeping” means in this story  
> _“That’s most of keeping.” Looking after something isn’t done once. It’s done again and again._  
> _That idea comes up all through the Grove’s stories. A big idea a story keeps returning to is its theme._  
> _For grown-ups: Noticed a theme (caring for something is ongoing work) that runs through the stories._


#### `reader:s4-complete` · [main](../../../side/S04.md#s4-complete)

_A week later the tide pools are full to the brim. The anemones have opened, as if surprised to be asked._  
**Marina**: Look at them. Showing off.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): how anemones survive in tide pools  
> **Marina**: Anemones look like flowers, but they’re animals. Those are tentacles, not petals.  
> **Marina**: When the water drops, they pull in tight to keep from drying out. Under water, they open up to catch bits of food.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that sea anemones are animals that close up to stay moist and open their tentacles to feed._

_In the deepest pool, wedged between two stones, something round and pearl-white is catching the light._  
_Your swimmer has seen it too. It dives before anyone can stop it: one kick, two, and a long silver string of bubbles._  
_It comes up with the thing balanced on its head, extremely pleased with itself, and sneezes. It is an egg._  
**Marina**: …Oh. Oh, you beauty.  
**Marina**: Once in a long while one comes down from behind the falls. Hazel saw one in forty years. It’s yours. You opened the gate.  
**Marina**: And your swimmers can dive here whenever they like. As deep as they want. I’ll teach them the rest.  
**Marina**: Take some shells for the Grove. Take the pool weed too; it’s lucky. It isn’t lucky. It smells. Take it anyway.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiplication as equal groups  
> **Marina**: I’ve filled 4 bags with 6 shells each. How many shells is that for the Grove?  
> - ✅ 24 → Twenty-four. Four sixes. Don’t spend them all at once.
> - 10 → That’s 4 and 6 added. But it’s 4 bags with 6 in each. 6, 12, 18, 24. Four lots of six is 24.
> - 20 → Close. Count in sixes, one for each bag: 6, 12, 18, 24. Four lots of six is 24.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied equal groups (4 × 6 = 24) by counting in sixes._

_Up at the dam, someone has hung a neat painted sign on the open sluice: OPEN BY ORDER. It doesn’t say whose._  

### Glossary

- **anemone**: A soft sea animal with a ring of tentacles that looks a bit like a flower.
- **tide**: The rise and fall of the sea, caused mostly by the pull of the Moon.
- **centimetre**: A small unit of length. A hundred centimetres make one metre.
- **outflow**: The place where water flows out of a pool or lake.
- **reservoir**: A lake made by holding water back, often behind a dam.
- **sluice**: A gate that can open or close to control how much water flows through.
- **erosion**: The slow wearing away of rock or soil by water, wind or ice.
- **rhythm**: A pattern of sounds or beats that repeats.

**Notes:** Hooks: water flow (gravity, erosion), dams and reservoirs, tide pools and the Moon’s pull on tides, warm springs. Marina explains that real tide pools are filled by the sea and hers sit too high for the tide, so the story’s stream-fed pools still make sense.
