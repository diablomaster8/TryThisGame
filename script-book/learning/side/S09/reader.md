# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S9: The Gate That Sticks

## S9. The Gate That Sticks

- `s9-step1`: Take the old trowel to Hazel. · _The old trowel lies by the cottage step, and Hazel is right there. Tap the trowel._
- `s9-step2`: Go down to the gate at dusk tomorrow. · _Corvin comes at sundown. Stand by the gate. You don’t need to open it._
- `s9-step3`: Be at the gate at first light, before the others come. · _Chapter 12 begins at dawn. Go to the gate before anything else._

#### `reader:s9-dusk` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-dusk)

_Dusk, on Hazel’s second evening home. The gate rattles._  
_Not the clean clack of someone who knows the trick. A long, unsure rattle. Then another._  
**Gran Hazel**: That’ll be him.  
_Hazel doesn’t get up from the step. She doesn’t look round. Her eyes are shut._  
**Gran Hazel**: Go on. I jammed it so I’d hear him. I’ve heard him. That’s my part done.  
_Corvin Vale is at the gate, with no hat, in a coat he has slept in. No runners behind him. No rows._  
_His hands are on the top bar, red and sore, like hands brought in out of the snow._  
**Corvin Vale**: I can feel my hands. I’d forgotten it hurts, warming up. Nobody mentions that it hurts.  

> **Lesson** (health, notice): why cold hands sting as they warm up  
> _When very cold hands warm up again, they often sting and ache for a while._  
> _That’s blood rushing back in, and the nerves waking up. It hurts, and then it passes._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that very cold hands can sting as they warm up, and why._

_His hand goes to his breast pocket, finds it flat, and comes away. He does it twice while you watch._  
_He lifts the latch and pushes, as anyone would. The gate shifts in its frame, and sticks._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): friction, and why lifting a dragging gate helps  
> _The gate is stuck. Its bottom edge drags on the ground, and the rubbing holds it still. That rubbing force is called friction._  
> _Why does lifting the gate before you push help?_  
> - ✅ Lifting takes its weight off the spot where it rubs. → Yes. Less weight pressing down means less friction. Then a push is enough to move it.
> - Lifting makes the gate lighter. → The gate weighs the same. But lifting takes its weight off the ground, so it rubs less. Less friction, easier push.
> - It’s magic. → It does feel like magic! But it’s friction: lift the weight off the rubbing spot, and a push can move it.
> _For grown-ups: Learned about friction, and why lifting a dragging gate makes it easier to push._

**Corvin Vale**: It used to open for me. I’d wait, and it would just swing. I thought that meant something.  
**Corvin Vale**: It was the cold from the stone in my pocket. It shrank the wood. I took it for a welcome.  
_He passes something through the bars, handle first: a trowel, its blade worn thin._  
**Corvin Vale**: Hers. I dug with it, under the long grass, twenty-two years ago. I’ve kept it oiled. Don’t say it’s from me.  

**Choices**

- **Why now?**
    - **Corvin Vale**: Because I can feel my hands. I didn’t have a reason before. I had a stone.
    - He goes down the lane. He looks back once, at the cottage, where Hazel sits with her eyes shut.
- **Do you want to come in?**
    - **Corvin Vale**: Want to? Yes.
    - **Corvin Vale**: Not tonight. I haven’t asked. If I ever come in, it’ll be because I asked.
    - He goes down the lane, and doesn’t look back. You think it costs him something not to.
- **Say nothing. Take the trowel.**
    - You take it. The handle is warm from his coat.
    - **Corvin Vale**: Thank you.
    - He goes down the lane. He looks back once, at the cottage, where Hazel sits with her eyes shut.

#### `reader:s9-trowel` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-trowel)

_Hazel turns the trowel over in her hands, and runs her thumb along the worn edge._  
**Gran Hazel**: I wondered where that went. I bought another. It was never as good.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): a lever turns on a pivot  
> **Gran Hazel**: Good trowel, this. When you dig, you push the handle down, and the blade lifts the soil up.  
> **Gran Hazel**: That makes it a lever: a bar that turns on a pivot. Here, the pivot is the edge of the hole.  
> **Gran Hazel**: A lever helps a small push lift something heavy. Corvin dug up a stone with this one.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what a lever and a pivot are, using a garden trowel._

**Gran Hazel**: You’ll want the story. You’ve been very good about not asking. It’s been unbearable to watch.  
**Gran Hazel**: He came to me at twenty-two. Clever. Quick. In a hurry, which I took for keen.  
**Gran Hazel**: He found the old clay tablets in this cottage. Older than me. Older than the wall. They showed him how to make a Nubbin keep very still, for ever.  
**Gran Hazel**: One winter he tried their method on my Nubbins, in my Grove. Quiet as stones, by morning.  
**Gran Hazel**: I stopped him. I said things. He said things. I was right, which is very tiring for everyone.  
**Gran Hazel**: That night he let strangers in through the gate. They took Nubbins, and the cold stone from under the fourth post.  
**Gran Hazel**: In the morning I wedged the gate.  
**You**: So he couldn’t get back in.  
**Gran Hazel**: No, love. So I’d hear him if he ever came back.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): work out a character’s motive  
> _Hazel wedged the gate so it would stick._  
> _Why did she do it?_  
> - ✅ So she’d hear Corvin if he ever came back. → Yes. A stuck gate rattles. She wanted to know if he came home. That’s her motive: her reason.
> - To keep Corvin out for ever. → That’s what you’d guess! But Hazel says no. She wanted to hear him if he came back. That’s her motive.
> - Because she couldn’t fix it. → Hazel can fix most things. She did it on purpose: so she’d hear him if he ever came back.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a character’s motive (her reason for doing something) from what she says._


**Choices**

- **How can you not hate him?**
    - **Gran Hazel**: I did, for a good while. Hating someone is heavy. I had Nubbins to look for, so I put it down.
    - **Gran Hazel**: I didn’t forgive him. I just needed both hands.
- **Did you find them? The ones he took?**
    - **Gran Hazel**: Most of them. It’s in the register. The rest I stopped looking for, one winter. I’ve never forgiven myself for stopping.
- **Sit down next to her. Say nothing.**
    - You sit. She leans, just a little, so your shoulders touch.
    - **Gran Hazel**: There. Better than anything you could have said. Don’t go getting big-headed about it.

#### `reader:s9-yours` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-yours)

_She sets the trowel on the step beside her, blade down, as if it might get away._  
**Gran Hazel**: He’ll be back tomorrow at dusk. Same time. He was always a creature of habit. That was half the trouble.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, notice): understand a saying (“creature of habit”)  
> _“A creature of habit” is a saying. It means someone who does the same things at the same times._  
> _It doesn’t mean Corvin is an animal. Though Hazel might enjoy you thinking so._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the meaning of a common saying, “a creature of habit”._

**Gran Hazel**: I’m not going down there. I’ve done my part. I listened for twenty-two years.  
**Gran Hazel**: It’s your gate now. Don’t look at me like that. I only wedged it.  
**You**: What do I say to him?  
**Gran Hazel**: Nothing, to start with. Rule three works on people too. It works on people worst of all.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, notice): patient listening helps people say hard things  
> **Gran Hazel**: When someone has something hard to say, the kindest thing is often to wait and listen.  
> **Gran Hazel**: If you rush to fill the quiet, they never get to say it.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that patient listening helps people say difficult things._


#### `reader:s9-ask` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-ask)

_Sundown. He is there before you, as if he never left. Perhaps he didn’t._  
**Corvin Vale**: I’ve been practising this. In the lane. Out loud. A sheep heard most of it.  
_He puts both hands on the top bar. He doesn’t try the latch._  
**Corvin Vale**: Twenty-two years ago I stood here and opened this gate for men with carts. I didn’t ask anyone.  
**Corvin Vale**: I’ve opened every gate since. Mine. The Circuit’s. Other people’s. I never once had to ask.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, notice): asking permission is a rule of respect  
> _Here is a basic rule of respect: you ask before you go into someone else’s home._  
> _Corvin has spent years not asking. Now he is going to try._  
> _For grown-ups: Talked about asking permission as a rule of respect._

**Corvin Vale**: So.  
_…_  
**Corvin Vale**: I would like to come in.  
_He says it to the gate, not to you. Then he looks up, and says it again, to you._  
**Corvin Vale**: I would like to come in. I don’t expect it. I’m only asking.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.2.4, ask): why a writer repeats a line  
> _Corvin says the same words twice: “I would like to come in.”_  
> _Why do you think he says it a second time?_  
> - ✅ The first time, he was too scared to look at you. → That’s how it reads. First he says it to the gate. The second time, he’s brave enough to say it to you.
> - He forgot he’d said it. → He’s been practising all day, so he hasn’t forgotten! The first time he said it to the gate. The second time, to you.
> - The sheep didn’t hear. → Ha. The sheep has heard plenty. He says it again because the first time was to the gate, and the second is to you.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why a writer repeats a line, and what the repetition shows._

**Corvin Vale**: I’ll wait here till first light. If it’s still shut then, I’ll understand. Better than anyone.  
_He lets go of the bar and steps back into the lane, out of the lamplight. Not far._  

#### `reader:s9-word` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-word)

_Word gets round, as it does in a valley this size. By supper, everyone knows Corvin Vale has asked to come in._  
**Ember**: He took Ash. I’m not telling you what to do. I’m telling you what he did. That’s different.  
**Ember**: And some of what sleeps in your long grass came out of his rows. They live on your side of that gate too.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): link a character’s point of view to what happened to her  
> _Everyone has a different point of view. Ember is the most careful about Corvin._  
> _Why do you think that is?_  
> - ✅ He took Ash from her. → Yes. What happens to us shapes how we see things. Ember was hurt the most, so she’s the most careful.
> - She doesn’t like gates. → Ha! Look back at her first line: “He took Ash.” She was hurt the most, so she’s the most careful.
> - She’s grumpy with everyone. → She can be! But here there’s a real reason: Corvin took Ash. Being hurt makes people careful.
> _For grown-ups: Linked a character’s point of view to what has happened to her._

**Luna**: Sable came out of his garden. I keep thinking that. And then I keep thinking: she came out.  
**Fern**: I’d let him in. Then I’d lie awake all night. Both of those are true, so I’m no use to you at all.  
**Clover**: The grey stays, you know. On him too, I expect. You can only make it lighter.  
**Byte**: For the record, I’ve done the numbers. People changing for good doesn’t happen very often.  
**Byte**: I’d still open the gate. I don’t know why. Don’t put that in the record.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, notice): fair decisions listen to every side  
> _Everyone gets a say, even when they disagree. Listening to every side before you choose is fair._  
> _But the choice is yours. It’s your gate now._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that fair decisions listen to every side, even when people disagree._

_Hazel says nothing at all. On her way out to the pond, she leaves the cottage door on the latch, which she never does._  

#### `reader:s9-gate` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-gate)

_First light. The pond is grey and still. Hazel sits beside it with a cup, her back to the gate._  
_Corvin is at the gate. He has been there all night._  
_There’s frost on his shoulders: the ordinary kind, the kind that melts._  
**Corvin Vale**: Hazel’s grandchild.  
_His hands rest on the top bar. They aren’t blue any more. They shake a little, the way warm hands do in the cold._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, notice): hinges, and why handles go far from them  
> _A gate swings on its hinges: the metal joints that hold it to the post._  
> _Push near the hinges and it barely moves. Push far from them, out by the latch, and it swings easily._  
> _That’s why latches and handles always go on the far side._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a push far from a hinge turns a gate more easily, which is why handles sit far from hinges._

_At the edge of the long grass, a grey ear turns towards his voice, and goes flat._  
**Corvin Vale**: I know who’s sitting by that pond. I know what I took from her. I’m asking anyway.  
_Behind you, Hazel drinks her tea and does not turn round. She is listening to every word._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): forgiveness and trust (think question)  
> _Corvin is sorry. He gave back the trowel. He asked, instead of taking._  
> _But the grey ear in the long grass still goes flat when he speaks._  
> _Can you forgive someone, but not trust them yet?_  
> - Yes. Forgiving and trusting are different. → That’s a thoughtful answer. Forgiving is letting go of the anger. Trust gets built slowly, one visit at a time.
> - No. If you forgive, you should trust them again. → That’s a fair answer, and a generous one. Some people feel that way. Others think trust takes longer than sorry.
> - It depends on what they did. → That’s a wise answer. Small things are easy to forgive and trust again. Big things, like taking Ash, can take years.
> - I’m not sure yet. → That’s an honest answer, and it’s allowed. Hazel took twenty-two years. You can take your time too.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about the difference between forgiving someone and trusting them (a think question with no wrong answer)._


**Choices**

- **Open it for him. Don’t show him how.**
    - You lift and push, and the gate opens. He watches your hands. You turn them so he can’t quite see.
    - **Corvin Vale**: Thank you.
    - **Corvin Vale**: I’ll have to knock, every time. That’s right. That’s more than right.
    - By the pond, Hazel lifts one hand a little and lets it fall. It might be a wave.
- **Leave it shut.**
    - You don’t move. He looks at you, then at the latch, and nods, as if you’ve told him the time.
    - **Corvin Vale**: Yes. All right. Yes.
    - **Corvin Vale**: It ought to cost something. I’d think less of this place if it didn’t.
    - **Corvin Vale**: Thank you for taking the trowel. I mean that.
    - He takes his hands off the gate one at a time. He goes down the lane, and is gone before Sparks arrives.
    - By the pond, Hazel finishes her tea. She sits a while longer than she needs to.

#### `reader:s9-complete` · [main](../../../side/S09.md#s9-complete)

_Sparks arrives first, at a run, as always. Then Fern. Then everyone: a whole valley coming up the lane._  
_Sparks, Fern, Marina, Clover, Pixel, Luna, Ember, Byte, Dusty and Nova._  
_One by one they lift and push. The gate opens for each of them, and sticks again behind them._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiply equal groups  
> _Every Keeper does two moves: lift, then push. Ten Keepers come through._  
> _How many moves is that altogether?_  
> - ✅ 20 → Yes. Ten groups of two: 10 × 2 = 20. Lift, push, twenty times.
> - 12 → That’s 10 + 2. But each of the ten Keepers does two moves: 10 × 2 = 20.
> - 10 → That’s one move each. But it’s lift AND push: two moves each, so 10 × 2 = 20.
> _For grown-ups: Used multiplication for equal groups (10 Keepers × 2 moves = 20)._

**Gran Hazel**: Good gate, that. I never did unwedge it.  
_She doesn’t say anything else about it, then or ever._  
_You understand that this is how she says it’s finished._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.2, notice): an object in a story can be a symbol  
> _In stories, an object can stand for a big idea. That’s called a symbol._  
> _Here, the gate that sticks stands for home: a place that hears who comes back, and lets in the ones who ask._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that an object in a story can be a symbol for a bigger idea._


### Glossary

- **friction**: A force that slows or stops things when they rub together.
- **trowel**: A small garden spade you hold in one hand.
- **lever**: A bar that turns on a point, to help you lift or move things.
- **motive**: The reason someone does something.
- **creature of habit**: Someone who likes to do the same things in the same way, every day.
- **sundown**: Sunset: the time when the sun goes down.
- **point of view**: The way one person sees and thinks about something.
- **hinge**: A metal joint that lets a gate or door swing open and shut.
- **forgive**: To stop being angry with someone for something they did.
- **symbol**: A thing that stands for an idea, like a heart standing for love.
