# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S11: The Blight

## S11. The Blight

- `s11-step1`: Go next door and look at the sick plum trees in Fern’s orchard. · _Go through the gap in the hedge, past Fern’s water butt. The sick trees are at the bottom of the slope._
- `s11-step2`: Check every tree in the orchard with Byte’s hand lens, and give it a label. · _Look at a leaf, the bark and the fruit of each tree. Then pick a label. Notice which way the wind blows._
- `s11-step3`: Catch one of the things riding the wind into the bottom row. · _Keep the sun in front of you (never look straight at it), so your shadow falls behind you. The bugs drop the moment your shadow touches them._
- `s11-step4`: Help Clover let her ladybirds go in the hedge where the beetles breed. · _Open Clover’s jar by the dead wild plum, or where it stood. Then step back and keep still._

#### `reader:s11-fern` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-fern)

_Fern is at your gate before breakfast. She holds a plum out on her flat hand, as if it might bite._  
**Fern**: Don’t touch it. Just look at it. Then tell me I’m imagining it.  
_The skin is freckled with black. When she tilts her hand, the plum caves in and pours out a soft grey dust._  
**Fern**: Three trees at the bottom of my orchard. Fine yesterday. Today they’re… that.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): some plant diseases are caused by a fungus, which spreads by spores  
> **Fern**: It’s the dust that frightens me. I think it’s spores. I think this is a fungus.  
> **Fern**: Mushrooms and mould are fungi too. Some fungi are friendly. Some make plants ill.  
> **Fern**: A fungus lets go of spores into the air, the way a dandelion lets go of seeds. Only far, far smaller.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that some plant diseases are caused by a fungus, and that fungi spread by tiny spores._

**Fern**: I know what you’re thinking. “Keeping the trees alive, badly.” Yes. I said it first. I’ve said it for years.  
_Through the hedge, wasps buzz over the windfalls, the plums that dropped in the grass. The whole slope smells of hot jam._  
**Fern**: Hazel grafted half those trees for me, the year I moved in. She whitewashed every trunk herself, every spring.  
**Fern**: I haven’t told anyone. If I tell someone, it’s real.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): sharing a worry with someone you trust  
> _Fern has kept a big worry all to herself. What would help her most?_  
> - ✅ Telling someone she trusts. → Yes. Telling someone doesn’t make the problem real. It was already real. Telling someone makes it shared. Then other people can help carry it.
> - Keeping it secret, so nobody gets upset. → That’s a kind thought. But secrets can make a worry feel heavier and heavier. When we tell someone we trust, they can help. That’s why Fern came to your gate.
> - Pretending it isn’t happening. → Lots of people try that. But the trees stay sick whether we look or not. Telling someone she trusts is braver, and it means help can come sooner.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why sharing a worry with a trusted person helps._


**Choices**

- **Go and look now.**
    - You go through the hedge. Fern follows two steps behind, as if the trees might hear her coming.
- **Put an arm round her first.**
    - **Fern**: Oh, don’t. I’ll go soggy. I’ve got a whole orchard to be soggy about.
    - She leans on you for exactly one second. Then she marches off through the hedge.
- **Say you’ll fetch Byte.**
    - **Fern**: Byte. Yes. Byte will put it in a chart. Then it’s a problem instead of a tragedy.

#### `reader:s11-ember` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-ember)

_Three trees at the bottom of the slope. Their leaves have curled up like little black fists._  
_Grey dust lies under them like spilt flour._  
_Someone else has seen. Ember stands at the orchard wall, arms folded so tight her knuckles are white._  
**Ember**: Burn it. All of it. Tonight, before the blight spreads.  
**Fern**: All of it? Ember, there are forty trees.  
**Ember**: There’ll be none if you wait. I know how waiting goes. You’re polite, you wait, and then it’s too late.  
_Clover has come up behind you with a basket of bandages. That’s what Clover brings to everything._  
**Clover**: You can’t burn forty trees for three. You don’t even know what it is yet.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 2.OA.A.1, ask): subtraction within 100  
> _Clover is thinking about the numbers. Forty trees. Three are sick._  
> _How many healthy trees would Ember burn?_  
> - ✅ 37 → Yes. 40 take away 3 is 37. Thirty-seven healthy trees, burned for three sick ones.
> - 43 → Careful: that’s 40 add 3. We want the trees that are left when we take the sick ones away. 40 − 3 = 37.
> - 3 → Three is how many are sick. The healthy ones are all the rest: 40 − 3 = 37.
> _For grown-ups: Subtracted within 100 (40 − 3) to see what a rash plan would cost._

**Ember**: I didn’t know what he was either, till he had Ash. Ask me how that went.  
_Fern sits down on an upturned crate between them, and says nothing at all._  
**Clover**: That’s cinder-love, that is. Holding on so tight you’d burn the lot. Don’t tell her I said so.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): working out a character’s motive from her past  
> _Why does Ember want to burn everything, so fast?_  
> - ✅ She lost Ash by waiting, and she’s scared it will happen again. → Yes. Ember’s past explains her now. She waited once, and lost Ash. So when something goes wrong, her fear shouts: don’t wait!
> - She likes fires. → Nothing in the story says that. Listen to what she says: “I know how waiting goes.” She lost Ash by waiting. She’s frightened of waiting again.
> - She’s cross with Fern. → She sounds cross. But she isn’t cross with Fern. She mentions Ash. Her anger is covering up a fear: that waiting will cost her everything again.
> _For grown-ups: Used a character’s past to explain why she acts the way she does._


#### `reader:s11-lens` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-lens)

_Byte arrives at a jog. He has a hand lens on a string, a roll of luggage labels, and a pencil behind each ear._  
**Byte**: Nobody burns anything until it’s been looked at. That isn’t a rule. That’s just being a person.  

> **Lesson** (science, do): how a hand lens magnifies  
> **Byte**: This is a hand lens. See how the glass is curved, thicker in the middle than at the edges?  
> **Byte**: That curve bends the light coming through it, so things look bigger. We say it magnifies them.  
> **Byte**: Try it. Hold the lens near a leaf, then move it slowly until the tiny spots look big and sharp.  
> Check: `edu:focus:lens:leaf`
> _For grown-ups: Used a hand lens and learned that its curved glass bends light to make small things look bigger._

**Ember**: You’ve got till dusk. Then I’m doing it, with or without your little labels.  
**Byte**: They’re standard labels. They’re not little. They’re regular.  
_He ties the first label to the first sick tree. In capitals: NOT FINE._  
_The next gets PROBABLY FINE. The next gets FINE (ACCORDING TO FERN). The water butt gets DO NOT DRINK._  
**Byte**: Forty trees, one lens. You look, I write. Clover, stop bandaging that branch. It isn’t hurt. It’s a branch.  
**Clover**: It looked lonely.  
**Byte**: Leaves for spots. Bark for holes. Fruit for dust. And don’t lick anything. I have to say that. I’ve met people.  

> **Lesson** (health, ask): staying safe around mould  
> **Byte**: While you’re looking: say you find a mouldy plum on the grass. What’s the safe thing to do?  
> - ✅ Leave it, and wash my hands if I touched it. → Correct. Mouldy food can make you ill. Leave it, and wash your hands. You may keep your tongue.
> - Taste a tiny bit, to check. → No! That’s the licking! I knew it! Never taste mouldy food or plants you don’t know. Leave it, and wash your hands if you touched it.
> - Sniff it really closely. → Close sniffing can put spores right up your nose. Not nice. Look with the lens instead. Leave it, and wash your hands if you touched it.
> _For grown-ups: Learned not to eat or sniff mouldy food, and to wash hands after touching it._

_On his way past the wall, he labels Ember, very small and very fast: VOLATILE. She doesn’t notice. Yet._  

#### `reader:s11-map` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-map)

_By mid-afternoon the orchard flutters with paper labels, like a washing line. You spread the map on Fern’s crate._  
_Three trees are NOT FINE, in a crooked row at the bottom. Six are INCONCLUSIVE, SMELLS. The rest are fine._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.D.8, ask): two-step subtraction  
> **Byte**: Forty trees on the map. Three NOT FINE. Six INCONCLUSIVE. How many are FINE?  
> - ✅ 31 → Yes. 40 − 3 = 37. Then 37 − 6 = 31. Two steps. Thirty-one fine trees.
> - 37 → Nearly. That takes away the three NOT FINE. Now take away the six INCONCLUSIVE too. 37 − 6 = 31.
> - 9 → Nine is the trees we’re worried about: 3 + 6. The fine ones are the rest. 40 − 9 = 31.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a two-step subtraction problem (40 − 3 − 6) from a map._

**Byte**: Inconclusive means I can’t tell yet. Those six have wasp bites. Wasps aren’t a disease. They’re just rude.  
**Byte**: Now look. It isn’t random. Every sick tree is in a line with the wind that comes up off the falls.  
**Byte**: An illness you catch by touching goes from each tree to the next one. This one skipped trees. It came through the air.  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): using a map pattern to see how a disease spreads downwind  
> _On the map, the sick trees make a line along the wind, with healthy trees in between._  
> _What does that pattern tell Byte?_  
> - ✅ Something is being carried on the wind. → Exactly. The wind carries things the way it blows. They land downwind. So we follow the line back the way the wind comes from, and find where it starts.
> - Trees pass it to the tree next door. → If they did, the sick trees would be in a clump, all touching. These have healthy trees between them. A line along the wind means the wind is carrying something. It lands downwind.
> - The bottom trees are just old. → Some are old. But old trees all over the orchard are fine. The sick ones line up with the wind. Something is riding it, and landing downwind.
> _For grown-ups: Read a pattern on a map to work out that a disease was spreading on the wind._

**You**: So what’s in the air?  
**Byte**: Good question. I have a jar.  

#### `reader:s11-jar` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-jar)

_Byte hands you a jam jar with a muslin lid. Clover hands you a second one, in case the first gets lonely._  
**Byte**: Something is landing on the sick side. If we catch one, we can stop guessing.  
_You crouch in the long grass. The sun is hot on your neck. Dust lifts off the black plums like smoke._  
_Something small and grey lands on a leaf. The moment your shadow touches the leaf, it folds its legs and drops._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): how small animals use their senses to escape danger  
> **Clover**: To a tiny bug, a sudden shadow can mean a bird swooping down to eat it.  
> **Clover**: So lots of small creatures drop or hide the moment the light changes. Their senses keep them alive.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that many small animals sense a sudden shadow as danger and hide._

**Clover**: Keep the sun in front of you (never look straight at it), so your shadow’s behind you. Shy things hide from shadows. I would.  

> **Lesson** (science, 5-ESS1-2, ask): shadows fall on the side away from the light  
> _The sun is low in the west. If you turn to face it, where will your shadow fall?_  
> - ✅ Behind me. → Yes. Light travels in straight lines. Your body blocks it, so your shadow lands on the far side. Sun in front, shadow behind. Now the bugs won’t see you coming.
> - In front of me. → That’s what happened before, when the sun was on your neck, behind you. A shadow always falls on the side away from the light. Face the sun, and it falls behind you.
> - Nowhere. Shadows go away in the sun. → Bright sun makes the sharpest shadows of all! Your body blocks the light, so the shadow falls on the side away from the sun. Face it, and it’s behind you.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out that a shadow falls on the side of an object away from the light._

**Ember**: Or we could burn the lot and not crawl about in the grass.  
_She says it, but she doesn’t leave. She stands at the wall and watches every catch you miss._  

#### `reader:s11-beetle` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-beetle)

_In the jar: a beetle no bigger than a lentil. It’s grey as pencil dust, with wings folded like a paper fan._  
**Byte**: Under the lens it’s covered in spores. Black ones. It isn’t the disease. It’s the postman.  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): the difference between a disease and the carrier that spreads it  
> _Byte calls the beetle “the postman”. What does he mean?_  
> - ✅ It carries the spores from tree to tree. → Yes. A postman doesn’t write the letters. He carries them. The fungus is the disease. The beetle is the carrier: the spores ride on it to the next tree.
> - The beetle is the disease. → Close, but no. The disease is the fungus. Its spores are stuck all over the beetle. The beetle just carries them, like a postman carries letters. It’s the carrier.
> - It bites the trees and makes them sick. → It might nibble. But look at the black spores on its back. They’re the problem. The beetle carries the spores to new trees, like a postman with letters. It’s the carrier.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a disease and the creature that carries it are not the same thing._

_Byte follows the sick trees back the way the wind comes. They lead to a dead wild plum in the hedge by the stream._  
_The dead tree is crawling with beetles. Each puff of wind lifts a few and carries them down the row._  
**Byte**: They can’t fly against the wind. Only the trees downwind of that stump get them. The rest are safe.  
**Byte**: We can contain it. Keep it in one place, so it can’t spread.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, notice): where the word “quarantine” comes from  
> **Byte**: Keeping the sick ones apart, so an illness can’t spread, has a name. Quarantine.  
> **Byte**: It comes from an old Italian word for forty. Long ago in Venice, ships had to wait forty days before anyone came ashore.  
> **Byte**: That way, if a sailor was ill, it showed before it spread. Forty. Like the trees. I find that oddly comforting.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what quarantine means and that the word comes from the forty days ships waited long ago._

**Clover**: Ladybirds eat beetle eggs, you know. I keep a jar of them at home. They have no manners at all.  
**Clover**: That one’s clean. Right in the middle of the sick row. Look.  
**Fern**: Grudge. Hazel’s old damson. Sourest fruit in the valley. I’ve been meaning to cut it down for years.  
_Byte ties a new label to the jar. It says: GUILTY._  

#### `reader:s11-dusk` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-dusk)

_The sun goes down behind the western hill. Bats come out over the stream. Then a light comes through the hedge, low and orange._  
_It’s Ember, with a burning torch. In the firelight her face isn’t angry. It’s very, very frightened._  
**Ember**: I’m sorry, Fern. I’m not waiting. I waited once.  
**Fern**: Ember…  
**Ember**: When Ash went, I kept thinking: tomorrow. I’ll go and get her back tomorrow. Then tomorrow was years.  
**Ember**: Fast is the only thing I’ve got. Don’t take it off me.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, ask): thinking about acting fast when you are scared  
> _Ember is scared. Her fear is telling her: act now, and act big._  
> _What do you think? Is it a good idea to act fast when you’re scared?_  
> - Yes. Sometimes waiting makes things worse. → That’s a fair answer. Ember learned it the hard way, with Ash. Sometimes fast really is right, like pulling your hand away from something hot.
> - No. Fear can make you do too much. → That’s fair too. Clover would say fear makes everything look bigger than it is. Ember’s fear saw forty trees. The real problem was three.
> - Stop, breathe, look at the facts. Then act fast. → That’s a thoughtful answer. Byte would like it: fast and right. A slow breath only takes a moment. Sometimes it’s the moment that saves thirty-seven trees.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how fear pushes us to act fast, and when pausing first helps._

_Byte holds the map. Clover holds Fern’s hand. Everybody looks at you._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-5-ETS1-2, ask): comparing solutions to a problem  
> _Three plans for the orchard. Which one stops the blight and saves the most trees?_  
> - ✅ Take out the three sick trees and the dead plum. Keep watching the rest. → Yes. It stops the beetles where they start, and it saves thirty-seven trees. Taking out only the sick ones and keeping them apart from the healthy ones is a quarantine.
> - Burn all forty trees. → That would stop the blight. But it destroys thirty-seven healthy trees too. A better plan removes only the three sick trees and the dead plum, where the beetles start.
> - Do nothing, and hope. → That saves trees today. But the beetles would keep blowing down the row, and more trees would fall ill. A better plan removes the three sick trees and the dead plum, where the beetles start.
> _For grown-ups: Compared three solutions to a problem and chose the one with the best result and least harm._


**Choices**

- **Stand between her and the trees.**
    - You step in front of the first healthy tree. The torch is close enough to warm your face.
    - **Ember**: Move.
    - **You**: No.
    - For a long moment nobody breathes. Then the torch drops, slowly, until it points at the grass.
    - **Ember**: You’d better be right. You’d better be right, or I’ll never forgive you.
    - She doesn’t leave. In the dark, without a word, she helps Fern chop down the three sick trees and the dead stump.
- **Hand her the map. Let the evidence speak.**
    - You hold out Byte’s map. She doesn’t want to take it. She takes it.
    - She reads it by her own torchlight: the line of the wind, the dead plum, the jar marked GUILTY.
    - **Ember**: Three. Not forty.
    - **Ember**: Three.
    - She pushes the torch into the water butt. It goes out with a long hiss, like a held breath.
    - **Ember**: Show me the stump. I’ll do the stump.
- **Take the torch with her. Burn only the three sick trees, together.**
    - You put your hand on the torch, next to hers. She looks at you. Then she lets you hold it too.
    - You burn the three sick trees and the dead wild plum, and nothing else. It takes a long time. Neither of you lets go.
    - **Clover**: Goodbye, trees. You were very good plums.
    - **Ember**: That’s how it should have gone, with Ash. Not on my own. Not in a rush.
    - Fern brings out tea. Ember drinks hers standing up, watching the fire, until it’s only warm.

#### `reader:s11-ladybirds` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-ladybirds)

_Clover unscrews her jar. Out pour ladybirds, hundreds of them, red as rosehips. They settle along the hedge._  
**Clover**: Off you go, then. Every beetle egg you can find. Manners not required.  
**Fern**: No fire. No poison. Just hungry little helpers. Hazel would have liked that.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-4, notice): using a predator to protect plants when a habitat changes  
> **Clover**: A ladybird is a predator. It hunts and eats other small creatures, which we call its prey.  
> **Clover**: Ladybirds eat greenfly and the eggs of other insects. Gardeners love them.  
> **Clover**: Using a predator to protect plants is a way to solve a problem without harming the rest of the garden.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that ladybirds are predators, and that gardeners can use them instead of poison to protect plants._

_One lands on your thumb and won’t get off. Then three more._  
_Each one is bright red, with seven neat black spots._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiplication as equal groups  
> _Four ladybirds on your thumb. Seven spots each. How many spots altogether?_  
> - ✅ 28 → Yes. Four groups of seven: 4 × 7 = 28. These are seven-spot ladybirds. Some people say the spots tell a ladybird’s age. They don’t. That’s a myth.
> - 11 → That’s 4 + 7. But each ladybird has seven spots, so it’s four groups of seven. 7, 14, 21, 28. 4 × 7 = 28.
> - 24 → Close! Let’s count in sevens, one ladybird at a time: 7, 14, 21, 28. 4 × 7 = 28.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied 4 × 7 by thinking of equal groups._

_After a while they are, more or less, yours._  
**Byte**: I tried to label them. It isn’t possible. They’re all called LADYBIRD now.  

#### `reader:s11-complete` · [main](../../../side/S11.md#s11-complete)

_A week later the orchard is quiet. Three stumps at the bottom of the slope. Thirty-seven green trees above them._  
_In the middle of the bare row stands one tree, untouched: Grudge, the old damson, heavy with sour purple fruit._  
**Fern**: Byte says the beetles hate Grudge’s bark. Too bitter. Hazel used to say Grudge was too stubborn to die.  
**Fern**: She always told me to graft off it. I never did. Who wants sour plums? Here. She left this at mine.  
_A short curved knife with a plum-wood handle, wound in green twine. The edge is still sharp._  
**Fern**: Bind a cutting of Grudge onto a young tree, and the young tree grows up stubborn too. Her trick. Yours now.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, notice): grafting, and how a helpful trait helps a plant survive  
> **Fern**: Grafting is real, you know. Lots of fruit trees are grown this way.  
> **Fern**: You join a twig from one tree onto a young tree. The two grow together, and the new branch keeps the old tree’s ways.  
> **Fern**: Grudge has bitter bark. In a beetle year, that’s what helped it survive when the others didn’t.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned about grafting, and that a trait like bitter bark can help a plant survive a pest._

**Fern**: You saved my orchard. That makes you its warden, whether you like it or not.  
_Ember comes past the wall. She has found Byte’s label in her pocket. She reads VOLATILE, and looks at him._  
_Byte takes it, crosses it out, and writes something else: FRIGHTENED. Then, underneath: (FAIR)._  
**Ember**: …Fair.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): understanding why a character changes their mind  
> _Why did Byte change VOLATILE to FRIGHTENED?_  
> - ✅ He understood that Ember’s anger was really fear. → Yes. At dusk, Byte saw Ember’s face in the firelight. She wasn’t wild. She was scared. The new label is fairer, and Ember knows it.
> - He wanted to tease her. → The first label was a bit of a tease. But look what he writes underneath: (FAIR). He changed it because he understood her: her anger was really fear.
> - He made a spelling mistake. → Byte never makes spelling mistakes. Just ask him. He changed it because he understood Ember better: her anger was really fear.
> _For grown-ups: Explained why a character changed his opinion of someone else._


### Glossary

- **orchard**: A field or garden of fruit trees.
- **fungus**: A living thing, like a mushroom or mould, that is not a plant and feeds on living or dead things. More than one are fungi.
- **blight**: A disease that makes plants wither, rot and die.
- **magnify**: To make something look bigger than it really is.
- **volatile**: Likely to flare up or explode suddenly, like a quick temper.
- **inconclusive**: Not clear enough to decide yet.
- **downwind**: In the direction the wind is blowing towards.
- **muslin**: Thin cotton cloth with tiny gaps that let air through.
- **carrier**: Something that carries a disease to others without being the disease itself.
- **quarantine**: Keeping sick living things apart from healthy ones so an illness can’t spread.
- **evidence**: Facts and things you have found that show what is true.
- **predator**: An animal that hunts and eats other animals.
- **prey**: An animal that is hunted and eaten by another animal.
- **graft**: To join a cutting from one plant onto another so they grow together.
- **warden**: Someone whose job is to look after and protect a place.
