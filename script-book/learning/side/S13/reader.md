# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S13: The Mill Cellar

## S13. The Mill Cellar

- `s13-step1`: Get past the rats in the grain store, and find the hatch to level two. · _Feed the rats, or creep past them along the wall. The hatch is under the sacks. The old hoist will lift them._
- `s13-step2`: Drain the flooded sluice room. Open its three gates in the right order. · _Water flows downhill. Open the lowest gate first, then the middle, then the top, or the room fills again._
- `s13-step3`: Find what is jamming the great wheel, and get past it to the bottom stair. · _Watch which gear shudders when the river pushes. Never stand between the spokes._
- `s13-step4`: Unlock the iron door and go down to the bottom of the cellar with Tobin. · _Use Tobin’s key on the iron door past the wheel pit. Take a lantern: there’s no light down there._
- `s13-step5`: Do what you decided with Aldous’s letter. · _Keep it safe at home, burn it in the mill’s old stove, or take it to the grave under the alders by the race._

#### `reader:s13-tobin` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-tobin)

_Autumn. Below the falls the river runs brown and loud. The old mill leans over it like a man trying to hear something in the water._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): spot a simile and say what it helps you picture  
> _The mill “leans over the river like a man trying to hear something”. A comparison that uses “like” or “as” is called a simile._  
> _What does this simile help you picture?_  
> - ✅ The mill is tipped forward, bent over the water. → Yes. Picture someone bending close to listen. The old mill is bent the same way, over the river.
> - The mill can really hear things. → It’s a fun thought, but a simile only compares. The mill isn’t listening. It leans the way a listening man leans.
> - The mill is very noisy. → The river is noisy! But this simile is about shape. The mill is bent over the water, like someone leaning in to hear.
> _For grown-ups: Recognised a simile and described the picture it creates._

_Its great wheel hasn’t turned in twenty years. Moss sits on the old millstone by the door. It’s the only thing here he approves of._  
_A very old man in a lamplighter’s coat is unlocking the door with a key the size of a ladle._  
**Old Tobin**: Hazel’s grandchild. Thought so. You’ve got her chin, and her habit of standing exactly where I’m about to swing a door.  
**Old Tobin**: Tobin. I lit the lamps in this valley for fifty-one years. Every lane, every bridge, every dusk.  
**Old Tobin**: Then they put in new lamps that light themselves, and I was free to tell stories full time. Nobody’s thanked them for that.  
**Old Tobin**: This was Aldous Vale’s mill. Corvin Vale’s father. Yes, that Corvin. Him with the cold garden.  
**Old Tobin**: There’s a cellar under it. Four levels, going down. I’ve kept it shut for twenty years.  
**Old Tobin**: But the river’s getting in. By spring it’ll have the lot. Somebody ought to see what’s down there first.  
**Old Tobin**: There was one winter, the year of the Three Hats, when the wheel ran so hard the floor smoked, and—  
_He stops. He looks at the door. He looks at you._  
**Old Tobin**: Where was I? Cellar. Yes. Dark.  
**Moss**: He has been telling that one since before you were born. He has never once reached the end.  
**Old Tobin**: I heard that, Moss. I reach the end all the time. I just don’t bring anyone with me.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-4, notice): how a watermill turns moving water into work  
> **Moss**: Before you go down, learn what’s above you. A mill turns moving water into work.  
> **Moss**: The river pushes the paddles on the wheel, and the wheel turns. The wheel turns an axle, and gears pass the turning along.  
> **Moss**: At the end, a heavy millstone spins on top of a still one. Grain caught between them is crushed into flour.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how a watermill changes the energy of moving water into turning that grinds grain into flour._


#### `reader:s13-level1` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-level1)

_Level one: the grain store. Sacks are piled to the ceiling, gone hard as stone. The air tastes of old flour and mouse._  
_Something scrabbles in the dark. Then a great many somethings._  
**Old Tobin**: Rats. Mill rats. Very organised.  
**Old Tobin**: Aldous used to say they were the only staff who never asked for a day off.  
_Dozens of small noses twitch at you from the shadows. They have smelt you already._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): how rats use their senses in the dark  
> **Old Tobin**: Rats don’t see sharply, but their sense of smell is excellent. They smell food long before they see it.  
> **Old Tobin**: Their whiskers help too. They feel their way along walls with them, in the pitch dark.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how rats use smell and whiskers to find food and find their way in the dark._

_Somewhere behind the sacks is the hatch to level two. The rats are between you and it._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): share equally (division)  
> **Old Tobin**: I make it forty rats. You’ve eight plums in your bag.  
> **Old Tobin**: If they share fairly, how many rats get each plum?  
> - ✅ 5 rats to each plum → Five. Forty shared into eight groups is five in each, because 8 × 5 = 40. Very fair. Rats respect fair.
> - 8 rats to each plum → Let’s check. 8 plums with 8 rats each would be 8 × 8 = 64 rats. Too many! We need 8 × something = 40. That’s 5. Five rats to a plum.
> - 4 rats to each plum → Close. 8 × 4 is only 32, so eight rats would get nothing. 8 × 5 = 40, so it’s five rats to a plum.
> _For grown-ups: Divided 40 by 8 by thinking about equal groups and multiplication._


**Choices**

- **Roll them some fruit, and walk past while they eat.**
    - You roll your plums into the dark. Forty small faces turn to them at once, like a choir turning to its conductor.
    - You walk straight past. Not one rat looks up.
    - **Old Tobin**: Bribery. Your grandmother’s method. Works on rats and parish councils.
- **Shade your lantern and creep along the wall.**
    - You creep along the wall in the dark, one step at a time.
    - A rat sits on your boot for a while. It decides you are furniture.
    - Behind you, Tobin creeps too. He is whispering a story about a much bigger rat, very loudly.

#### `reader:s13-sluice` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-sluice)

_The old hoist lifts the hard sacks with a shriek. Under them is a hatch, and three things nobody has touched in twenty years._  
**Old Tobin**: Loot. That’s the proper word for it. I had it off a pedlar.  
**Old Tobin**: A ladle. Plain, that. Look at it. The Gently Used Ladle of Second Thoughts.  
**Old Tobin**: A sock. Also Plain. The Sock of Uncertain Origin. Don’t ask it where it’s been.  
**Old Tobin**: And the cheese rind the rats were guarding. Oh, that’s Fine, that is. The Rat-Nibbled Cheese Rind of Courage.  
**You**: Does everything get a name?  
**Old Tobin**: Everything found in a cellar gets a name. It’s the rule. I made it up just now, and it’s very old.  
_Below the hatch you can hear water. Not running. Just lying there in the dark, breathing._  
**Old Tobin**: Level two’s the sluice room. It’s been flooded since Aldous went.  
**Old Tobin**: There are three gates, one above another down the wall. Open them in the wrong order and you’ll be swimming.  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): water flows downhill because gravity pulls it  
> **Old Tobin**: Three gates: a low one, a middle one and a top one. The water has to go out through them, down to the river.  
> **Old Tobin**: Which gate do you open first?  
> - ✅ The lowest gate. → Right. Water always flows downhill, pulled by gravity. Open the bottom gate first and the water has somewhere lower to go. Then the middle, then the top, and the room drains instead of filling.
> - The top gate. → Then the top water pours down into the room, and the low gate is still shut. Splash. Water flows downhill, pulled by gravity. Give it a way out at the bottom first.
> - The middle gate. → Better than the top, but the water below it still has nowhere to go. Water flows downhill, pulled by gravity. Open the lowest gate first, so it can drain away.
> _For grown-ups: Used the idea that water flows downhill (because of gravity) to solve the sluice-gate puzzle._

**Old Tobin**: Marina could swim it. Marina could swim a soup.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): spot exaggeration used to be funny  
> _Tobin says “Marina could swim a soup.” Does he mean it?_  
> - ✅ No. It’s a joke that means she’s a brilliant swimmer. → That’s it. Saying something much bigger than the truth, to make a point, is called exaggeration. Nobody can swim in soup. Probably.
> - Yes. Marina really swims in soup. → Ha! Marina swims in the sea and the tide pools, not soup. Tobin is exaggerating: making it bigger than true, for fun. He means she can swim almost anywhere.
> _For grown-ups: Recognised exaggeration used for humour and explained what the speaker really meant._


#### `reader:s13-level2` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-level2)

_The last gate groans open. The water rushes out of the room with a long sigh, like a bath let out after twenty years._  
_On the wet floor are two eels, very annoyed about it._  
_And up one wall is a row of chalk marks. Somebody measured a boy here, year after year._  
_Each mark has a number beside it. The lowest says 98 cm. The highest says 131 cm._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.2, ask): find a difference in length by counting on  
> _The boy’s lowest mark is 98 cm. His highest is 131 cm._  
> _How much did he grow between the two?_  
> - ✅ 33 cm → Yes. Count on: from 98 to 100 is 2, and from 100 to 131 is 31. 2 + 31 = 33 cm.
> - 43 cm → Let’s count on carefully. From 98 to 100 is 2. From 100 to 131 is 31. 2 + 31 = 33 cm.
> - 29 cm → Nearly. Count on from 98: 2 gets you to 100, then 31 more gets you to 131. That’s 33 cm.
> _For grown-ups: Found the difference between two lengths (131 cm and 98 cm) by counting on through 100._

_Beside the last mark, in a square grown-up hand, someone has written: FIT FOR THE SLUICE._  
**Old Tobin**: …  
**Old Tobin**: Where was I.  
_He doesn’t say the rest. He goes on down the steps ahead of you, and he doesn’t look at the wall._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): use a clue to work out how a character sees someone  
> _Think about the words by the last mark: FIT FOR THE SLUICE._  
> _What do they tell you about how Aldous saw his son?_  
> - ✅ He was checking when the boy was big enough to work. → Yes. Not “well done”, not even the boy’s name. He measured his son to see when he could work the sluice. Tobin’s silence tells you he finds that very sad.
> - He was proud of how tall the boy was getting. → Maybe a little. But look at the words: “fit for the sluice” means ready to work it. He measured his son to see when he could be put to work. Tobin’s silence says how sad that is.
> - He wanted to keep the boy away from the water. → It would be nice to think so. But “fit for the sluice” means ready to work at it. He was waiting until the boy could do the job.
> _For grown-ups: Used a written clue in the setting to infer how one character saw another._


#### `reader:s13-wheelpit` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-wheelpit)

_Drying on the steps, where the flood left them: a pair of boots, a doorknob with no door, and a scarf like pond weed._  
**Old Tobin**: Squeaky Boots of Announcing Yourself. Fine. The Damp Doorknob of Not Quite Opening. Also Fine.  
**Old Tobin**: And that’s Rare. The Soggy Scarf of Lingering Optimism. Wring it out all you like. It still thinks it’ll dry.  
_Level three is the wheel pit. The great wheel sits half in the cellar and half in the river, dripping._  
_Behind it, iron gears as big as cartwheels fit together, tooth into tooth._  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): how gears pass turning along  
> **Old Tobin**: Gears pass turning from one wheel to the next. When two gears fit together, they turn opposite ways.  
> **Old Tobin**: And when a big gear turns a small one, the small one spins faster. That’s how a slow waterwheel turns a fast millstone.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that meshing gears turn in opposite directions, and a big gear makes a small one spin faster._

_The wheel doesn’t turn. Each time the river leans on it, the whole mill shudders, and dust comes down like flour._  
_Up through the floorboards, Moss calls that he is staying on his millstone, thank you. He is far too old for underneath._  
**Old Tobin**: Mind the wheel. It’s stuck, not dead. There’s a difference, and the difference is what takes your fingers off.  

> **Lesson** (health, ask): stay safe around big machines  
> **Old Tobin**: The wheel could lurch at any moment. What’s the safe way to act around a big machine that might move?  
> - ✅ Keep well back, and never put hands or feet near the moving parts. → Good. That’s how you keep all your fingers. And if you’re not sure a machine is safe, ask a grown-up first.
> - Touch it to check whether it’s moving. → No touching! A machine can start without warning. Keep well back from moving parts. If you’re not sure it’s safe, ask a grown-up.
> - Stand between the spokes to get a better look. → That’s the one place you must never stand. If the wheel lurches, the spokes come round. Keep well back, and keep hands and feet away from moving parts.
> _For grown-ups: Talked about keeping a safe distance from machines and asking a grown-up when unsure._

**Old Tobin**: The stair to the bottom is past the gears. Find what’s jamming them, and step round it.  

#### `reader:s13-level3` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-level3)

_In the biggest gear, driven in hard, is a wooden wedge. It didn’t fall there. Somebody put it there._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): use equal groups to solve a gear puzzle (division)  
> _The big gear has 48 teeth. The small gear it drives has 12._  
> _When the wheel turns again, each time the big gear goes round once, how many times will the small gear go round?_  
> - ✅ 4 times → Yes. 48 teeth go past, and the small gear needs 12 for each turn. 48 ÷ 12 = 4, because 4 × 12 = 48.
> - 12 times → 12 is how many teeth the small gear has. Each turn uses up 12 of the big gear’s 48 teeth. 48 ÷ 12 = 4. It goes round 4 times.
> - 36 times → That’s 48 − 12. But we want how many twelves fit into 48: 12, 24, 36, 48. That’s 4 turns.
> _For grown-ups: Divided 48 by 12 using equal groups to work out how many times a small gear turns._

_A tag hangs from it on a bit of string, in the same square hand: STOPPED. LEAVE IT._  
**You**: Somebody wedged it on purpose.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-PS2-1, ask): balanced forces mean no movement  
> _Every few seconds the river pushes the wheel hard. But it doesn’t turn. Why not?_  
> - ✅ The wedge pushes back just as hard, so the pushes balance. → Yes. When two pushes are equal and opposite, they are balanced, and nothing moves. Knock the wedge out, and the river’s push wins. The wheel turns.
> - The river isn’t really pushing. → It is. You can feel the whole mill shudder. But the wedge pushes back equally. Balanced forces mean no movement.
> - The wheel is too old to turn. → Tobin says it’s stuck, not dead. The wedge balances the river’s push. Take the wedge away and the wheel would turn.
> _For grown-ups: Explained that balanced forces (the river and the wedge) cause no movement._

**Old Tobin**: Aldous. His last winter.  
**Old Tobin**: That step, there. That’s where they found him, the day he died. Sitting down. First time anyone ever saw him sit.  
_Past the wheel, a narrow stair goes down to an iron door. It is locked. The lock has been oiled._  
**Old Tobin**: Not that one. Not yet.  
**Old Tobin**: There’s a thing you ought to know first, about him and your grandmother. It’s hers to tell, not mine.  
**Old Tobin**: She wrote most things down. When you’ve read it, come back, and I’ll find the key.  
**Old Tobin**: I know exactly where it is. That’s the trouble.  

#### `reader:s13-key` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-key)

**Old Tobin**: He was her apprentice. Before that, he was this mill’s boy.

> **Old Tobin**: Out at dusk to light them, back at dawn to put them out.  
> **Old Tobin**: Now they switch on by themselves. Quicker. Not as much company.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how street lamps were lit by hand in the past, and compared that with lights that switch on by themselves today._

**Old Tobin**: A sieve that sighs when you shake it: the Haunted Flour Sifter of Polite Sighing. Storied, that.

**Old Tobin**: And a little hat cut from a broken grindstone. Somebody meant to hand that down, so it’s an heirloom. The Millstone Hat of Considerable Weight.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, do): build a name from a describing word, a noun and a feeling  
> **Old Tobin**: Your turn. Every loot name has the same pattern. An odd describing word, a plain object, then “of” and a small feeling.  
> **Old Tobin**: Find something in your pocket and name it. Say, the Crumpled Hanky of Mild Surprise.

**Old Tobin**: Down we go. I’ll come with you. I’ve never been in. I’ve only ever kept it shut.  

#### `reader:s13-desk` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-desk)

_The bottom room is small, dry and very cold._  
_A desk. A chair pushed in square. A lamp with its wick trimmed._  
_On the hook behind the door hang a miller’s apron, still floury, and a pocket watch. It stopped at four minutes past five._  
**Old Tobin**: Five in the morning. That’s when the wheel started, every day of his life.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): read and write time to the minute  
> _The watch stopped at four minutes past five in the morning._  
> _Which clock time shows that?_  
> - ✅ 5:04 → Yes. The hour comes first, 5, then the minutes past it, 04. Four minutes past five is 5:04.
> - 4:05 → Careful: 4:05 is five minutes past four. The hour comes first. Four minutes past five is 5:04.
> - 5:40 → 5:40 is forty minutes past five. We need just four minutes past, so it’s 5:04.
> _For grown-ups: Wrote “four minutes past five” as a digital time (5:04)._

_On the desk is a ledger. Inside the cover, in chalk, are three rules._  
_ONE. THE WHEEL DOES NOT STOP. TWO. NOTHING IDLES. THREE. WHAT CANNOT KEEP UP IS LET GO._  
_Under them, in a child’s careful hand, the same three rules are copied out again, with pencil lines to keep the letters straight._  
_You turn the pages. Sacks, hours, men, wheel speed. Every year the hours go up. The word “rest” never appears._  
_Near the back: “Boy, 12, put on the night shift. New method: the wheel never stops. Output up a third.”_  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.His.2.K-2, ask): use an old record book to learn about life in the past  
> _A ledger is a record book. Old records like this help us find out how people lived in the past._  
> _What does this ledger tell you about life at the mill?_  
> - ✅ People worked very long hours with no rest, even a boy of twelve. → That’s what it says, plain as chalk. Long ago, in many places, children worked long hours in mills and factories. Today many countries have laws to stop that, and children go to school.
> - The mill was a fun, relaxed place. → Look for the word “rest”. It never appears. The hours only go up, every year. The ledger shows hard work and no rest, even for a boy of twelve.
> - Nothing. It’s only numbers. → Numbers tell stories too. Hours that only go up, and a boy of 12 on the night shift, tell us how hard life was there. That’s why historians read old ledgers.
> _For grown-ups: Used an old ledger as a historical source and compared children’s lives then and now._

_Folded inside the back cover is a letter. It was addressed, but never sent. The name on it is Corvin._  
**Old Tobin**: I found it the day they found him. I read it. I shouldn’t have. Then I shut the door on it, so nobody else had to carry it.  
**Old Tobin**: Twenty years. It got heavy, keeping it from people. I didn’t know it would.  

#### `reader:s13-letter` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-letter)

_The paper is good paper, the kind you’d buy for something that mattered. You unfold it._  
**Aldous Vale**: Corvin.  
**Aldous Vale**: I have started this nine times. The other eight went in the stove. You’d say that was waste. You’d be right. I taught you that.  
**Aldous Vale**: They say in the valley that you did a wrong thing at the old woman’s Grove, and left in the night. I don’t know what. I know how.  
**Aldous Vale**: Quickly. In the dark. Without once sitting down to think. I know whose hands those were. They were mine first.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): work out what a metaphor really means  
> _Aldous writes: “I know whose hands those were. They were mine first.”_  
> _He doesn’t mean real hands. What is he really saying?_  
> - ✅ Corvin learned his rushing, cold way of doing things from his father. → Yes. Here “hands” stands for a way of doing things. Saying one thing to mean another is called a metaphor. Aldous is owning up: Corvin learned it from him.
> - Corvin borrowed his father’s gloves. → Ha! Not quite. Here “hands” means how someone does things. Aldous means: you do things my way, quick and in the dark.
> - Aldous wants his hands back. → It’s a metaphor, so it isn’t about real hands at all. “Hands” means a way of doing things. Aldous is saying Corvin learned his ways from him.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the meaning of a metaphor in a letter (“they were mine first”)._

**Aldous Vale**: I can tell you the weight of every sack this mill ground the year you were born. I can’t tell you what you laughed at.  
**Aldous Vale**: Your mother used to sit by the mill race in the evenings, with her feet in the water. I called it idling.  
**Aldous Vale**: I would give the whole mill to sit there once with her now.  
**Aldous Vale**: I won’t say sorry. I don’t know how, and you’d hear it as one more order. You always heard me that way. I made sure of it.  
**Aldous Vale**: I’ve stopped the wheel. It’s very quiet. I think you’d hate it. I think you’d get used to it.  
**Aldous Vale**: If you ever stop, stop here. You could sit as long as you liked. Nobody would count.  
**Aldous Vale**: Your father.  
_…_  
**Old Tobin**: “Nobody would count.” He counted everything. His own boy’s hours, in a book. And then he wrote that.  
**Old Tobin**: It isn’t mine to decide what happens to it. It never was. I only held the door shut.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-awareness, ask): think about whether we must become like the people who raised us  
> **Old Tobin**: Aldous had three rules, and the boy copied them out. Hazel had three rules too, and hers were kind ones.  
> **Old Tobin**: Here’s a hard one, and I’ve no answer to it. Do we have to turn out like the grown-ups who raised us? Or can we choose differently?  
> - We can choose differently, even if it’s hard. → That’s a hopeful answer, and a fair one. Aldous himself stopped the wheel, right at the end. Late. But he chose.
> - We copy them a lot, without even noticing. → That’s honest, and it’s often true. The boy copied those rules in his best writing. Noticing what you’ve copied is the first step to choosing what to keep.
> - A bit of both. → I think that’s where I’d land too. We carry what we’re given. Then we decide what to keep, and what to set down.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how habits pass down through families, and whether we can choose differently (a think question with no single right answer)._


**Choices**

    - You fold it along its old folds and put it inside your coat, next to Hazel’s notebook.
    - **Old Tobin**: When he can feel again. Yes. Cold hands can’t hold a thing like that. It would slip straight through them.
    - **Old Tobin**: You’ll know when. Your grandmother always knew when. It was infuriating.
- **Burn it. Some things should end with the person.**
    - **Old Tobin**: …That’s a real answer. I’ve thought it myself, most winters.
    - **Old Tobin**: He wrote nine and burned eight. You’d only be finishing what he started.

    - **Old Tobin**: Read to the old one, and left with the man. Yes.
    - **Old Tobin**: Nobody ever read Aldous anything. He never had the time to listen. He has now.

#### `reader:s13-letter-given` · [main](../../../side/S13.md#s13-letter-given)

_Variant of `s13-letter`._

_Hazel turns the trowel over in her lap. You think of the letter in the tin box under your bed, and slip in to fetch it._  
_You go down the lane after him. Frost is coming down blue on the hedges, and your breath goes ahead of you._  
_He is at the bend by the stile, his hands pushed under his arms. He keeps stopping to feel them._  
**Corvin Vale**: Hazel’s grandchild. Twice in one evening. People will talk.  
_You hold out the letter. He sees the writing on the front, and doesn’t take it._  
**Corvin Vale**: That’s my father’s hand. He wrote as if he were being timed.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): choose the right meaning of a multiple-meaning word  
> _Corvin says, “That’s my father’s hand.” “Hand” has more than one meaning._  
> _Which meaning does he use here?_  
> - ✅ His handwriting. → Yes. Your “hand” can mean your handwriting. Corvin knows his father’s writing at a glance.
> - His real hand, at the end of his arm. → That’s the usual meaning. But he’s looking at a letter, so here “hand” means handwriting.
> - A helping hand. → Good thinking, that’s another meaning. But he’s looking at the writing on the front, so here it means handwriting.
> _For grown-ups: Used context to pick the right meaning of “hand” (handwriting)._

**You**: It was in the mill cellar. Tobin kept it. Your father wrote it after you’d gone.

### Glossary

- **lamplighter**: A person whose job was to light the street lamps by hand every evening.
- **cellar**: A room under a building, below the ground.
- **organised**: Well planned and tidy, with everyone knowing their job.
- **bribery**: Giving someone a treat so they will do what you want.
- **pedlar**: Someone who travels about selling small things.
- **sluice**: A gate that controls how much water flows through a channel.
- **optimism**: The feeling that things will turn out well.
- **shudders**: Shakes suddenly and hard.
- **wedge**: A block that is thick at one end and thin at the other, pushed into a gap to hold something still.
- **apprentice**: Someone who learns a job by working alongside an expert.
- **heirloom**: A precious thing handed down in a family, from older people to younger ones.
- **ledger**: A book where someone writes down numbers, such as money, sales or hours worked.
- **idles**: Sits about doing nothing, or does no work.
- **mill race**: The fast channel of water that runs to a mill and turns its wheel.
- **mallet**: A hammer with a big wooden head.
- **stile**: Steps or a gap that let people climb over a fence or wall.
- **chaff**: The dry husks left over when grain is separated from the rest of the plant.
- **deciduous**: Describes trees that drop all their leaves in autumn and grow new ones in spring.
- **headstone**: A stone at one end of a grave, often with the person’s name on it.
