# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S15: Lucky Eggs

## S15. Lucky Eggs

- `s15-step1`: Help Byte keep a tally of what comes out of thirty Mystery Eggs. · _Stand by the wagon. Each time a child opens an egg, make a mark under PLAIN or SPARKLY. Bundle your marks in fives._
- `s15-step2`: Get Jackpot on his own and ask him some questions. · _Dashwood naps after lunch. Creep along the stream side of the wagon, where a bell has fallen off. Don’t touch the wheels._
- `s15-step3`: Shine a lantern through the Legendary egg and see what’s really inside. · _This is called candling. It works best somewhere dim. Try under the bridge._
- `s15-step4`: Set up an honest egg stall with Byte, and chalk up the real odds. · _Byte has a slate and chalk. Put the stall by the bridge or at your gate, and write the true numbers on the board._

#### `reader:s15-wagon` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-wagon)

_A wagon comes down the lane, striped red and cream. A hundred little bells on its roof ring at every pothole._  
_On the driver’s seat sits a man in a mustard waistcoat. On his shoulder sits a brass parrot that ticks._  
**Dashwood Prance**: Children of the valley! Gather round! Dashwood Prance has come, and Dashwood Prance has brought you luck!  
**Dashwood Prance**: Mystery Eggs! Ten Shells each! Every egg a winner! Some eggs more winner than others!  
**Jackpot**: MOST EGGS ARE PLAIN.  
**Dashwood Prance**: Thank you, Jackpot.  
_A poster unrolls down the side of the wagon in gold letters: 1 IN 3 SPARKLY! Underneath, much smaller: (approx.)_  
_In the window, on a velvet cushion, an egg glitters like frost on a web. Its card says LEGENDARY. NOT FOR SALE (YET)._  
**The children**: Sparkly! Sparkly! Sparkly!  
**Pip**: I’ve got forty Shells. I’ve saved since my birthday. That’s four eggs. That’s one and a third sparkly!  
_Byte, the inventor Pixel’s husband, appears at your elbow with a clipboard. His shoelace is undone, as it was all the way down the aisle at his own wedding._  
_He has the face of someone who has just heard maths done wrong._  
**Byte**: One in three. Says who? I want to see it happen. Thirty eggs. We count every single one.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.B.3, do): keeping a tally in bundles of five  
> **Byte**: We’ll keep a tally. One stroke for every egg, under PLAIN or SPARKLY.  
> **Byte**: Four strokes standing up. The fifth goes across them, like a gate. That’s a bundle of five.  
> **Byte**: Bundles are quick to count: five, ten, fifteen… Try it. Mark the first five plain eggs as one gate.  
> Check: `edu:tally:eggs:gate-of-five`
> _For grown-ups: Learned to keep a tally, bundling marks in fives so they are quick to count._

**Byte**: And while we count, read that poster again. Properly.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): spotting the small print in an advert  
> **Byte**: Which part of that poster should make you most careful?  
> - ✅ The tiny word (approx.). → Yes. “Approx.” is short for approximately: roughly, more or less. Tiny writing under a big promise is a clue. It means: check before you believe it.
> - The shiny gold letters. → Gold letters are there to grab your eye. They’re not a lie, just a lot of shine. The real clue is the tiny word: (approx.). It means “roughly”. Tiny writing under a big promise means: check.
> - Nothing. Posters always tell the truth. → Some do. But posters are made to make you buy. Some stretch the truth. See the tiny word, (approx.)? It means “roughly”. Tiny writing under a big promise means: check.
> _For grown-ups: Looked closely at an advert and noticed how small print can soften a big promise._


**Choices**

- **Buy an egg yourself. For science.**
    - **Dashwood Prance**: A Keeper! A person of taste! Choose with your heart!
    - You choose with your heart. It’s plain. Jackpot looks at it and says nothing, very loudly.
    - **Byte**: That’s one. Twenty-nine to go. Don’t look like that. It’s data.
- **Ask Pip to wait before she spends it all.**
    - **Pip**: Wait? The sparkly ones might run out!
    - **Dashwood Prance**: They might! They very well might! That’s the magic of a limited time!
    - **Jackpot**: THEY WILL NOT RUN OUT.
- **Ask Dashwood where his odds come from.**
    - **Dashwood Prance**: From the heart, friend! From the very bottom of the heart, where the best numbers live!
    - **Byte**: That is not where numbers live.

#### `reader:s15-tally` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-tally)

_By noon Byte’s card is full. Thirty eggs. Thirty faces, mostly falling._  
_The tally says it all. PLAIN: five gates and two extra strokes. SPARKLY: three strokes._  
**Byte**: Twenty-seven plain. Three sparkly. That isn’t one in three. Not even close.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.NF.A.1, ask): chance as a fraction: 3 in 30 is 1 in 10  
> **Byte**: Three sparkly in thirty eggs. Split the thirty into three equal groups of ten. One sparkly in each.  
> **Byte**: So what’s the real chance of a sparkly egg?  
> - ✅ 1 in 10 → Yes. 3 in 30 is the same as 1 in 10. As a fraction, one tenth: 1/10. The poster said 1 in 3. That would be ten sparkly in thirty. We saw three.
> - 1 in 3 → That’s what the poster says. If it were true, we’d have seen ten sparkly in thirty. We saw three. Three groups of ten, one sparkly in each: 1 in 10.
> - 3 in 10 → That would be nine sparkly in thirty. We only saw three. Three groups of ten, one sparkly in each: 1 in 10. One tenth.
> _For grown-ups: Read a tally chart and saw that 3 in 30 is the same chance as 1 in 10 (one tenth)._

**Byte**: The poster promised ten sparkly. We got three. Ten divided by three is three point three three three… and the threes go on forever.  
**Byte**: That’s called recurring. I’ve underlined it. Twice.  
_Pip sits on the bridge with four plain eggs in her lap. She isn’t crying. She is being extremely brave at the water._  
**Pip**: The plain ones are nice too. They’re warm.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): helping a friend with disappointment  
> _Pip is disappointed, and trying very hard to be brave. What might help her?_  
> - ✅ Sit with her, and tell her it’s all right to feel disappointed. → Yes. You sit on the bridge beside her. Feelings are allowed, even the droopy ones. …They are quite warm, though.
> - Tell her to cheer up. → People often say that. But it can make someone feel they’re not allowed to be sad. Sitting with her, and saying it’s all right to feel disappointed, helps more.
> - Buy her another egg. → That’s a kind thought. But remember the tally: another egg would very likely be plain too. Sitting with her, and saying it’s all right to feel disappointed, might help most.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about how to comfort a friend who is disappointed._


#### `reader:s15-sneak` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-sneak)

**Byte**: We need a witness. Someone who’s seen the books.  
**Byte**: That parrot. Every time Dashwood says something untrue, it corrects him. I don’t think it can help it.  
_After lunch the wagon goes quiet. The mustard waistcoat hangs over the step. Inside, a snore like a slow saw._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): what a simile tells you  
> _Dashwood’s snore is “like a slow saw”. What does that tell you about it?_  
> - ✅ It’s a long, rough, rasping sound. → Yes. A saw going slowly through wood goes rrrrasp… rrrrasp. So does Dashwood. Comparing two things with “like” or “as” is called a simile.
> - He’s sawing wood in his sleep. → Ha! No. The snore only sounds like a saw. It’s a comparison. A slow saw goes rrrrasp, so the snore is long and rough. Comparing with “like” is a simile.
> - It’s a very quiet snore. → A saw isn’t quiet. Think of the sound: rrrrasp… rrrrasp. So the snore is long and rough. Comparing with “like” is a simile.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out what a simile (“a snore like a slow saw”) tells the reader._

_Jackpot sits on a perch at the open window, ticking. His eyes are brass buttons. One is sewn back on with red thread._  
**Byte**: Careful of the bells. Touch the wagon and they’ll all ring. Come round the stream side. There’s a gap where one fell off.  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-PS4-1, notice): sound is made by vibration  
> **Byte**: A bell rings because it vibrates. It shivers, very fast, when something knocks it.  
> **Byte**: The shivers shake the air, and the air carries the sound to your ears.  
> **Byte**: Bump the wagon, and a hundred bells shiver at once. Hence: tiptoe.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a bell makes sound by vibrating, and the air carries the sound._

_Stepping stones lead along the stream. One wobbles. One is a frog._  

#### `reader:s15-jackpot` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-jackpot)

**Jackpot**: GOOD AFTERNOON. I CANNOT LIE. IT IS A FAULT IN MY SPRINGS.  
**You**: How many eggs are sparkly, Jackpot?  
**Jackpot**: ONE IN TEN. HE KNOWS. HE WROTE IT IN THE LEDGER IN VERY SMALL WRITING.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiplying by 10 in a money problem  
> **Byte**: This morning the children bought thirty eggs, at ten Shells each. How many Shells did Dashwood take?  
> - ✅ 300 Shells → Yes. 30 × 10 = 300. Three hundred Shells, for three sparkly eggs.
> - 40 Shells → That’s 30 + 10. But every one of the thirty eggs cost ten Shells. Thirty lots of ten: 30 × 10 = 300.
> - 30 Shells → That would be one Shell an egg. They were ten each. 30 × 10 = 300 Shells.
> _For grown-ups: Multiplied 30 × 10 to work out money taken in a story problem._

**Byte**: And the poster?  
**Jackpot**: HE SAYS THE POSTER IS A POEM. I CANNOT LIE. A POEM IS NOT A NUMBER.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): telling a fact you can check from a claim you can’t  
> **Jackpot**: I CANNOT LIE. WHICH OF THESE CAN BE CHECKED?  
> - ✅ “One egg in ten is sparkly.” → CORRECT. YOU COUNTED IT. A FACT CAN BE CHECKED. Right. “Every egg a winner” is a feeling pretending to be a fact. You can’t measure it.
> - “Every egg a winner!” → HOW WOULD YOU CHECK IT? WHAT IS A WINNER? HE NEVER SAYS. That’s a feeling pretending to be a fact. “One in ten” is a fact: we counted it.
> - “Dashwood has brought you luck!” → LUCK CANNOT BE WEIGHED. I HAVE TRIED. That’s a claim you can’t check. “One in ten” is a fact: we counted it.
> _For grown-ups: Sorted a checkable fact from claims that can’t be checked._

**You**: What happens to the eggs nobody buys?  
**Jackpot**: THEY GO UP THE VALLEY. TO A MAN WHO BUYS IN BULK. HE NEVER HAGGLES. HE NEVER SMILES.  
_Among the stickers on the tailboard, you notice one small, neat silver square: the mark of Corvin’s Circuit, up the valley._  
**Jackpot**: ALSO THE LEGENDARY EGG IS NOT LEGENDARY. I HAVE TRIED NOT TO SAY THAT. IT IS VERY UNCOMFORTABLE.  
_The snoring stops. You both freeze. It starts again._  

#### `reader:s15-legendary` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-legendary)

_Dashwood wakes up exactly as charming as he went to sleep. He polishes the Legendary egg on his sleeve._  
**Dashwood Prance**: Admiring her? Course you are. Hold her if you like. One Shell a minute. Feel the luck soak in.  
**Byte**: Here’s three Shells. We’ll need exactly three minutes.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals: a quarter of an hour  
> **Byte**: One Shell a minute. How many Shells would it cost to hold her for a quarter of an hour?  
> - ✅ 15 Shells → Yes. An hour is 60 minutes, so a quarter of an hour is 15. Fifteen Shells. I am not paying that.
> - 4 Shells → Four is how many quarters make an hour. But one quarter of 60 minutes is 15 minutes. So, 15 Shells.
> - 25 Shells → Close-ish. An hour is 60 minutes. Split it into four equal parts: 15 each. So, 15 Shells.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out that a quarter of an hour is 15 minutes._

_Under the bridge it is dim and green, and smells of watercress. Byte lights the stub of a lantern._  
**Byte**: Candling. Hazel showed everybody. Shine a light behind the egg, and you can see what’s growing.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): candling: light passes through a thin eggshell  
> **Byte**: Farmers really do this with hens’ eggs. An eggshell is translucent: it lets some light through, but not a clear picture.  
> **Byte**: Shine a bright light behind it, and anything inside shows up as a shadow.  
> **Byte**: That’s why we’re under the bridge. In the dark, the glow is much easier to see.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how candling works: a thin eggshell lets light through, showing shadows inside._

**Byte**: A sparkly egg glows all through, like a pearl. A plain one shows a little curled shadow. This one… we’ll see.  

#### `reader:s15-painted` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-painted)

_The light comes through the shell. The glitter on it throws stars all over the underside of the bridge._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): understanding a metaphor  
> _The glitter “throws stars” all over the bridge. Are there real stars under the bridge?_  
> - ✅ No. It means little sparkles of light that look like stars. → Yes. Describing one thing as if it really were another is called a metaphor.
> - Yes, real stars. → That would be a very crowded bridge! The sparkles only look like stars. Describing one thing as if it really were another is called a metaphor.
> - Someone threw the glitter at the bridge. → Nobody threw anything. The light bounces off the glitter and makes little star-like sparkles. Describing one thing as if it really were another is called a metaphor.
> _For grown-ups: Explained what a metaphor (“throws stars”) means._

_Under the glitter, the egg glows plain pink. Inside is a small curled shadow, and it’s moving._  
**Byte**: Plain. It’s a plain egg, painted. There’s a thumbprint in the varnish. A big one.  
**Byte**: A healthy plain egg, mind. It’ll hatch in about a week. It just won’t be legendary. It’ll be a Nubbin.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, notice): what grows inside an egg  
> **Byte**: That curled shadow is the baby growing inside. In a bird’s egg it’s called an embryo.  
> **Byte**: It feeds on the yolk while it grows. When it’s big enough, it hatches, and a new life cycle begins.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that an embryo grows inside an egg, fed by the yolk, as part of a life cycle._

**Byte**: So the LEGENDARY card was a lie. And it’s still a perfectly good egg. Both of those are true.  
_Glitter comes off on your fingers. It doesn’t come off for days._  

#### `reader:s15-verdict` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-verdict)

_You come back up the bank with glitter on your hands. Dashwood sees it, and his smile stays exactly where it is._  
_He steers you round the back of the wagon, where the children can’t see, and sits down on the step._  
**Dashwood Prance**: When I was eight, a man came through my village with a wagon. Not as nice as mine.  
**Dashwood Prance**: He sold me a golden egg for every Shell I had. I carried it home in my hat. I slept curled round it like a cat.  
**Dashwood Prance**: It hatched brown. Brown as a boot. It had been painted.  
**Dashwood Prance**: And I loved that brown thing more than anything since. The best week of my life was the week before it hatched.  
**Dashwood Prance**: That’s what I sell, friend. Not eggs. The week before.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): understanding a character’s motive from his past  
> _What does Dashwood mean when he says he sells “the week before”?_  
> - ✅ The exciting feeling of waiting and hoping. → Yes. That happy, fizzy, waiting feeling has a name: anticipation. Dashwood had it once, as a boy. He’s been trying to sell it ever since.
> - Eggs that are a week old. → Not quite. Think about his story. The best week was the one before his egg hatched. He means the fizzy feeling of hoping. That feeling is called anticipation.
> - Nothing. He’s making it up. → Jackpot says he believes it, and Jackpot can’t lie. He means the fizzy feeling of waiting and hoping. That feeling is called anticipation.
> _For grown-ups: Used a character’s childhood story to understand why he does what he does._

**Jackpot**: HE BELIEVES THIS. I CANNOT LIE. HE DOES.  
_Round the front of the wagon, the children are waiting. Pip holds her four plain eggs. Byte holds the tally._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): thinking about honesty and fairness in selling  
> _Dashwood sells hope, and he really believes in it. But his poster wasn’t true._  
> _What do you think? Is it fair to sell hope with a poster that isn’t true?_  
> - No. People should know the real odds, and then choose. → That’s a fair answer. Byte would agree: Pip spent her birthday Shells on a promise that wasn’t true. Knowing the truth lets people make their own choice.
> - Maybe. People enjoyed the hoping. → That’s a fair answer too. Dashwood’s best week ever was a week of hoping. Byte would ask: would Pip still have bought four eggs, if she’d known it was 1 in 10?
> - He could sell the hope, and tell the truth. → That’s a thoughtful answer. Maybe the week before is just as lovely when you know the odds. An honest stall might be the way to find out.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether hope can be sold fairly when the advertising isn’t true._


**Choices**

- **Show everyone the tally and the painted egg.**
    - You climb on the wagon step and hold up the painted egg. Byte reads the tally aloud, slowly, including the recurring.
    - **The children**: ONE IN TEN?
    - The roof bells ring as children kick the wheels. Dashwood’s smile finally goes, and something small looks out from behind it.
    - **Dashwood Prance**: It was a poem. It was only ever a poem.
    - He drives off before dark. He leaves his spare trestle, a crate of eggs, and the parrot, who refuses to get on.
    - **Jackpot**: I CANNOT LIE. I DID NOT WANT TO GO.
- **Make him post the true odds and give the children their Shells back.**
    - **Dashwood Prance**: Refunds? Refunds! Do you know what that does to a man’s margins?
    - **Jackpot**: HIS MARGINS ARE ENORMOUS.
    - Dashwood counts Shells back into small hands, one by one. He does it with a flourish. He can’t help that either.
    - He paints out the 3 on his poster and paints in a 10. Then, after a long look, he adds: STILL WORTH IT.
    - **Pip**: I’m keeping one egg. I want to see what comes out. I don’t mind if it’s plain.
    - He leaves the valley the next morning with a lighter crate. The parrot stays, by very honest agreement.

#### `reader:s15-odds-board` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-odds-board)

_Byte nails up a slate. He chalks it in big, honest numbers: PLAIN 9 IN 10. SPARKLY 1 IN 10. LEGENDARY: NO._  
_Underneath, smaller: ALL OF THEM ARE WARM._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.NF.A.1, ask): tenths that make a whole  
> **Byte**: 9 in 10 plain. 1 in 10 sparkly. Nine tenths and one tenth. What do they make together?  
> - ✅ Ten tenths: one whole. Every egg. → Yes. 9/10 + 1/10 = 10/10, which is one whole. Every egg is either plain or sparkly.
> - Ten twentieths. → Careful. When the bottom numbers match, you only add the top numbers. Tenths stay tenths. 9/10 + 1/10 = 10/10: one whole. Every egg.
> - Half the eggs. → Half would be five tenths. Nine and one make ten tenths: the whole crate. Every egg is plain or sparkly.
> _For grown-ups: Added tenths (9/10 + 1/10) to see that they make one whole._

**Pip**: Those are worse odds. Why do I like it better?  
**Byte**: Because you know where you are. Knowing where you are is the second-best feeling there is. After winning.  
_The children queue anyway. Some buy. Some just come to read the board._  
**Pip**: Gran gave me ten Shells, for being brave about the eggs. An egg, or a bag of honey drops for the walk home?  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Eco.1.K-2, notice): scarcity: having only so much means choosing  
> **Byte**: Ten Shells can be an egg, or honey drops. Not both. When you only have so much, you have to choose.  
> **Byte**: That’s why true numbers matter. You can’t choose well if the board is telling you a poem.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that having limited money means choosing, and that honest information helps people choose._


#### `reader:s15-complete` · [main](../../../side/S15.md#s15-complete)

_The last egg in the crate is wrapped in a red spotted handkerchief. It glows all through, like a pearl in a lamp._  
**Jackpot**: THAT ONE IS REAL. I CANNOT LIE. I CHECKED. I CHECKED TWICE.  
_Tucked in the knot is a card in loopy gold writing: “For the Keeper. Every egg a winner. This one actually. —D.P.”_  
**Byte**: Statistically, that’s the only certain thing he ever sold.  

> **Lesson** (maths, ask): the language of chance: certain, unlikely, impossible  
> **Byte**: Chance words. Picking a sparkly egg from Dashwood’s full crate, with your eyes shut. Was it…  
> **Byte**: certain, unlikely or impossible?  
> - ✅ Unlikely. → Yes. 1 in 10 is unlikely, but not impossible. Three children did get one. This egg, checked twice? Certain. That’s the difference.
> - Certain. → Certain means it must happen. Twenty-seven of the thirty eggs were plain, so it wasn’t certain. 1 in 10 is unlikely.
> - Impossible. → Impossible means it can never happen. But three children did get sparkly ones. 1 in 10 is unlikely, not impossible.
> _For grown-ups: Used the words certain, unlikely and impossible to describe chance._

_Jackpot hops onto your shoulder. He ticks. He weighs about as much as a teapot._  
**Jackpot**: I WOULD LIKE TO LIVE WITH YOU. I CANNOT LIE. I HAVE WANTED TO SAY THAT FOR A WHILE.  
_On his way out, Dashwood pushed the painted egg into Pip’s hands. She hasn’t let go of it since._  
_Pip carries the painted egg home in her hat. A week later it hatches, brown as a boot._  
_She loves it beyond all reason._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.2, ask): finding the theme of a story  
> _Pip loves her plain brown Nubbin beyond all reason. What do you think this story is saying?_  
> - ✅ Plain things can be precious, and the truth is worth knowing. → Yes. That big idea is the story’s theme. Pip knew the truth, chose anyway, and loves what she got.
> - Sparkly things are always best. → Dashwood might say so! But look at Pip. Her plain brown Nubbin is the thing she loves most. The story’s big idea, its theme, is that plain things can be precious, and the truth is worth knowing.
> - Never buy an egg. → The children still buy eggs at the honest stall. They just know the odds now. The story’s big idea, its theme, is that plain things can be precious, and the truth is worth knowing.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out the theme, the big idea, of a story._


### Glossary

- **approximately**: Roughly; close to a number, but not exactly. Often shortened to “approx.”
- **legendary**: So special or famous that people tell stories about it.
- **tally**: A quick way of counting by making a mark for each thing, in bundles of five.
- **recurring**: Happening again and again. A recurring number repeats forever, like 3.333…
- **witness**: Someone who saw or knows what really happened.
- **ledger**: A book where someone writes down money and things bought and sold.
- **bulk**: A very large amount, bought all at once.
- **candling**: Shining a light through an egg to see what is growing inside.
- **translucent**: Letting some light through, but not clearly enough to see through.
- **varnish**: A clear, shiny coat painted on to protect something or make it gleam.
- **embryo**: A baby animal in its very first stage, before it is born or hatched.
- **anticipation**: The excited feeling of waiting for something you are looking forward to.
- **margin**: The money a seller keeps after paying for what they sell.
- **odds**: How likely something is to happen, like 1 in 10.
- **statistically**: Going by the numbers you have counted.
- **certain**: Sure to happen.
