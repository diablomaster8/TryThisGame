# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S16: The Workyard

## S16. The Workyard

- `s16-step1`: Go upriver with Sparks and Clover and see the Workyard. · _Follow the river path north, past the stepping stones. You’ll hear the saws long before you see the sheds._
- `s16-step2`: Watch the Workyard’s Nubbins for a day. Write down what each one does when nobody is asking. · _Stay hidden behind the log piles and sheds. If a Nubbin sees you, it starts working to be polite, and that note doesn’t count._
- `s16-step3`: Build a Fair Roster with Brisk: jobs the Nubbins like, rests, fruit breaks, a bell, and no more treadwheel. · _Put each Nubbin’s card under the job it chose when nobody was asking. Then hold Sparks’s ladder while she moves the lights to the waterwheel._
- `s16-step4`: Go back to the Workyard in a week, and see how the Fair Roster is working. · _Any day from a week on. Follow the river path north. Brisk will have the kettle on._

#### `reader:s16-sparks` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-sparks)

_Sparks turns up with a coil of wire over each shoulder, like a very small, very loud pirate electrician._  
**Sparks**: Job upriver! A big one! A sawmill wants lights in every shed. I need someone to hold the ladder and say “wow”.  
**Sparks**: The owner’s called Brisk Hollins. Nice man. Very nice. Suspiciously cheerful. I’ve brought Clover. She’s good with ladders.  
**Clover**: I’m not good with ladders. I’m good at not falling off them. It’s different.  
_The river path is soft after the rain. Somewhere ahead the saws are going: a bright, busy whine, like wasps in a jar._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-2, notice): sound bends round things; light travels in straight lines  
> **Clover**: Funny. We can hear the saws, but we can’t see them.  
> **Sparks**: Sound spreads out and bends round trees and corners. Light goes in straight lines, so the trees block the view.  
> **Sparks**: That’s why you hear a sawmill long before you see it. Same with thunder. And me.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that sound can travel round obstacles, while light travels in straight lines._

**Sparks**: He says his Nubbins love it there. Love it! He wrote it in his letter twice, and underlined it both times.  
**Clover**: Twice is a lot of underlining.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): reading between the lines  
> _Why does Clover think twice is a lot of underlining?_  
> - ✅ When someone insists very hard, something might not be quite right. → Yes. Clover is reading between the lines: noticing what the words don’t say. If the Nubbins really loved it, would Brisk need to underline it twice?
> - She thinks underlining is untidy. → Ha. Maybe! But Clover is noticing something else. When someone insists very hard, it can be a clue that something isn’t quite right. That’s reading between the lines.
> - She doesn’t like Brisk. → She hasn’t met him yet! She’s noticing a clue in his letter. When someone insists very hard, it can mean something isn’t quite right. That’s reading between the lines.
> _For grown-ups: Noticed a clue in how something was written, not just what it said._


#### `reader:s16-yard` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-yard)

_The Workyard opens out at a bend in the river. Sheds, log piles, a waterwheel that isn’t turning, and Nubbins. Dozens._  
_Each one wears a tiny hard hat made from half a walnut shell, painted yellow. They chop, they stack, they haul._  
_Over the gate, a banner says WORK IS PLAY! In the middle of the yard, three Nubbins run inside a big wooden treadwheel._  
_Wires run from the treadwheel to every shed. Every bulb in the yard flickers in time with their feet._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-4, ask): a device that changes energy from one form to another  
> _Why do the bulbs flicker in time with the Nubbins’ feet?_  
> - ✅ Their running turns the wheel, and the wheel makes the electricity. → Yes! The energy changes form: running feet, turning wheel, electricity, light. Every step is a push. Between pushes the wheel slows a bit, so the lights flicker.
> - The bulbs are dancing. → I wish! No. The running feet turn the wheel, and the wheel makes the electricity. Energy changes form: moving feet, turning wheel, electricity, light. Each push makes a flicker.
> - The wind is blowing the wires. → Good guess, but the flicker matches their feet exactly. The running turns the wheel, and the wheel makes the electricity. Energy changes form: moving feet, turning wheel, electricity, light.
> _For grown-ups: Explained how a treadwheel changes movement energy into electricity and then light._

**Brisk Hollins**: Welcome! Brisk Hollins. Pleased as punch. Output’s up this month. More logs than ever!  
**Brisk Hollins**: I’m no Circuit man, mind. Mine get Sundays off.  
_A big Power Nubbin trundles past with a log three times his size on his back, humming. His hat says LUG in pencil._  
**Brisk Hollins**: That’s Lug. Best worker I’ve got. Can’t stop him. Tried once. He sulked for a week.  
_Behind the log pile sit five Nubbins. Their colours have worn grey at the edges, like a flag left out all summer._  
**Clover**: Those ones are tired. Not lazy. Tired.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, notice): reading how someone feels from their body  
> **Clover**: Look at how they sit. Slumped. Eyes half shut. Colours faded. Bodies tell you a lot, if you look.  
> **Clover**: Lazy means not wanting to. Tired means your body can’t any more. They need different things.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned to notice how someone feels by looking at their body, and that tired is not the same as lazy._


#### `reader:s16-shadow` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-shadow)

**Brisk Hollins**: Tired? Nonsense! Look at the chart!  
_He unrolls a chart. A red line climbs so steeply it runs off the paper and carries on, in pencil, across the table._  
**Clover**: That chart is about logs. I’m asking about them.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.B.3, ask): a graph only shows what it measures  
> _Look at the labels on Brisk’s chart. What does it actually measure?_  
> - ✅ How many logs get made. → Yes. A chart can only tell you about the thing it counts. This one counts logs. It can’t tell you how a single Nubbin feels.
> - How happy the Nubbins are. → That’s what Brisk thinks it shows. But read the labels: LOGS, and WEEKS. A chart can only tell you about the thing it counts. This one counts logs, not feelings.
> - How long the table is. → Ha. The line does run all the way across it. But read the labels: LOGS, and WEEKS. A chart only tells you about the thing it counts. This one counts logs.
> _For grown-ups: Read a graph’s labels to see what it does and doesn’t measure._

**Brisk Hollins**: Same thing! Happy Nubbins, happy logs. The logs are very happy.  
**Sparks**: Brisk. When nobody’s telling them what to do, what do they do?  
_Brisk opens his mouth to answer. He shuts it again. He has clearly never once wondered._  
**Brisk Hollins**: …Well. Good question. Splendid question. Find out! Have a clipboard. I’ve got forty.  
**Clover**: Observe them from where they can’t see you. If they know they’re watched, they’ll work to be polite. Nubbins are like that.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): observing animals without changing what they do  
> **Clover**: Scientists who study wild animals often watch from a hide: a little hut or screen the animals can’t see into.  
> **Clover**: That way the animals behave as they really would. And the notes are true.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that scientists observe animals from hiding so the animals behave naturally._


**Choices**

- **Start with Lug.**
    - Lug puts down a log, sees you, and at once picks up a bigger one, to show you. You’ll need to be sneakier.
- **Start with the tired ones behind the log pile.**
    - **Clover**: Good. Start with the ones nobody’s counting.
- **Start with the treadwheel.**
    - **Sparks**: The wheel. Yeah. That thing’s bothered me since we walked in. It’s wired backwards, for a start.

#### `reader:s16-notes` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-notes)

_By sundown your clipboard is full of small handwriting and one large sawdust thumbprint._  
_You watched eight Nubbins. Here is what each one chose, when nobody was asking._  
_The two choppers chop on their breaks too. They like the thunk. One chops the same stump all day, very happily, for no reason._  
_The stacker stacks. The two wheel runners step out the moment Brisk turns his back, and sit under the bench with their eyes shut._  
_The two tired ones you watched never once choose work. They lie in a patch of sun and watch the river go by._  
_And Lug, on his breaks, carries offcuts. Not to the stack. Out to the bank, behind the reeds, one at a time._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.B.3, do): turning notes into a bar chart and reading it  
> _Turn your clipboard into a bar chart. One bar for each choice: CHOP 2, STACK 1, REST 4, CARRY 1._  
> _Which bar is tallest? REST: four of the eight Nubbins. Four out of eight is one half._  
> _Half the Nubbins you watched chose rest, when nobody was asking._  
> Check: `edu:chart:bar:nubbin-choices`
> _For grown-ups: Made a bar chart from observation notes, found the tallest bar, and saw that 4 out of 8 is one half._

_You follow him. In the reeds stands a little lean-to of planks and bark, roofed with moss. All five tired ones are asleep inside._  
**Clover**: He built them a house.  
**Sparks**: Nobody asked him to. He just… did.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): using evidence to explain a character’s actions  
> _Why do you think Lug built the lean-to?_  
> - ✅ He saw the tired ones needed somewhere to rest, and he cared. → Yes. The evidence: he carried the wood on his own breaks, one piece at a time, and nobody asked. Lug never speaks. His actions say it for him.
> - Brisk told him to. → Look at the evidence: Sparks says nobody asked Lug. Brisk didn’t even know it was there. Lug saw the tired ones needed somewhere to rest, and he cared.
> - He wanted to show off. → Show-offs like an audience. Lug hid the lean-to behind the reeds. He saw the tired ones needed somewhere to rest, and he cared. His actions say it for him.
> _For grown-ups: Used evidence from the story to explain why a character did something._


#### `reader:s16-roster` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-roster)

_You show Brisk the clipboard, and then the lean-to in the reeds. He stands in the wet grass a long time._  
**Brisk Hollins**: I thought they loved it. They looked like they loved it. They were always so busy.  
**Brisk Hollins**: …Busy isn’t the same thing, is it.  
_He takes down WORK IS PLAY! and puts up a new banner: WORK IS… OFTEN PLAY!_  
**Sparks**: That’s worse.  
**Brisk Hollins**: I know. I’m working on it.  
**Sparks**: Right. That treadwheel’s coming off the wires. Your lights go on the waterwheel. Like a normal person’s.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-PS3-4, notice): the waterwheel: a wheel and axle that turns moving water into electricity  
> **Sparks**: A waterwheel is a simple machine: a wheel and axle. The axle is the rod in the middle. It turns with the wheel.  
> **Sparks**: The river pushes the paddles round. Hook the axle to a generator, and the moving water’s energy becomes electricity.  
> **Sparks**: And a river never gets tired. It just keeps coming.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a waterwheel is a wheel and axle, and that it can change moving water’s energy into electricity._

**Clover**: And every Nubbin gets a card on a roster, under the job it picked. And breaks. With fruit.  

> **Lesson** (health, ask): why bodies need rest, food and water  
> **Clover**: Why do you think workers need breaks, with fruit?  
> - ✅ Working tires your muscles. Rest, food and water help your body recover. → Yes. Muscles get tired when they work hard. Rest lets them recover, and food and water refuel you.
> - Breaks are just for being lazy. → Lots of people think that. But a tired body works badly, and can get hurt. Rest lets tired muscles recover, and food and water refuel you.
> - Only weak workers need breaks. → Even the strongest runners rest between races. Even Lug. Every body’s muscles get tired. Rest lets them recover, and food and water refuel you.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that rest, food and water help a tired body recover._

**Brisk Hollins**: And the ones who don’t want a job at all? What then? What’s a yard for?  
_Nobody answers. Brisk puts up another banner: REST IS A KIND OF WORK?? It has two question marks._  

#### `reader:s16-bell` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-bell)

_By evening the Fair Roster hangs by the gate: a board of little painted cards, one for every Nubbin, under the job it chose._  
_Beside it hangs a small brass bell, low enough for the smallest Nubbin to reach. Clover has painted on it: NO THANK YOU._  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, ask): fair rules work for everyone  
> _Why did Clover hang the bell so low?_  
> - ✅ So everyone, even the smallest Nubbin, can use it. → Yes. A rule is only fair if everyone can use it. A bell the small ones can’t reach would be no use to them.
> - So nobody steals it. → A low bell is actually easier to take! Clover had a better reason. She wanted everyone, even the smallest Nubbin, to reach it. A fair rule works for everybody.
> - Because Clover is short. → I heard that. She hung it low so everyone, even the smallest Nubbin, could reach it. A fair rule works for everybody.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why fair rules must work for everyone, including the smallest._

_The big treadwheel stands still. Sparks has hung a sign on it: RETIRED. A Nubbin is already asleep inside it._  
_A wheel runner waddles to the bell. It looks round at everyone. Then it rings it. Once. Very softly._  
_Then it goes and sits in the sun. Nobody stops it. It looks astonished._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, notice): saying no, and having it respected  
> **Clover**: The wheel runner looks surprised because nobody ever listened to its “no” before.  
> **Clover**: Saying no is allowed. When you ask first, and listen to the answer, that’s called consent.  
> **Clover**: It goes for people too. Your body, your answer. And a good friend listens.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that saying no is allowed, and that asking first and listening is called consent._

**Brisk Hollins**: Well, I’ll be. I’ll be. Output’s going to be awful.  

#### `reader:s16-question` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-question)

_Brisk walks you to the river path, hands in his pockets. He has a new slogan in his head. You can tell._  
**Brisk Hollins**: Can I ask you something, Keeper? Honestly? Your lot at the Grove. They fetch you things, don’t they?  
**Brisk Hollins**: Apples. Sticks. Do you ask them? Or do they just do it, because you’re the one who feeds them?  

> **Lesson** (science, K-LS1-1, notice): what animals need to be well  
> _Real working animals, like sheepdogs and horses, need more than food._  
> _They need water, shelter, rest, a chance to play, and someone who notices how they are feeling._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that working animals need water, shelter, rest and play, not just food._

_You think of your Nubbins dragging fallen apples to your door. How pleased they look. How pleased you look back._  
**You**: I don’t know.  
**Brisk Hollins**: No. Me neither. That’s the trouble with being liked. You never find out what they’d do if they didn’t like you.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): thinking about fairness and consent for animals  
> _Brisk has asked a hard question. When an animal does a job because you feed it, is it really choosing?_  
> _What do you think?_  
> - Yes. If it looks happy, it’s choosing. → That’s a fair answer. Lug really does love carrying logs. Clover would add: the NO THANK YOU bell is how you find out for sure.
> - Not always. It might just want the food. → That’s a fair point. Brisk’s Nubbins looked busy, and some of them were worn out. Asking, and listening for a no, is how you find out.
> - You can’t know unless it’s allowed to say no. → That’s a thoughtful answer. It’s exactly what the bell is for. A yes only really counts if no was allowed too.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about whether an animal that works for food is really choosing, and why a “no” must be allowed._

**Brisk Hollins**: Now. The five in the reeds. They don’t want to work. Not a bit. What do I do with them?  

**Choices**

- **Send them to the Grove. They can rest with you.**
    - **Brisk Hollins**: …Yes. Yes, that’s kind. Lug will want to visit. Lug will want to carry them there.
    - Lug, who has been listening, is already lifting the whole lean-to off the ground, with them inside it.
- **Keep them on, as “morale officers”.**
    - **Brisk Hollins**: Morale officers! Officially in charge of sitting in the sun and being lovely. Yes!
    - **Clover**: They’ll be the best in the yard at it. Leave them where Lug can see them.
    - **Sparks**: Are we sure that isn’t just a job with a nicer hat?
- **Let them go wild, up the river.**
    - **Brisk Hollins**: Wild? Out there? Who’ll feed them? Who’ll—
    - **Clover**: They will, maybe. Some Nubbins are happiest belonging to nobody.
    - Brisk looks up the river a long while. He doesn’t like it. He doesn’t say no.

#### `reader:s16-week` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-week)

_A week later the Workyard is louder, and slower. The saws stop for fruit at eleven o’clock and at three. Somebody has hung a hammock._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals in hours  
> _Fruit at eleven o’clock, and again at three in the afternoon. How long between the two fruit breaks?_  
> - ✅ 4 hours → Yes. Eleven to twelve is one hour. Twelve to three is three more. 1 + 3 = 4 hours.
> - 8 hours → That’s 11 − 3. But clocks go round: after twelve comes one again. Count on: eleven to twelve is one hour, twelve to three is three more. 4 hours.
> - 3 hours → Nearly! Twelve to three is three hours. But don’t forget eleven to twelve. 1 + 3 = 4 hours.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out a time interval across noon by counting on in hours._

_Brisk’s banner has been through several drafts. You can read them all, crossed out, one under the other:_  
_WORK IS PLAY! · WORK IS… OFTEN PLAY! · REST IS A KIND OF WORK?? · ASK FIRST._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): drafting and redrafting  
> _Brisk wrote four drafts. Which one says best what he learned?_  
> - ✅ ASK FIRST. → Yes. Two words that hold the whole story. Writers often need several drafts. Each try gets a little closer to what they really mean.
> - WORK IS PLAY! → That was his first draft, before he watched the Nubbins. It turned out not to be true for all of them. Writers often need several drafts to find what they mean. His last one, ASK FIRST, says what he learned.
> - REST IS A KIND OF WORK?? → He was getting closer! But two question marks means he wasn’t sure yet. Writers often need several drafts to find what they mean. ASK FIRST says what he learned.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that writers redraft to get closer to what they mean, and chose the clearest draft._

**Brisk Hollins**: Output’s down eleven per cent. Lights stay on, mind. The waterwheel’s steadier than three tired Nubbins. Who knew?  
**Sparks**: I knew. I said. I said it loudly.  
_Lug trundles past with a log on his back, humming. Nobody asked him. He just likes it._  

#### `reader:s16-complete` · [main](../../../side/S16.md#s16-complete)

**Brisk Hollins**: Here. Can’t have a Keeper on my yard without a hat. Regulations. My regulations. New ones.  
_A yellow hard hat in your size, painted to match the walnut-shell ones. On the brim, in Lug’s pencil: KEEPER._  

> **Lesson** (health, notice): how a hard hat protects your head  
> _A real hard hat has a tough shell, and straps inside that hold it a little way off your head._  
> _If something drops on it, the shell and the gap take the knock, not your head. That’s why builders wear them._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how a hard hat protects the head, and why builders wear them._

**Brisk Hollins**: And take a copy of the roster. Try it at the Grove. Let them pick their jobs, and see what they pick.  
**Clover**: And see what they don’t. That’s just as worth knowing.  
_Back home you nail the Fair Roster by the cottage door. By morning, three Nubbins have pushed their cards under jobs._  
_One has chosen watering. One has chosen fetching apples. One has pushed its card under “sitting with Moss”, and won’t be moved._  
**Sparks**: That last one isn’t a job.  
**Clover**: It is to Moss.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.6, ask): your point of view and a character’s  
> _Sparks says sitting with Moss isn’t a job. Clover says it is, to Moss. What’s your point of view?_  
> - Sparks is right. A job is making or fixing something. → That’s a fair point of view. Sparks likes jobs you can see: lights on, wires fixed. Clover sees it from Moss’s side. Same Nubbin, two ways of looking.
> - Clover is right. Keeping someone company helps them. → That’s a fair point of view too. Moss is old and fading, and company matters to him. Sparks sees it differently. Same Nubbin, two ways of looking.
> - Both. It depends whose eyes you look through. → That’s a thoughtful answer. To Sparks it’s sitting. To Moss it’s everything. Seeing a thing from more than one side is a big part of being fair.
> _For grown-ups: Compared their own point of view with two characters’ different views._


### Glossary

- **electrician**: Someone whose job is to fit and fix electric wires and lights.
- **suspiciously**: In a way that makes you think something might be wrong.
- **treadwheel**: A big wheel that turns when people or animals walk or run inside it.
- **output**: How much of something is made, like logs from a sawmill.
- **observe**: To watch something carefully, to learn about it.
- **hide**: Here: a small hut or screen where you can watch animals without them seeing you.
- **offcuts**: Small leftover pieces cut off a bigger piece of wood.
- **lean-to**: A simple shelter with a sloping roof, often propped against something.
- **roster**: A list that shows who does which job, and when.
- **axle**: The rod through the middle of a wheel, which turns with it or lets it turn.
- **consent**: Saying yes because you really want to, when saying no is allowed too.
- **astonished**: Very, very surprised.
- **slogan**: A short, catchy phrase that is easy to remember.
- **morale**: How cheerful and confident a group of people feels.
- **draft**: One try at writing something, before it is finished.
- **per cent**: Out of every hundred. Eleven per cent means eleven in every hundred.
- **regulations**: Official rules.
