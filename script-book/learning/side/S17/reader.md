# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S17: Nightjar

## S17. Nightjar

- `s17-step1`: Search the nursery and the fence for clues. · _Look closely at the straw, the nursery roof and the top rail of the fence. Show Luna anything you find._
- `s17-step2`: Creep through Luna’s garden to the hide by the nursery without being heard. · _Move when the wind rustles the leaves. Keep to moss and thyme, off the gravel, in the shadows of the big flowers._
- `s17-step3`: Climb slowly to Nightjar’s nest on the cliff. · _Use the cracks with ferns growing in them: those are the solid ones. Keep still whenever she turns her head._
- `s17-step4`: Carry the Grove’s eggs back to the nursery, one at a time, while Nightjar watches. · _Carry each egg in both hands, and walk, don’t run. If you hurry, she follows you down and you start that egg again._
- `s17-step5`: Visit Nightjar’s nest on the night the wild egg hatches. · _Take the cliff path after dark. Sit below the ledge and wait. She shuffles aside when she’s ready._

#### `reader:s17-missing` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-missing)

_Autumn. The first conkers are down, and the nursery straw is damp with dew._  
_In the straw there is a round, warm dent where an egg was last night. It is the fourth dent this fortnight._  
**Luna**: One a night. Always the warmest one. Never a crack, never a crumb of shell. Whatever it is, it’s careful.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): using clues to back up an idea  
> _Luna says, “Whatever it is, it’s careful.” Which clue tells her that?_  
> - ✅ There’s never a crack or a crumb of shell. → Yes. A clumsy thief would break things. Not one crumb means something handled the eggs very gently.
> - It takes one egg a night. → That clue tells us how often it comes, not how it behaves. The careful clue is the shells: never a crack, never a crumb.
> - The straw is damp with dew. → The dew tells us it was a cool, damp night. It doesn’t tell us about the thief. The careful clue is the shells: never a crack, never a crumb.
> _For grown-ups: Picked the clue in the text that supports a character’s idea._

**Nova**: Morning. I’m not stopping. I was passing, and I heard there was a mystery, and I’m not stopping.  
**Nova**: Badger.  
**Luna**: It’s not a badger.  
**Nova**: A tall badger.  
**Luna**: Look at the straw before you trample it, both of you. Then we can talk about tall badgers.  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-LS1-2, notice): eggs need steady warmth to grow  
> **Luna**: Notice it always takes the warmest egg. A bird’s egg needs steady warmth, or the chick inside can’t grow.  
> **Luna**: That’s why parent birds spend most of the day and night sitting on their eggs.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a bird’s egg needs steady warmth from a parent for the chick inside to grow._


#### `reader:s17-clues` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-clues)

_On the top rail of the fence: a grey feather as long as your forearm, soft as smoke. Its edge is torn, as if on rock._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): choosing the right unit of length  
> _You want to write down how long the feather is. Which unit would you use?_  
> - ✅ Centimetres → Yes. Centimetres suit things you can hold in your hands. This feather is about 30 centimetres long.
> - Kilometres → A kilometre is a thousand metres: about a quarter of an hour’s walk. Far too big for a feather! Centimetres suit things you can hold. This feather is about 30 centimetres long.
> - Metres → A metre is about one big stride. The feather is much shorter than that. Centimetres fit better. This feather is about 30 centimetres long.
> _For grown-ups: Chose centimetres as the sensible unit for measuring a feather, comparing it with metres and kilometres._

_On the roof, faint scratches. Something landed very gently here, and left very gently._  
_And the dent in the straw is still warm, hours later. Something kept that egg warm until the last second._  
**Luna**: That’s an owl’s. A big owl. Bigger than an owl has any business being.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): how owls fly silently  
> **Luna**: Run your finger along the front edge. Feel that soft fringe, like the teeth of a comb?  
> **Luna**: Most birds’ feathers are stiff and smooth there. An owl’s break up the rushing air, so its wings hardly make a sound.  
> **Luna**: That’s how an owl flies silently. Mice don’t hear it coming. And nor did we.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the soft, comb-like edges of owl feathers let owls fly almost silently._

**Nova**: Two badgers in a coat, holding an owl feather to throw us off.  
**Luna**: Nova.  
**Nova**: It stays on the list. Lists should be long. Tonight, then? I’m not stopping. I’m just staying up.  

#### `reader:s17-night` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-night)

_After dark, Luna’s garden feels like another country. The white flowers are wide open, and the raked paths glow faintly._  
**Luna**: We watch the nursery from in here. The flower beds give shadow. The gravel doesn’t.  
**Luna**: Gravel tells everyone everything.  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): owls’ amazing hearing  
> **Luna**: We have to be quiet because owls hear astonishingly well.  
> **Luna**: Many owls have a round, flat face, like a dish. It gathers sounds and steers them to their ears.  
> **Luna**: Some owls can catch a mouse hidden under snow, just by listening for it.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that an owl’s dish-shaped face gathers sound, giving it superb hearing for hunting._

**Luna**: Stay on the moss. Stay low. If something lands, don’t stare at it. Owls hate being stared at. So do I.  
**Nova**: Can I bring the list?  
**Luna**: You can bring the list in your head. Quietly.  

> **Lesson** (science, 2-PS1-1, do): sorting materials by a property: quiet or noisy underfoot  
> _Before you set off, test the ground. Tap each patch: moss, thyme, gravel, dry leaves, an old snail shell._  
> _Sort them into quiet and noisy. Soft, springy things are quiet. Hard, brittle, crunchy things are noisy._  
> Check: `edu:sort:ground:quiet-noisy`
> _For grown-ups: Sorted materials into quiet and noisy by testing them, linking sound to how soft or hard they are._


**Choices**

- **Follow Luna’s path exactly.**
    - **Luna**: Good. Left foot on the thyme. It doesn’t mind. It smells nicer for it.
- **Find your own way through.**
    - You step on a snail shell. It cracks, as loud as a firework. The whole garden holds its breath. So does Luna.
    - **Luna**: …Your own way is very loud.

#### `reader:s17-reveal` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-reveal)

_Midnight. The wind drops. Something crosses the moon without a sound, and the garden goes still beneath it._  
_An owl lands on the nursery roof. She is enormous, grey as ash, and one long feather on her left wing is torn._  
_She lifts an egg out of the straw in her feet, slowly, the way you would carry a held breath._  
_Then she turns her head right round and looks at your hiding place. For a long moment she only looks._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, ask): why owls turn their heads  
> _You can look sideways just by moving your eyes. Why does an owl turn its whole head instead?_  
> - ✅ Its eyes can’t move in their sockets. → Yes. An owl’s eyes are shaped like short tubes, fixed in place. So it turns its head instead. Its neck bends so well that it can turn its head about three-quarters of the way round.
> - Because it’s showing off. → It does look like showing off! But an owl has to do it. Its eyes are fixed in place and can’t roll like ours. So it turns its head, nearly three-quarters of the way round.
> - To hear better. → Turning helps owls hear too. But the main reason is its eyes: they can’t move in their sockets. So it turns its whole head, about three-quarters of the way round.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that owls’ eyes are fixed in their sockets, so owls turn their heads to look around._

**Nightjar**: Hoo-oo.  
_Then she’s gone towards the cliffs, low over the hedges, carrying the egg as if it were the only thing in the world._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-2, notice): how owls see at night  
> **Luna**: How did she find the nursery at midnight? Her eyes. An owl’s eyes are huge for the size of its head.  
> **Luna**: Big eyes let in lots of light, so owls can see well when it’s nearly dark. Animals active at night are called nocturnal.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that owls’ very large eyes gather lots of light, helping them see at night._

**Nova**: …I’m crossing off the badgers.  
**Luna**: Nightjar. That’s what Hazel called her. I thought she was a story. The biggest owl in the valley, Hazel said.  
**Luna**: And the saddest.  

#### `reader:s17-cliffs` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-cliffs)

_Next night you follow the way she flew, past the ridge to the high cliffs. The wind smells of salt and cold stone._  
_Halfway up, wedged in a crack, is a ledge. On it is a nest the size of a cartwheel._  
**Nova**: I’ve slept up here. Years back. An owl called all night, the same two notes. I thought it was the wind.  
**Luna**: She hunts at the turn of the tide. That’s soon. We could climb up while she’s out.  
**Luna**: Or we could wait for her, and let her see us coming. Owls remember faces.  
**Luna**: Either way, slowly. It’s her home, not ours.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, ask): being fair to animals: not frightening them  
> _Luna says it’s Nightjar’s home. How would you feel if someone climbed into your home while you were out?_  
> - ✅ Scared or cross, coming back to find a stranger there. → Most of us would. Animals feel fear too. Letting her see you coming gives her the choice. That’s fairer, even if it’s slower.
> - I wouldn’t mind. → Maybe you’re very easy-going! But a wild mother on her nest would probably be frightened. Letting her see you coming gives her the choice. That’s fairer.
> - It depends who it was. → That’s true. A friend is different from a stranger. To Nightjar, you’re still a stranger. Letting her see you coming is how you start to stop being one.
> _For grown-ups: Put themselves in an animal’s place and thought about fair, gentle ways to approach wildlife._


**Choices**

- **Climb while she’s out hunting.**
    - You climb fast, hand over hand. You are nearly at the ledge when a shadow drops past you, silent, and settles.
    - She is home. She sits on the nest and looks at you, and does not move.
    - **Nova**: Well. She was always going to be home, wasn’t she.
- **Wait for her, where she can see you.**
    - You sit on a rock below the ledge, in plain view. She comes home at the turn of the tide and looks at you a long time.
    - Then she shuffles over, just a little, as if making room.
    - **Luna**: That’s an invitation. Or a warning. With owls it’s mostly both.

#### `reader:s17-nest` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-nest)

_The nest is woven of heather, sheep’s wool and grey feathers. Under her, packed snug in a ring, are eggs._  
_Five eggs. Four glow faintly, the way the Grove’s eggs do. Every one is whole. Every one is warm._  
_She has turned them so that each one gets its time in the middle._  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-LS1-2, notice): how birds incubate and turn their eggs  
> **Luna**: Real birds do this. Sitting on eggs to keep them warm is called incubating.  
> **Luna**: Many birds grow a bare patch of skin on their belly, so their warm skin touches the eggs.  
> **Luna**: And they turn their eggs often. It helps the chick inside grow properly and stops it sticking to the shell.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that birds incubate eggs with their body heat and turn them often to help the chicks grow._

_Woven deep into the lining, where they can never be dropped, are two very old eggshells, grey and cracked across._  
**Luna**: Hazel told me about a night before my time. Spring, blossom out. Then a cold came down the valley, out of season.  
**Luna**: Frost on the blossom. Ice in the stream. In the morning the owl on the cliff was calling and calling.  
**Luna**: Hazel said she never really stopped. That was twenty-two years ago.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Social awareness, notice): understanding grief  
> _Those two old eggs never hatched. The chicks inside died in the cold, and they can’t come back._  
> _Nightjar still keeps their shells. That deep sadness after losing someone you love is called grief._  
> _Grief can last a long time. It isn’t wrong. It shows how much she loved them._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the word grief, in plain words about death, and that grief is a sign of love._

_…_  
**Nova**: That number keeps turning up.  
**Nightjar**: Hoo-oo.  
_She settles lower over the eggs, the way you would pull a blanket up over someone already asleep._  

#### `reader:s17-return` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-return)

_She won’t let you take them all at once. You lift two, and she clacks her beak once, like a door shutting._  
**Luna**: One at a time, then. Down and back. Let her watch each one go, and see you come back for the next.  
**Nova**: That’s four climbs. In the dark.  
**Luna**: That’s four climbs. In the dark.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiplication as equal groups  
> _Nova says four climbs. But each egg needs one trip down and one trip back up._  
> _Four eggs. How many trips is that altogether?_  
> - ✅ 8 → Yes. 4 eggs, 2 trips each: 4 × 2 = 8. Down, up, down, up, down, up, down, up. Nova will be thrilled.
> - 4 → That’s just the trips down. You have to come back up each time too. 4 eggs, 2 trips each: 4 × 2 = 8 trips.
> - 6 → Close! Let’s count in pairs: down-up for each egg. 2, 4, 6, 8. 4 eggs, 2 trips each: 4 × 2 = 8 trips.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a trips problem as 4 equal groups of 2 (4 × 2 = 8)._

_Nightjar watches you lift the first egg._  
_Her eyes follow it all the way down, and all the way back up to you._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, notice): building trust slowly  
> **Luna**: This is why we go one at a time. Each trip shows her the egg is safe and you come back.  
> **Luna**: Trust gets built like that: small promises, kept, again and again.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that trust is built through small promises kept over and over._


#### `reader:s17-wild` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-wild)

_Dawn. Four eggs are back in the nursery straw. You climb up one last time for the lantern you left behind._  
_There is still one egg under her. Small, speckled like lichen, and it doesn’t glow. It isn’t the Grove’s._  
**Nova**: That’s nobody’s. Wild. Its mother never came back, I’d guess. It happens.  
_Nightjar looks at the egg, then at you. She does not hide it. She does not move off it, either._  
**Luna**: Leave it with her. Let somebody keep something warm and have it work.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): thinking about right and wrong when someone is hurting  
> _Nightjar took eggs that weren’t hers. But she kept every one safe and warm, because she was grieving._  
> _Was what she did wrong?_  
> - Yes. They weren’t hers to take. → That’s a fair answer. Taking what isn’t yours causes worry, even if you mean well. Luna might add: you can say something was wrong and still feel sorry for the one who did it.
> - No. She was sad, and she kept them safe. → That’s a fair answer. She never harmed a single egg. Nova might add: the nursery still worried all fortnight. Kind reasons don’t stop other people being upset.
> - A bit of both. → That’s a fair answer. Lots of things are like that: a wrong thing, done for a sad, loving reason. Understanding why helps you choose what to do next.
> _For grown-ups: Think question (no right answer): considered whether an action can be wrong even when the reason is love and grief._

_You leave it. On the way down, Luna stops on the path._  
**Luna**: Next spring she’ll come back to the nursery. Grief keeps a better calendar than I do. What do we do?  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): metaphors: saying one thing is another  
> _Luna says grief “keeps a better calendar” than she does. What does she mean?_  
> - ✅ Sad feelings remember the time of year a sad thing happened, and come back then. → Yes. Nightjar’s chicks died in a cold spring. So every spring, the sadness comes round again, right on time. Grief can’t really keep a calendar. Talking about a feeling as if it were a person with a diary is a metaphor.
> - Owls can’t read calendars. → True, and funny! But Luna means something bigger. Grief remembers the time of year a sad thing happened, and comes back then. Saying it “keeps a calendar” is a metaphor.
> - Grief is over quickly. → It’s nearly the opposite. Grief remembers, sometimes better than we do. Her chicks died in spring, so spring brings the sadness back. That’s why she’ll come back next spring.
> _For grown-ups: Explained the meaning of a metaphor about grief._


**Choices**

- **Leave the nursery open, with a lantern lit for her.**
    - **Nova**: A night nurse. With a beak. That’s either brilliant or a disaster.
    - **Luna**: If she wants eggs to sit on, she can sit on ours, where we can see. Hazel would have done that.
    - **Luna**: Then worried all night. Then done it again.
- **Move the nursery in by the stove, with a latch.**
    - **Luna**: That’s fair. The eggs come first. Feed them before you feed yourself, nearly.
    - **Nova**: She won’t take it personally. She’ll just sit on the roof and look at the chimney. Sadly. For ever.
    - **Luna**: Then I’ll leave her a mouse on the step now and then. Grief gets hungry.

#### `reader:s17-feathers` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-feathers)

_Some weeks later there is a feather on your windowsill. Grey, soft as smoke, laid down carefully._  
_Next night, there is another._  
**Luna**: She’s calling you. The wild one must be hatching.  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, notice): how long a big owl’s egg takes to hatch  
> **Luna**: A big owl sits on her eggs for about four or five weeks before they hatch.  
> **Luna**: All that time she keeps them warm and turns them. She hardly leaves the nest at all.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a big owl incubates its eggs for about four or five weeks before they hatch._

**Luna**: Go after dark. Take nothing. Just go.  
**Nova**: I’m not coming. I’m not stopping.  
**Nova**: I’ll be on the cliff path by accident, about nine.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.MD.A.1, ask): converting weeks to days  
> _Say it takes 4 weeks. A week has 7 days. How many days are there in 4 weeks?_  
> - ✅ 28 → Yes. 4 × 7 = 28. That’s 28 nights of sitting still on a cold cliff. Owls are very patient.
> - 11 → That’s 4 + 7. But we need 4 lots of 7. 7, 14, 21, 28. Four weeks is 28 days.
> - 24 → Close. Let’s count in sevens: 7, 14, 21, 28. Four weeks is 28 days.
> _For grown-ups: Converted 4 weeks into 28 days by counting in sevens._


#### `reader:s17-hatching` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-hatching)

_The wind has dropped. The egg under her is rocking. Nightjar shifts off it, just enough, and you both watch._  
_A crack. A beak like a fingernail. Then a damp grey thing the size of a dumpling, eyes shut tight, very offended._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS1-1, do): an owl’s life cycle, and how a chick hatches  
> **Luna**: See the tiny hard point on its beak? That’s an egg tooth. Chicks use it to chip their way out. It drops off soon after.  
> **Luna**: Owlets hatch with their eyes shut and a coat of soft, fluffy feathers called down.  
> _Put the owl’s life in order: egg, hatchling, fledgling (learning to fly), adult._  
> Check: `edu:order:owl-life-cycle`
> _For grown-ups: Learned how a chick hatches using its egg tooth, and put an owl’s life cycle in order._

**Nightjar**: Hoo-oo. Hoo-oo.  
_The same two notes. They don’t sound the same at all._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.2.3, ask): how a character’s feelings change the meaning  
> _Nightjar calls the same two notes she always calls. Why don’t they sound the same?_  
> - ✅ Because now she’s happy, not grieving. → Yes. The notes are the same. The feeling behind them has changed, and you can hear it.
> - Because she has a cold. → Ha! No. Look at her mood: happy. Same notes, new feeling. For the first time in twenty-two years, her call isn’t sad.
> - Because it’s a different owl. → It’s Nightjar, all right. The torn feather’s still there. Same owl, same notes, new feeling. Now she’s happy.
> _For grown-ups: Explained how a character’s change of feeling changes how the same words or sounds come across._

_Later the owlet tumbles across the rock and into your lap. Nightjar watches. She does not call it back._  
**Luna**: She’s sharing. Owls don’t share. Look at that.  

#### `reader:s17-complete` · [main](../../../side/S17.md#s17-complete)

_At the end of autumn Nightjar moults, and the wind brings her old grey feathers down into the Grove._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): why birds moult  
> _Feathers wear out, like old socks. So birds moult: old feathers drop out, and new ones grow in their place._  
> _Most birds don’t lose them all at once. They swap a few at a time, so they can still fly and keep warm._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that birds moult, replacing worn feathers a few at a time._

**Luna**: I’ve sewn them into a cloak. Don’t ask how long it took. I’ll tell you, and then you’ll feel bad.  
_It is as light as breath and as warm as a bird._  
_That night, on the cliff top in the cloak, you hear a hush of wings behind you. Two careful feet take hold of your collar._  
_The valley turns under you, silver and black. The Grove comes up to meet you, as gently as an egg set down in straw._  
**Nova**: Show-off. I walked for years to see this valley properly, and you did it in a minute.  
**Nova**: …Take me next time.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, notice): asking for what you want  
> _Nova never asks for things. “Take me next time” was hard for her to say. That’s why it came out so quietly._  
> _Asking for what you’d like can feel brave. Good friends are glad you asked._  
> _For grown-ups: Noticed that asking for what you want can take courage, and that friends welcome it._


### Glossary

- **fortnight**: Two weeks, or fourteen days.
- **trample**: To tread heavily on something and squash it.
- **nocturnal**: Awake and active at night, and asleep in the day.
- **incubate**: To keep eggs warm so the babies inside can grow.
- **grief**: The deep sadness you feel when someone you love has died.
- **metaphor**: Words that describe something as if it were something else, to make you see it in a new way.
- **owlet**: A baby owl.
- **moult**: When a bird’s old feathers drop out and new ones grow in their place.
