# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S18: The Grove Museum

## S18. The Grove Museum

- `s18-step1`: Dig three fossils from the cliff layers with Dusty, and sort them from oldest to youngest. · _Lower layers were laid down first, so they are older. Note how deep each find was, then sort them._
- `s18-step2`: Run the moth trap in Luna’s garden and wait quietly for three night finds. · _Sit still near the sheet: every time you move, the finds come more slowly. At dawn, look under the ash tree._
- `s18-step3`: Find three shore creatures in Marina’s rock pools before the tide turns. · _Look under the weed and the flat stones, and put each stone back the way you found it. When the water reaches the barnacles, the tide is turning._
- `s18-step4`: On a clear night on the hill, find the Pole Star with Nova, then wait up for the Moon. · _Find the seven stars of the Plough. The two at the end of the pan point to the Pole Star. The Moon rises late, in the east._
- `s18-step5`: Fill the centre case, then talk to Professor Delve at the barn door. · _Place the centre-case exhibit in the middle of the barn, then speak to the professor by the door._

#### `reader:s18-barn` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-barn)

_Hazel’s old barn smells of hay, apples and long-ago rain. Swallows’ nests line the rafters like little clay cups._  
_In the middle of the floor stands a mole in a tweed waistcoat, beside a wheelbarrow full of empty glass cases._  
**Professor Delve**: Ah. The grandchild. Professor Delve, archivist. I wrote to your grandmother about a museum. She said yes.  
**Professor Delve**: “In principle,” Hazel said. That means “yes, one day”. That was eleven years ago.  
**Professor Delve**: Moles are patient. I have come to collect my “one day”.  
**Professor Delve**: Four wings. Three exhibits each. Fossils, night creatures, shore life, the heavens. Twelve. A tidy number.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.1, ask): multiplication as equal groups  
> **Professor Delve**: Check my sums, please. Four wings, three exhibits in each. How many exhibits altogether?  
> - ✅ 12 → Correct. 4 groups of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. Or 4 × 3 = 12. A tidy number, as I said.
> - 7 → That is 4 + 3. But we have four groups, with three in each. 3, 6, 9, 12. Four lots of three is 12.
> - 16 → That would be four in each wing. We have three. 3, 6, 9, 12. Four lots of three is 12.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out 4 groups of 3 as 4 × 3 = 12._

**Professor Delve**: Every exhibit gets a plaque, and every plaque must be true. Not nearly true. Not true-ish. True.  
_A small brown moth drifts down from the rafters and lands on the end of his nose._  
**Professor Delve**: Oh. Oh dear. Oh, no, not one of—  
_Professor Delve faints, quite neatly, into the wheelbarrow._  
**Professor Delve**: I’m perfectly all right. I’m simply horizontal.  
_Dusty stands in the doorway, muddy, holding a trowel with nothing to dig. Dusty’s Nubbin, Fossil, died two days ago._  
**Dusty**: I heard “fossils”. From the lane. My hands want something to do. Can they do this?  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Self-management, notice): how keeping busy can help with grief  
> _Losing Fossil is a big loss, and Dusty is still very sad. It’s sad, and it’s all right to be sad._  
> _Sometimes, when you’re grieving, it helps to give your hands a job, especially alongside a friend._  
> _It doesn’t make the sadness go away. It gives it somewhere to sit for a while._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a gentle, shared task can help someone who is grieving._


#### `reader:s18-fossils` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-fossils)

_The cliff is striped like a cake: sand, then red rock, then grey shale, then a dark band just above the beach._  
**Dusty**: Deeper’s older. Rock goes down in layers, like blankets on a bed. Nobody puts the bottom blanket on last.  
_A coiled shell near the top. A fern pressed flat in the red rock. And deep in the dark band, a little armoured bug._  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-ESS1-1, do): rock layers: deeper layers are older  
> **Dusty**: Put the three finds in order, oldest first. Remember the blankets: the deepest layer went down first.  
> _Oldest: the armoured bug from the dark band. Then the fern from the red rock. Youngest: the coiled shell near the top._  
> Check: `edu:order:fossils:oldest-first`
> _For grown-ups: Put fossils in order of age using the rule that deeper rock layers are older._

**Dusty**: Fossil used to sit right there while I dug. Supervising. She was terrible at it. She slept.  
**Dusty**: …It’s quiet without the snoring.  
_Back at the barn, Professor Delve reads each plaque aloud before he’ll let it go on the wall._  
**Professor Delve**: “Trilobites were sea animals with jointed shells, relatives of insects and crabs. They lived for over 250 million years.”  
**Professor Delve**: Relatives of insects. Distant ones. Very distant. I have seen the family tree, and I do not plan to visit.  
**Professor Delve**: “Ferns have no flowers and no seeds. They spread by spores, which usually form under their leaves.”  
**Professor Delve**: “Ammonites were sea animals related to squid and octopus. They died out about 66 million years ago.”  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-1, ask): what fossils tell us about the past  
> **Dusty**: Trilobites and ammonites lived in the sea. But we dug them out of a cliff, on dry land.  
> **Dusty**: What does that tell us?  
> - ✅ Long ago, this land was under the sea. → Yes. When they died, mud and sand on the sea floor buried them. Over millions of years the mud pressed into rock, and the shells turned into fossils. Later the land rose.
> - They crawled up the cliff. → Good thought, but they were sea animals. They couldn’t live out of water. They’re in the cliff because this land was once sea floor. Mud buried them, and over millions of years it became rock.
> - Someone put them there. → Nobody could. They’re inside solid rock, deep in the layers. This land was once sea floor. Mud buried them, and over millions of years it became rock.
> _For grown-ups: Used sea fossils found on land to work out that the area was once under the sea._

**Professor Delve**: True, true and true. Wing one. Somebody check my nose for moths.  

#### `reader:s18-night` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-night)

_Luna hangs a white sheet between two apple trees and sets a lantern under it. The sheet glows like a sail at sea._  
**Luna**: A moth trap. Nothing gets hurt. They land on the sheet, we look, we draw, and at dawn they go home.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): moths and butterflies: how to tell them apart  
> **Luna**: Most moths fly at night, and lots of them are drawn to lights. That’s why the lantern works.  
> **Luna**: To tell a moth from a butterfly, look at the antennae, the feelers on its head.  
> **Luna**: A butterfly’s end in little knobs. Most moths’ are thin, or feathery like tiny ferns.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that most moths fly at night and can be told from butterflies by their antennae._

**Luna**: The museum gets the drawings. Living things aren’t exhibits. They’re neighbours.  
**Professor Delve**: I shall supervise from a safe distance. Of about a mile.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): exaggeration for a joke  
> _Luna’s garden is not a mile long. So why does Professor Delve say “about a mile”?_  
> - ✅ He’s exaggerating, to show how scared of moths he is. → Yes. To exaggerate is to make something sound much bigger than it is. Here it’s a joke, and it tells us how he feels.
> - He measured it. → A mile is a long walk: about 20 minutes. He’d be in the next valley! He’s exaggerating: making it sound bigger than it is, to show how scared he is.
> - He can’t count. → He counts very well. He’s a professor! He’s exaggerating: making it sound bigger than it is, to show how scared he is.
> _For grown-ups: Recognised exaggeration used for humour and to show a character’s feelings._

_He sits under a blanket at the far end of the garden, holding a tin of smelling salts like a cup of tea._  
**Luna**: Now the hard part. We wait, and we don’t talk.  
**Luna**: …That was me talking. Starting now.  

#### `reader:s18-night-finds` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-night-finds)

_Near midnight a moth lands on the sheet, green and black like lichen on old bark. You lose it twice while looking at it._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-LS4-3, ask): camouflage helps animals survive  
> _You lost the moth twice, and it was right in front of you. Why is it so hard to see on bark?_  
> - ✅ Its colours match the lichen, so it blends in. → Yes. That’s called camouflage. Birds hunting by day fly right past it. Moths that blend in best are the ones most likely to survive.
> - It’s invisible. → It just seems that way! It’s there, but its green and black match the lichen. Blending in like that is called camouflage. It keeps the moth safe from hungry birds.
> - It’s too small to see. → It’s small, but you could see it easily on the white sheet. On bark, its colours match the lichen. That’s camouflage, and it hides the moth from birds.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that camouflage helps animals hide from predators and survive._

**Luna**: Merveille du Jour. It means “marvel of the day”, and it flies by night. Somebody had a sense of humour.  
_From the far end of the garden comes a soft thump. Professor Delve has fainted from a mile away, just to be safe._  
_By dawn you have a drawing of the moth, a spider’s web lifted whole onto black card, and an owl pellet from under the ash._  
**Professor Delve**: “The Merveille du Jour is a moth that flies in autumn. Its green and black wings hide it on lichen-covered bark.”  
**Professor Delve**: “Owls swallow small prey whole. The fur and bones they can’t digest are pressed into a pellet and coughed up.”  
**Professor Delve**: “Garden spiders spin webs from silk made in their own bodies. They often eat the old web before spinning a new one.”  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, do): spiders are not insects  
> _Count the legs in your drawings. Tap each one. The moth has 6 legs. The spider who made the web has 8._  
> _Insects have 6 legs and 3 body parts. Spiders have 8 legs and 2 body parts. So a spider isn’t an insect at all._  
> Check: `edu:sort:legs:insect-spider`
> _For grown-ups: Counted legs to sort an insect (6) from a spider (8), and learned spiders are not insects._

**Professor Delve**: Recycling. Admirable. Somebody turn the moth drawing to face the wall whenever I walk past.  

#### `reader:s18-shore` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-shore)

_Low tide past the ridge. Marina’s rock pools are full of sky, and the seaweed smells of salt and pennies._  
**Marina**: Rules. Turn a stone, turn it back the same way up. That’s somebody’s roof.  

> **Lesson** (science, K-ESS3-3, do): looking after living things in their habitat  
> _Lift a flat stone gently. Underneath: tiny shrimps, a curled worm, a periwinkle. It’s damp and dark down there._  
> _Now turn it back the same way up. The creatures underneath need the shade and wet to stay alive._  
> Check: `edu:turn:stone:same-way-up`
> _For grown-ups: Turned a rock-pool stone back carefully, learning to protect the creatures that live under it._

**Marina**: Living things go back before the tide turns. The museum gets drawings, and whatever they’ve finished with.  
**Marina**: And the tide comes back whether you’re ready or not. It’s very rude like that.  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.MD.A.1, ask): time intervals: working out when the tide comes in  
> **Marina**: On most coasts the sea comes in and goes out about twice a day.  
> **Marina**: From low tide to high tide takes about 6 hours. Low tide today is 11 o’clock in the morning. When is high tide?  
> - ✅ About 5 o’clock in the afternoon. → Spot on. 11 o’clock, plus 1 hour is 12, then 5 more hours is 5 o’clock. Plenty of time, if we don’t dawdle.
> - About 6 o’clock. → Count on from 11: 12, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. That’s 6 hours, and we land on 5 o’clock.
> - About 4 o’clock. → Nearly! Count on from 11: 12, 1, 2, 3, 4 is only 5 hours. One more hour, and we land on 5 o’clock.
> _For grown-ups: Added 6 hours to 11 o’clock to find a later time._

**Professor Delve**: I have brought a bucket, a net and a deep respect for things with pincers.  
_A small crab sidles past the professor’s boot. He doesn’t faint. He looks extremely pleased about it._  
**Professor Delve**: Not a moth. A good day.  

#### `reader:s18-shore-finds` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-shore-finds)

_A sea star the colour of a ripe apricot, one arm short and growing back. A limpet, clamped in its own worn hollow._  

> **Lesson** (science, ask): a sea star is not a fish  
> **Marina**: Some people call it a starfish. Is a sea star really a fish?  
> - ✅ No. It has no backbone, no fins and no gills like a fish. → Right. That’s why “sea star” is the better name. It moves on hundreds of tiny tube feet under its arms. Fish don’t have those.
> - Yes. It lives in the sea. → Lots of things live in the sea that aren’t fish: crabs, limpets, seaweed. A sea star has no backbone and no fins. That’s why “sea star” is the better name.
> - Yes. It’s got “fish” in its name. → Names can trick you! A sea star has no backbone and no fins, so it isn’t a fish. That’s why “sea star” is the better name.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that a sea star is not a fish, because it has no backbone or fins._

_And a hermit crab halfway between houses, hurrying out of a cramped winkle shell into a roomy whelk._  
**Professor Delve**: Drafts for the plaques. “A sea star can regrow an arm it has lost. Many have a simple eye at the tip of each arm.”  
**Professor Delve**: “A limpet grazes on the rock when the tide is in. When the tide goes out, it returns to its own spot, its home scar.”  

> **Lesson** (science, 4-LS1-1, notice): how a limpet survives low tide  
> **Marina**: Try to lift a limpet once it’s clamped down. You can’t. Don’t try too hard, mind.  
> **Marina**: Its strong foot grips the rock and holds a little seawater in. That stops it drying out till the tide comes back.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned how a limpet’s shell and strong foot help it survive when the tide goes out._

**Professor Delve**: “A hermit crab keeps its soft body inside an empty snail shell. When it outgrows one, it moves to a bigger one.”  
**Marina**: Look. It’s left the old shell behind. Perfect, that. Somebody else will want it. A smaller crab.  
**Professor Delve**: Or a museum. A museum would want it very much. I am only saying.  

**Choices**

- **Take the empty shell for the museum.**
    - **Marina**: Fair. There are plenty of shells. There’s only one museum.
    - **Professor Delve**: It shall have a velvet cushion. A small one. The crab would want it small.
- **Leave the shell for the next crab.**
    - **Marina**: Good. That’s how pools work. Somebody’s old house is somebody’s new one.
    - **Professor Delve**: Then we shall have a drawing. Drawings never need a bigger house.

#### `reader:s18-stars` · [main](../../../side/S18.md#s18-stars)

_Nova is on the western hill at nightfall, flat on her back in the cold grass beside the memorial flowers._  
**Nova**: Clear night. No moon till late. Best sky in the valley. I’ve slept under most of them, so I’d know.  
**Nova**: I brought you something for the museum. I don’t give people things. It’s a rock. Please don’t say anything nice. I’ll go red.  
_She drops a stone the size of a walnut into your palm: black, heavy for its size, with a crust like burnt toast._  
_A Nubbin sniffs the rock that looks like burnt toast. It is still not toast._  
**Nova**: I watched it come down over the moor, years back. Walked two days to find it. Carried it ever since.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.3.3, ask): working out a character’s hidden feelings  
> _Nova has carried that rock for years. Then she says, “Please don’t say anything nice.” Why?_  
> - ✅ It matters a lot to her, and she feels shy showing it. → Yes. Some people go gruff when they care most. Carrying it for years gives her away.
> - She doesn’t care about the rock. → Nobody carries a rock for years and walks two days for it unless they care. She feels shy about showing how much it matters, so she keeps it short.
> - She’s in a hurry. → She’s lying in the grass all evening. Not much of a hurry! She cares a lot, and feels shy showing it, so she keeps it short.
> _For grown-ups: Used a character’s actions to understand feelings she doesn’t say out loud._

**Professor Delve**: A meteorite! It fell out of the sky and hasn’t a single wing on it. I could kiss it.  

> **Lesson** (science, notice): what a meteorite is  
> **Professor Delve**: A meteorite is a rock from space that falls all the way to the ground.  
> **Professor Delve**: That black crust formed as its outside melted, rushing down through Earth’s air.  
> **Professor Delve**: Many meteorites have iron metal inside. That’s why it feels so heavy for its size.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what a meteorite is, why it has a burnt crust, and why many feel heavy._

**Nova**: Please don’t. It’s had a long walk.  
**Nova**: Now. Find me north. Then you can have the other two exhibits as well.

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): multiplying to find a total cost  
> **Professor Delve**: Suppose 9 visitors come on a Saturday, and each pays 2 Shells. How many Shells is that?

> - 11 → That is 9 + 2. But each of the nine pays two. 9 lots of 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18. 18 Shells.
> - 16 → Close. That would be 8 visitors. Let us count all nine in twos. 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18. 18 Shells.

### Glossary

- **archivist**: Someone whose job is to look after old records and important things from the past.
- **horizontal**: Lying flat, side to side, like the horizon.
- **shale**: A soft grey rock made from mud pressed hard over a very long time. It splits into thin layers.
- **antennae**: The two long feelers on an insect’s head, used to touch and smell.
- **exaggerate**: To make something sound bigger or more than it really is.
- **camouflage**: Colours or patterns that help an animal blend in with what is around it.
- **sidles**: Moves sideways, slowly or shyly.
- **tide**: The rise and fall of the sea, which happens about twice a day.
- **backbone**: The row of bones down the middle of the back of animals such as fish, birds and people.
- **meteorite**: A rock from space that has fallen all the way to the ground.
- **on loan**: Lent for a while, and given back later.
- **migrate**: To travel a long way to a different place at a certain time of year, and back again.
- **upkeep**: The work and money needed to keep something in good condition.
- **curator**: A person who looks after the things in a museum and explains them to visitors.
