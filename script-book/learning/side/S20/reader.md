# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Side story S20: Fort Night

## S20. Fort Night

- `s20-step1`: Round one: gather snow and sticks, and build your wall before the other fort does. · _Put lots of children on gathering early. Wall first, catapult second: a catapult with no wall is just a target with a spoon._
- `s20-step2`: Round two: send Zip to scout the other fort, and get him back without being seen. · _Snowdrifts block their lookout’s view. Dash between them when the lookout turns away. Zip’s ears go flat if he’s spotted._
- `s20-step3`: Round three: send the units that beat theirs, and take their flag. · _Splashy beats Sturdy: wet slush soaks a big snowball and it slumps. Watch what they switch to, and switch too._
- `s20-step4`: Build one shelter out of both forts, and get every child inside. · _Speedy children fetch, Sturdy children roll, Splashy children pack the gaps. Count heads: there should be 20. Wren is small and easy to miss._

#### `reader:s20-declaration` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-declaration)

_The snow after the Long Night is the deep, squeaky kind. Every child in the valley is out in it. None of them is cold, they say._  
_A sheet of cereal-box card is tied to your gate. The capitals are very angry and the spelling is very brave._  
_“DECLARASHUN OF WAR. FORT NIGHT. THE COMMON. BRING MITTENS. — THE OTHER FORT”_  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): spelling the “-tion” ending  
> _DECLARASHUN is very brave spelling. How is the word really spelt?_  
> - ✅ DECLARATION → Yes! The ending “-tion” says “shun”. You can hear it in station, potion and, sadly, detention.
> - DECLARASHON → It does sound like that! But the “shun” sound at the end of a word is very often spelt “-tion”: declaration.
> - DECKLERATION → Good try at the start. It’s “dec”, not “deck”: declaration. And the end is “-tion”, which says “shun”.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that the “shun” sound at the end of many words is spelt “-tion” (declaration, station)._

_Sparks’s cousin Tilly is back for the winter holidays, and has found out about snow._  
**Tilly**: You’re commanding one fort. I’m commanding the other. One of us is going to win, and it’s me.  
_On the Common, two forts are marked out in sticks: one by the hedge, one by the frozen ditch._  
_Neither has a wall. Both have flags._  
**Pixel**: Morning. I’m the referee. I’ve written the rules. Forty-one pages. Nobody will read them.  
**Pixel**: Byte has. Twice. He’s my husband. He had notes. I ignored the notes.  
**Pixel**: I’ll enforce them anyway.  
**Pixel**: Three rounds. Each round, the referee teaches one idea. This is educational. Nobody tell the children.  
**Pixel**: Round one: economy. Snow makes walls. Sticks make the catapult. More gatherers early means more of everything later.  
**Pixel**: Units: Speedy beats Splashy. Splashy beats Sturdy. Sturdy beats Speedy. It goes round and round, like the seasons. Or a grudge.  

> **Lesson** (maths, ask): following a rule in a cycle, like rock-paper-scissors  
> **Pixel**: Speedy beats Splashy. Splashy beats Sturdy. Sturdy beats Speedy.  
> **Pixel**: Say the other fort sends Sturdy rollers at you. What do you send back?  
> - Speedy → Ruling: squashed. Sturdy beats Speedy. Look for the unit that beats Sturdy: Splashy.
> - ✅ Splashy → Correct. Splashy beats Sturdy. It’s a cycle, like rock, paper, scissors. Every unit beats one and loses to one, so nobody can win every time.
> - Sturdy → Sturdy against Sturdy. Two big snowballs, very slowly. We’ll be here till spring. Splashy is the one that beats Sturdy.
> _For grown-ups: Used a cycle rule (each unit beats one and loses to one, like rock-paper-scissors) to choose the right counter._

_Behind Pixel, a small boy in a bobble hat is hit in the ear by a snowball. Nobody has started yet._  
**Gordon**: I’m all right!  
**Pixel**: That’s Gordon. Gordon gets hit. It isn’t a rule. It’s more like weather.  
**Wren**: Commander! I’m Wren! What’s our battle cry, commander?  
**Wren**: Also, what’s a grudge? Pixel said it.  
**Gordon**: It’s when a snowball hits you and you remember it for ever. I remember all of them.  

**Choices**

- **“FOR THE FORT!”**
    - **Wren**: FOR THE FORT!
    - **Pixel**: Classic. Solid. Ruling: approved.
- **“FOR WREN’S NAN’S GOOD MITTENS!”**
    - **Wren**: FOR MY NAN’S GOOD MITTENS!
    - **Wren**: She knitted them. They’ve got thumbs and everything.
- **“REMEMBER GORDON!”**
    - **Gordon**: I’m right here.
    - Your whole fort roars it anyway. Gordon looks touched. Then he is hit by a snowball.

#### `reader:s20-round1` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-round1)

**Wren**: Yes, commander! On it, commander!  
_Every time you point at Wren, she salutes and says something. You point at her rather a lot._  
**Wren**: Yes, commander. Yes, commander. Commander, that’s nine times. I’m only little.  
**Pixel**: Notice: your sticks are low. Sticks are scarce now. Notice: you can’t recruit more children. You’ve run out of mittens.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Eco.1.K-2, ask): scarcity: choosing when there isn’t enough  
> **Pixel**: Your sticks are scarce. You can’t get any more. What’s the smart thing to do?  
> - ✅ Save them for the most important job, and build the rest from snow. → Correct. When there isn’t enough of something, you have to choose what it’s for. That’s called scarcity. Everybody deals with it. Shops, towns, and forts.
> - Use them all on the first thing you see. → Then there are none left for the thing that matters. When something is scarce, choose carefully what it’s for. Save sticks for the most important job.
> - Cry into your mittens. → You’ve run out of mittens. A better plan: when something is scarce, save it for the most important job and use snow for the rest.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that scarcity (not having enough) means choosing what to use something for._

**Pixel**: Ruling: a snowball that hits a snowman is friendly fire. The snowman has feelings. He’s called Derek.  
_At the whistle, your wall is knee-high, fat and lumpy. Theirs is shoulder-high, thin, and has battlements._  

> **Lesson** (science, 3-5-ETS1-2, ask): comparing two designs: why a wide base is steadier  
> _Compare the walls. Yours: low, fat and lumpy. Theirs: tall and thin. Which one is harder to knock over?_  
> - ✅ Mine: low and wide. → Yes. A wide base and a low height make things steady. That’s why pyramids are wide at the bottom, and why a tall, thin wall wobbles.
> - Theirs: tall and thin. → It looks grander! But tall and thin is wobbly: one good push and it tips. A low, wide wall is steadier. Remember that.
> - They’re exactly the same. → Same snow, different shapes! The low, wide one is steadier. Shape matters as much as stuff.
> _For grown-ups: Compared two wall designs and learned that a wide base and low height make a structure steadier._

**Tilly**: Economy! I put everyone on snow from the very start. I learned it off the Tax Toad.  
**Pixel**: Round one: a draw. Theirs is taller. Yours is fatter. Ruling: both of them are walls.  

#### `reader:s20-scout` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-scout)

**Pixel**: Round two: scouting. You can’t beat what you can’t see.  
_Sparks lends you a Swift Nubbin called Zip: thin as a whisker, faster than gossip, and unable to sit still for any reason._  
_Zip vibrates in your mittens like a sneeze that hasn’t happened yet._  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.4.5, ask): spotting a simile  
> _A simile compares two things using “like” or “as”. Which of these is a simile?_  
> - ✅ “like a sneeze that hasn’t happened yet” → Yes! Zip is compared to a sneeze that’s about to happen: fizzing and ready to go. “Thin as a whisker” is another one.
> - “Round two: scouting.” → That’s just Pixel being a referee. Look for “like” or “as”: Zip vibrates “like a sneeze that hasn’t happened yet”.
> - “That’s fair.” → That’s Gordon being very reasonable. A simile uses “like” or “as”: “like a sneeze that hasn’t happened yet”.
> _For grown-ups: Identified a simile (a comparison using “like” or “as”) in the story._

**Pixel**: Send the scout round their fort. Count what they’ve got. Don’t let him get caught.  
**Pixel**: Being caught means a snowball to the face. For Zip, that’s most of his body.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Geo.2.K-2, do): drawing a simple map seen from above  
> **Pixel**: Scouts make maps. Maps are drawn as if you were a bird looking straight down.  
> **Pixel**: Draw the Common: your fort by the hedge, theirs by the frozen ditch, and three snowdrifts for Zip to hide behind.  
> Check: `edu:draw:map:the-common`
> _For grown-ups: Drew a bird’s-eye map of a place, marking landmarks and hiding spots._

**Gordon**: Can I scout?  
**Pixel**: No, Gordon. You’d get hit.  
**Gordon**: That’s fair.  

#### `reader:s20-intel` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-intel)

_Zip comes back with snow on his eyebrows and something urgent to say, which he says by running round you nine times._  
_You read it in his tracks. Their wall is magnificent. Behind it, there is almost nobody._  
_Three of their children have gone home: one lost a mitten, one lost a boot, and one just wanted his tea._  
_Zip’s tracks say ten were here this morning. Of the seven left, four are busy patting the wall._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.D.8, ask): a two-step take-away problem  
> _Zip’s tracks say: their fort started with 10 children. 3 went home. 4 more are busy patting the wall._  
> _How many are left to defend it?_  
> - 7 → That’s after the 3 went home: 10 − 3 = 7. But 4 more are busy with the wall. 7 − 4 = 3.
> - ✅ 3 → Yes! Two steps: 10 − 3 = 7, then 7 − 4 = 3. Three defenders. “Almost nobody” is right.
> - 6 → Let’s do it in two steps. 10 − 3 = 7 still there… then 7 − 4 = 3 left. Three defenders.
> _For grown-ups: Solved a two-step subtraction problem (10 − 3 − 4 = 3)._

**Tilly**: We are FINE. We have LOADS of people. Some of them are in the wall. As bricks.  
**Pixel**: Ruling: children are not bricks.  
**Pixel**: …Ruling: nearly.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.K-2, ask): fair play: using a chance or waiting (think question)  
> _Their fort is thin right now. Is it fair to attack while three of them are missing?_  
> - Yes. In a game, you use your chances. → That’s a fair answer. Lots of games reward spotting a chance. The other commander would probably do the same, only louder.
> - No. A fair fight means both sides are ready. → That’s a fair answer too. Footballers often kick the ball out when someone on the other team is hurt. It isn’t a rule. It’s fair play.
> - It depends on what everyone agreed. → That’s a thoughtful answer. Fairness often comes down to the rules everyone agreed before the game. Pixel’s forty-one pages don’t say.
> _For grown-ups: Weighed up whether it is fair to take advantage of the other side’s bad luck in a game._


**Choices**

- **Attack now, while they’re thin. It’s a war. They’d do the same.**
    - **Wren**: CHARGE! Yes, commander! Charging, commander!
    - Your fort pours across the Common. Their wall is splendid, and almost nobody is behind it.
    - **Tilly**: That’s not FAIR!
    - **Tilly**: …That’s exactly what I’d have done. I hate it.
    - **Pixel**: Round two to your fort. Scouting works. It’s horrible. That’s the lesson.
- **Wait till their three come back. Beating half a fort isn’t winning.**
    - You send Zip back with a snowball that has a smiley face pressed into it. It means “we’ll wait”.
    - Ten minutes later, one mitten, one boot and one boy full of tea come trudging back up the lane.
    - **Tilly**: You waited for us? That’s either very nice or very sneaky.
    - **Pixel**: Round two: a draw. Ruling: honour is worth one point. I’ve just added it. Page forty-two.

#### `reader:s20-counters` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-counters)

**Pixel**: Round three. The last one. Counters.  
**Pixel**: Look at what they’re sending. Send what beats it. That’s all strategy is. Everything else is shouting.  
_Over the ditch, the other fort is massing Sturdy: big children rolling big snowballs, slow as weather._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 4.OA.C.5, do): applying a cycle rule to a sequence  
> **Pixel**: They’ll send three waves: Sturdy, then Speedy, then Splashy. Match each wave with the unit that beats it.  
> **Pixel**: Remember the cycle. Speedy beats Splashy. Splashy beats Sturdy. Sturdy beats Speedy.  
> Check: `edu:match:counters:3-waves`
> _For grown-ups: Applied a rule to a sequence: matched each wave with its counter (Splashy, then Sturdy, then Speedy)._

**Tilly**: SURRENDER OR BE ROLLED!  
_Your fort answers with its battle cries. “I AM NOT COLD, I AM BRAVE!” “FOR DEREK!” “MUM SAID BE BACK BY FOUR!”_  
_Above the hill the sky has gone the colour of a pigeon. Nobody is looking at it except Pixel._  
**Pixel**: Hm.  
**Gordon**: Is that sky going to hit me?  
**Pixel**: This time, Gordon, it’s going to hit everybody.  

> **Lesson** (science, K-ESS2-1, notice): reading the sky for a change in the weather  
> **Pixel**: Thick, dark grey clouds rolling in. The wind picking up. When the sky changes like that, the weather often changes too.  
> **Pixel**: Weather watchers look up. Commanders, it turns out, look at flags.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that thickening dark clouds and rising wind are signs the weather may change._


#### `reader:s20-blizzard` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-blizzard)

_You are one dash from their flag when the snow stops falling down and starts falling sideways._  
_Then it is everywhere. You can’t see their fort. You can’t see your own. You can only hear Gordon, being hit by weather._  
**Pixel**: STOP. Whistle. Full stop. That’s a real one. That’s a real blizzard.  

> **Lesson** (science, K-ESS2-1, notice): what a blizzard is  
> _A blizzard is a snowstorm with very strong wind that goes on for a long time._  
> _The wind blows the snow sideways, so you can hardly see. When everything turns white, people call it a whiteout._  
> _For grown-ups: Learned what a blizzard is: strong wind, blowing snow, and very poor visibility._

_Pixel’s whistle is lost in the wind. Somebody small is crying, and you can’t tell which fort they belong to._  
**Tilly**: Our wall’s fallen in! It all fell in! I can’t find everyone!  
_Your own wall is still standing: knee-high, fat and lumpy. It is nowhere near big enough for everybody._  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Responsible decision-making, ask): putting safety before winning  
> _Everyone is scared and the game has stopped. What matters most now?_  
> - ✅ Getting every child, from both forts, somewhere safe. → Yes. When something real goes wrong, the game stops and people come first. All of them, whichever fort they’re from.
> - Grabbing the flag. We were one dash away! → It’s tempting! But a game can wait. People can’t. The most important thing is getting every child safe.
> - Finding just your own fort. → Your fort matters. But so does the small crying person, whoever they belong to. In a real emergency, everyone counts.
> _For grown-ups: Decided that keeping everyone safe matters more than winning a game._


#### `reader:s20-merge` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-merge)

_Through the white you can just make out the hedge, the ditch, and children from both forts, standing about like dropped gloves._  
**Pixel**: Referee’s ruling.  
**Pixel**: …I don’t have a ruling. Commander? You’ve got a wall. They’ve got the snow. Nobody’s got enough.  

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): combining what you have to solve a shared problem  
> _You have a wall but not enough snow. They have snow but no wall. What’s the best plan?_  
> - ✅ Put them together. One wall, everyone’s snow. → Yes. Neither side has enough alone. Together, they do. That’s what teamwork is for.
> - Keep your wall for your own fort. → Your wall is too small even for your fort, and the other children are cold. Put the wall and the snow together, and everyone fits.
> - Wait for the blizzard to stop. → Blizzards can last for hours. Better to build now: your wall plus their snow makes one shelter for everyone.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out that two groups can solve a problem together by combining what each one has._

**Tilly**: Is this a trick? Is this a strategy?  
**You**: Yes.  
_A strategy is just a plan for getting what you want. Right now, what you want is everybody warm._  

**Choices**

- **Kick open your wall and shout them all in.**
    - You kick a gap in your own wall. “IN! EVERYONE IN!”
    - Your fort has been taught all morning to do what you say. It does it, and starts pulling the others in by their scarves.
- **Carry your wall to them, snowball by snowball.**
    - Your gatherers know how to move snow fast. You taught them that this morning.
    - It turns out that was what it was for.
- **Meet in the middle. Both walls, one shelter.**
    - You meet in the ditch between the forts. Both sides bring snow. Both sides bring sticks.
    - The catapult becomes a roof beam. Derek the snowman becomes a door.

#### `reader:s20-count` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-count)

**Pixel**: Head count.  
**Pixel**: …Everyone. Even Gordon.  
_The small crier was Wren, who had lost a mitten. It was on her head the whole time._  
**Gordon**: I got hit by a whole sky.  
_Inside it is close and blue and smells of wet wool. Forty mittens steam. Zip is eating snow. It is not supper. Pixel rules against it, then allows it._  

> **Lesson** (maths, 3.OA.A.3, ask): division as sharing into pairs  
> _Forty mittens are steaming. Every child has two. How many children are in the shelter?_  
> - ✅ 20 → Yes! 40 ÷ 2 = 20. Twenty children, and not one mitten missing. Ruling: that is everyone.
> - 40 → That’s the mittens! Each child has two, so share the 40 into pairs. 40 ÷ 2 = 20 children.
> - 10 → That would be if each child had four mittens. Two each: 40 ÷ 2 = 20 children.
> _For grown-ups: Divided 40 mittens into pairs to find 20 children (40 ÷ 2 = 20)._

**Tilly**: That’s my wall, in our roof. That bit’s mine.  
_The roof is sticks from both forts, leaning together in pointed pairs, with snow packed on top._  

> **Lesson** (science, do): why triangles make strong frames  
> **Pixel**: Look up. Two sticks leaning together, with the ground underneath, make a triangle.  
> **Pixel**: Push on a triangle and it keeps its shape. Push on a square and it squashes into a diamond.  
> **Pixel**: That’s why roofs and bridges are full of triangles.  
> **Pixel**: Tap every triangle you can find in the roof.  
> Check: `edu:tap:shape:triangle:roof`
> _For grown-ups: Found triangles in a roof frame and learned that triangles keep their shape under pressure, which makes frames strong._

**Wren**: Commander? Which fort won?  
_The blizzard blows all afternoon. Pixel teaches the shelter a song with forty-one verses, one for each page of the rules._  
_At last the wind drops, as suddenly as it came. Somebody pokes a hole in the roof. It is night._  

#### `reader:s20-complete` · [main](../../../side/S20.md#s20-complete)

_The sky is clear and black and enormous. Every star the valley owns is out, and a few it has borrowed._  
_Nobody goes home. Both forts lie on their backs in one fort and look up._  
**Wren**: That one’s a mitten. That one’s Gordon. That one’s Derek.  

> **Lesson** (science, 1-ESS1-1, ask): why the stars show on a clear night, and constellations  
> _Why can you see so many stars tonight?_  
> - ✅ The clouds have gone, and it’s properly dark. → Yes. The blizzard’s clouds have blown away, so nothing hides the stars. Long ago, people joined stars into pictures too, called constellations. Wren has just invented the Mitten.
> - The blizzard blew new stars in. → Ha! The stars were there all along. The blizzard’s clouds were hiding them. Now the clouds have gone, you can see them all.
> - Stars only come out in winter. → Stars are there all year, even in the daytime. The Sun is just so bright that it hides them. Tonight it’s dark and clear.
> _For grown-ups: Learned that stars are always in the sky but are hidden by clouds and by daylight; met the word constellation._

**Pixel**: Final ruling. Both forts win. I’ve written it in pen. Pen is final.  
**Tilly**: So what was it for, then? The economy and the scouting and the counters?  
**You**: So we could build one fort fast, when it mattered.  
**Tilly**: …That’s annoying. That’s a really good answer.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, RL.4.2, ask): finding the theme of a story  
> _A theme is the big idea a story is about. What do you think this story’s theme is?_  
> - ✅ Skills and plans matter most when they help everybody. → Yes. Gathering, scouting and teamwork were practice for a war. In the end they built a shelter for both sides.
> - Always win, whatever happens. → Nobody won! Or everybody did. The big idea is that the skills mattered most when they helped everyone.
> - Snow is cold. → True, and Gordon would agree. But the big idea is bigger: plans and skills matter most when they help everybody.
> _For grown-ups: Identified the theme of a story: skills and plans matter most when used to help everyone._

_Up at the cottage a lamp is lit in the window, and somebody in a blanket is watching the Common._  
_Tilly puts two fingers in her mouth and whistles the supper call, rising at the end. From the window, faintly, it comes back._  
**Tilly**: Somebody up there still knows the supper call.  
**Pixel**: Prizes. Commander’s hat: it was a tea cosy. Snowball launcher: a prototype. Ruling: point it away from Gordon.  
**Pixel**: And the fort’s yours. I’ll build you one for the Grove that only comes out when it snows.  
_All morning you practised winning. In the end, the useful part was building something big enough for everybody._  

### Glossary

- **referee**: The person who makes sure everyone follows the rules of a game.
- **economy**: How people get, share and use the things they need.
- **scarce**: In short supply, so there isn’t enough for everything.
- **battlements**: The up-and-down gaps along the top of a castle wall.
- **scout**: Someone sent ahead to look around and bring back information.
- **magnificent**: Very grand and impressive.
- **strategy**: A plan for how to win or reach a goal.
- **blizzard**: A snowstorm with very strong wind that lasts a long time.
- **constellation**: A group of stars that people join up into a picture.
