# Learning Grove · Grove Readers (ages 7–10): Hana's Hiragana, Japanese (LJ1–LJ6)

## LJ1. Konnichiwa, Hana!

- `lj1-step1`: Say hello to Hana at the gate in Japanese. · _Tap Hana and choose Greet. Say “konnichiwa” (こんにちは). A little bow is a nice touch._
- `lj1-step2`: Teach three Nubbins to bow when you say “konnichiwa”. · _Tap a Nubbin and say こんにちは a few times. Nubbins learn by copying you._
- `lj1-step3`: Say “oyasumi” (good night) to everyone before bed. · _At dusk, tap each Nubbin, Nova and Hana, and say おやすみ._

#### `reader:lj1-s1` · [main](main.md#lj1-s1)

_Someone is racing up the lane. The gate sticks, like always, so she climbs it._  
_A girl in running shoes drops down on your side, sketchbook first. Nova follows, carrying both bags._  
**Nova**: This is Hana. She’s staying with me for the summer. She raced me from the bridge.  
**Hana**: I won by eleven seconds. Nova says she let me win. Nova is a terrible liar.  
**Hana**: こんにちは (konnichiwa, “hello”)!  

> **Lesson** (literacy, L.3.4, ask): a word with two meanings: こんにちは  
> _Hana said こんにちは (konnichiwa) as she came through the gate. What do you think it means?_  
> - ✅ Hello → はい! Hello. It can also mean “good afternoon”. One word, two jobs.
> - Goodbye → No, goodbye is a different word. I only just got here! こんにちは means “hello”, or “good afternoon”.
> - Thank you → Not that one. Thank you is tomorrow’s word. こんにちは means “hello”, or “good afternoon”.
> _For grown-ups: Worked out that こんにちは (konnichiwa) means “hello” and also “good afternoon”._

_She bows: a small, quick dip from the waist, then straight back up._  
**Hana**: That’s おじぎ (ojigi), bowing. At home we bow to say hello, thank you and sorry.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, notice): bowing (おじぎ) as a greeting in Japan  
> **Hana**: A small bow is friendly. A deeper bow shows more respect.  
> **Hana**: So you bow a little to a friend, and lower to say a big thank you, or a big sorry.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that in Japan people bow (おじぎ, ojigi) to greet, thank and apologise, and that a deeper bow shows more respect._

**Hana**: はじめまして (hajimemashite) means “nice to meet you”. You only say it the first time you meet someone.  
**Hana**: わたしは はな です (watashi wa Hana desu) means “I am Hana”. Your own name goes in the middle, like a sandwich.  
**Hana**: Then finish with よろしくおねがいします (yoroshiku onegaishimasu): “please treat me kindly”, or “pleased to meet you”.  
**Nova**: I practised that all the way here. On the ticket man, a cow and a postbox.  
**Hana**: Ready? You say hello. I say はい (hai, “yes”). Today はい is my answer to everything.  
**Nova**: Everything?  
**Hana**: はい.  

**Choices**

- **Bow and say “konnichiwa”.**
    - You bow. Hana bows back, a little deeper. She seems to think that means she won.
    - **Hana**: はい! That’s “yes, exactly”. You meant that bow.
- **Wave and say “konnichiwa”.**
    - **Hana**: A wave works too. Friends wave all the time. Next time, add a tiny bow. Like this. Easy.
- **Try the lot: “Hajimemashite. Watashi wa… desu. Yoroshiku onegaishimasu.”**
    - **Hana**: You did the whole sandwich! It took Nova three days to get that right.
    - **Nova**: Two days.
    - **Hana**: No. Three.

> **Lesson** (literacy, notice): friendly and polite forms of a greeting  
> **Hana**: Japanese has friendly words for family and friends, and polite words for grown-ups and people you don’t know well.  
> **Hana**: おはよう is friendly. おはようございます is polite. The polite one is longer. It usually is.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned the friendly おはよう (ohayō) and the polite おはようございます (ohayō gozaimasu), and when to use each._

**Fern**: Good morning to you too. I’ve been up since five, apologising to the trees.  
**Hana**: ありがとうございます (arigatō gozaimasu)! That’s a polite thank you. With friends, it’s just ありがとう (arigatō).  
**Hana**: Right. The plan. We teach them こんにちは first, because it works most of the day.  
**Hana**: Not now, strictly. Now is おはよう. But one word at a time. They’re Nubbins.

**Hana**: ごめんなさい (gomen nasai, “I’m sorry”)! Sorry, sorry!

**Fern**: That one’s Dumpling. He eats things. Bows aren’t food, Dumpling.  
**Hana**: But that was a good おじぎ. Very sincere.  
**Hana**: すみません (sumimasen, “excuse me”), everybody! Come a bit closer? That’s how you ask nicely.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, D2.Civ.8.3-5, ask): why languages have polite forms (think question)  
> _Hana uses short words with friends and longer, polite words with grown-ups._  
> _Why do you think some languages have different polite forms?_  
> - To show respect. → That’s a good answer. A polite form is a way of showing respect with words, a bit like a deeper bow.
> - So people know how well you know each other. → That’s a thoughtful answer. The words you choose can show whether someone is a close friend or someone new.
> - English does it too, a bit. → Sharp thinking! English has “hi” and “good morning”, “thanks” and “thank you very much”. Many languages do this in their own way.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about why languages have polite and friendly ways of saying the same thing._

**Hana**: Who shall we start with?

**Luna**: Konbanwa. Did I say it right? Nova taught me last summer. I’ve been saving it.  
**Hana**: Perfect!  

> **Lesson** (literacy, notice): when は is read “wa”  
> **Hana**: Look at the last letter of こんばんは and こんにちは. It’s は. On its own, は says “ha”.  
> **Hana**: But at the end of these two greetings, it says “wa”. Japanese likes to surprise you.  
> _For grown-ups: Noticed that the last letter of こんにちは and こんばんは is は, read “wa” here, not “ha”._

**Fern**: I’m off to bed. Goodbye, all.  
**Hana**: Not さようなら (sayōnara)! That’s goodbye for a long time. You’ll see us in the morning.  
**Hana**: Say またね (mata ne, “see you”). Or at bedtime, おやすみ (oyasumi, “good night”).  
**Fern**: Mata ne, then. Oyasumi.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): choosing the right goodbye  
> _Fern will be back over the fence tomorrow morning. Which goodbye fits best tonight?_  
> - さようなら (sayōnara) → That one’s for a goodbye that lasts a long time. Fern will be back tomorrow, so おやすみ or またね is better.
> - ✅ おやすみ (oyasumi) → はい! She’s going to bed, so おやすみ. And またね works too: see you.
> - こんにちは (konnichiwa) → That’s hello! Fern’s leaving. At bedtime, say おやすみ.
> _For grown-ups: Chose between さようなら (goodbye for a long time), またね (see you) and おやすみ (good night) for the situation._

**Hana**: For grown-ups you want to be extra nice to, it’s おやすみなさい (oyasuminasai). The polite one.  
**Hana**: Now you. Everyone gets an oyasumi before bed. Nubbins, Nova, Dumpling, me. That’s the rule.

### Glossary

- **こんにちは (konnichiwa)**: Hello, or good afternoon.
- **おじぎ (ojigi)**: Bowing. People in Japan bow to greet, thank and say sorry; a deeper bow shows more respect.
- **はじめまして (hajimemashite)**: Nice to meet you. Only said the first time you meet someone.
- **わたしは ___ です (watashi wa ___ desu)**: I am ___. Put your name in the gap.
- **よろしくおねがいします (yoroshiku onegaishimasu)**: Please treat me kindly, or pleased to meet you. A polite thing to say after your name.
- **はい (hai)**: Yes.
- **いいえ (iie)**: No.
- **おはよう (ohayō)**: Good morning, to family and friends.
- **おはようございます (ohayō gozaimasu)**: Good morning, the polite way.
- **ありがとう (arigatō)**: Thank you.
- **ありがとうございます (arigatō gozaimasu)**: Thank you, the polite way.
- **ごめんなさい (gomen nasai)**: I’m sorry.
- **すみません (sumimasen)**: Excuse me, or sorry.
- **polite**: Showing good manners and respect to someone, often with special words.
- **こんばんは (konbanwa)**: Good evening.
- **さようなら (sayōnara)**: Goodbye, when you won’t see someone for a long time.
- **またね (mata ne)**: See you.
- **おやすみ (oyasumi)**: Good night, the friendly way.
- **おやすみなさい (oyasuminasai)**: Good night, the polite way.

**Notes:** Politeness notes stay within the brief: friendly forms for family and friends, polite forms for grown-ups and people you don’t know well. The bowing facts are the brief’s only. Day and night (lj1-s3-done) is a well-established fact.

## LJ2. The Hiragana Pond

- `lj2-step1`: Find あ い う え お on the stepping stones, in order, and trace each one. · _Start with あ, nearest the bank. Follow the numbered strokes on each stone._
- `lj2-step2`: Trace か き く け こ in the sand. · _Follow the numbered strokes, in order. Top to bottom, left to right, mostly._
- `lj2-step3`: Write はな, Hana’s name, on her sketchbook. · _は first, then な. You can write across or down._

#### `reader:lj2-s1` · [main](main.md#lj2-s1)

_Hana has been up since dawn, painting the flat stones in the pond. Marina watches, the way she watches anyone touching water._  
**Marina**: She asked you first. Mostly.  
**Hana**: These are hiragana. It’s one of the ways we write Japanese. There are forty-six basic ones.  
**Hana**: Today we need five: あ い う え お. You say them a, i, u, e, o. Like “ah, ee, oo, eh, oh”.  
**Hana**: They come first in the hiragana chart. Nearly every other hiragana ends in one of those five sounds.  
_She has hidden the five painted stones among plain ones. Some face up. Some are sideways._  
**Hana**: Find them in order, あ first. Step on the wrong one and you have to say “sorry, stone”.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, do): recognising the five vowel kana あ い う え お  
> **Hana**: Find あ, い, う, え and お on the stones, in that order. Say each sound as you step on it.  
> Check: `edu:find:ja:vowels`
> _For grown-ups: Found and read the five Japanese vowel letters あ い う え お (a, i, u, e, o), in order._

**Hana**: Then we spell words. うえ (ue) means “up”. いえ (ie) means “house”. あお (ao) means “blue”.  

> **Lesson** (literacy, ask): reading a two-letter word: うえ  
> **Hana**: Which two stones spell うえ, “up”?  
> - ✅ う, then え → はい! う, え. Ue. Up!
> - え, then う → Close! That spells え, う, which isn’t a word. Read it in order, left to right: う first, then え.
> - あ, then お → That spells あお: blue! Nice reading. But for うえ, step on う, then え.
> _For grown-ups: Read the word うえ (ue, “up”) letter by letter, in order._

**Marina**: And if somebody falls in?  
**Hana**: Nobody falls in. I’m very fast.  
_On the お stone sits a frog. It is fat, wet and not interested in hiragana at all._  
**Hana**: That’s fine. That is a normal frog and I am being normal about it.

> **Lesson** (literacy, notice): the three Japanese scripts  
> **Hana**: Hiragana is for Japanese words, like はな. Katakana is mostly for borrowed words, like ピンク.  
> **Hana**: And kanji carry meaning, like 花. Lots of Japanese sentences use all three at once.  
> _For grown-ups: Learned that Japanese uses three scripts: hiragana, katakana (mostly for borrowed words) and kanji (characters that carry meaning)._

**Hana**: Like ピンク (pinku). Say it.  
_You say it. It sounds a lot like “pink”._  
**Hana**: Exactly. Japanese borrowed it, and it came out a bit different.  

> **Lesson** (social-studies, L.3.4, ask): how words travel between languages (think question)  
> _Japanese borrowed “pink” and made it ピンク (pinku). English borrowed “koi” from Japanese._  
> _When a word moves into another language, what do you think changes, and what stays?_  
> - The sound changes a bit, but the meaning stays. → That’s a good answer. The word gets a new accent to fit its new home, and usually keeps its meaning. In Japanese, こい is any carp. In English, koi are the colourful pond kind.
> - It gets new letters to fit the new language. → That’s a fair answer. Pink got katakana letters. Koi got English letters. Same idea, new clothes.
> - Both languages end up sharing a little piece. → That’s a lovely answer. Borrowed words are like presents that languages give each other.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what happens to a word, like pink or koi, when it is borrowed into another language._

**Hana**: And は is sneaky. Mostly it says “ha”. But in わたしは, it says “wa”, because it points at who the sentence is about.  
**Hana**: Write はな on my sketchbook. Big. You can go across in a row, or down in a column. Japanese can do both.  

**Choices**

- **Write it across, left to right.**
    - You write は, then な, side by side across the page.

### Glossary

- **おぼん (Obon)**: A summer time in Japan when families remember relatives who have died.
- **とうろうながし (tōrō nagashi)**: Floating paper lanterns on a river, often at the end of Obon. Customs differ from place to place.
- **かわ (kawa)**: River. In kanji: 川.
- **ひかり (hikari)**: Light.
- **き (ki)**: Tree. In kanji: 木.
- **やま (yama)**: Mountain. In kanji: 山.
- **みず (mizu)**: Water.
- **あめ (ame)**: Rain.
- **ゆき (yuki)**: Snow.

**Notes:** Obon and tōrō nagashi are described only with the brief’s facts; no religious detail. The floating is Hana’s family’s custom for her ojiichan, which she invites the player to share: it is never a game, a count or a reward, and the counting lesson happens later, at the party, with Nubbins. Grief is plain and gentle. No lantern is made for Hazel, and nothing says whether she is alive.
