# Learning Grove · Little Sprouts (ages 3–6, read aloud): Hana's Hiragana, Japanese (LJ1–LJ6)

## LJ1. Konnichiwa, Hana!

- `lj1-step1`: Say hello to Hana at the gate. Say “konnichiwa”! · _Tap Hana. Say hello. A little bow is lovely too._
- `lj1-step2`: Teach three Nubbins to bow when you say “konnichiwa”. · _Tap a Nubbin and say “konnichiwa”. Say it a few times. Nubbins learn by copying._
- `lj1-step3`: Say “oyasumi” (good night) to everyone. · _In the evening, tap each friend and choose Say, then “oyasumi”._

#### `sprout:lj1-s1` · [main](main.md#lj1-s1)

_Bang! The gate swings open._  
_In runs a girl with a sketchbook. Nova follows, slowly._  
**Nova**: This is Hana. She raced me here!  
**Hana**: I won. By a lot.  
**Hana**: こんにちは (konnichiwa, “hello”)!  
_(Say it: kon-nee-chee-WAH. Your child says it back.)_  
_Hana bows. A quick little dip, then up again._  
**Hana**: はじめまして (hajimemashite, “nice to meet you”)!  
_(Say it: hah-jee-meh-MASH-teh. Say it slowly, together.)_  
**Hana**: We only say that the very first time we meet.  

> **Lesson** (sel, ELOF: Social and Emotional Development, do): greeting someone new with a bow  
> **Hana**: At home, we bow to say hello. And thank you. And sorry.  
> **Hana**: A deeper bow shows extra respect.  
> _(Stand up together. Bow and say: konnichiwa!)_  
> Check: `edu:bow:konnichiwa`
> _For grown-ups: Greeted Hana with konnichiwa (“hello”) and a small bow, and heard that in Japan people bow to greet, thank and say sorry._


**Choices**

- **Bow and say “konnichiwa”.**
    - You bow. Hana bows back, a bit lower.
    - **Hana**: Lower means I win. Probably.
- **Wave and say “konnichiwa”.**
    - **Hana**: A wave is friendly too!
    - **Hana**: Next time, add a tiny bow. Like this.
- **Say “hajimemashite” with a bow.**
    - **Hana**: Hajimemashite! The first-time word! Perfect!
    - **Hana**: Nova took three days to learn that.
    - **Nova**: Two days.

> _(Your child answers hai or iie. Both are fine!)_  
> Check: `edu:say:ja:hai-iie`
> _For grown-ups: Answered silly questions with hai (“yes”) and iie (“no”), nodding and shaking their head._


#### `sprout:lj1-s2` · [main](main.md#lj1-s2)

> **Lesson** (sel, CASEL: Relationship skills, ask): saying sorry after a bump  
> _Hana bumped Dumpling by mistake. What can she do?_  
> - ✅ Say sorry and help him up. → Yes! Gomen nasai, and a hand up. Better! Hana bows low. Dumpling copies, and lands on his face.
> - Run away. → Running away won’t help Dumpling. Say sorry, then help him up.
> - Pretend it didn’t happen. → Dumpling still feels bumped! Saying sorry helps him feel better.
> _For grown-ups: Thought about what to do after bumping someone by mistake: say sorry (gomen nasai) and help._

**Hana**: It’s morning, so we say ohayō.  
**Hana**: But Nubbins learn one word at a time. Konnichiwa first!

#### `sprout:lj1-s3` · [main](main.md#lj1-s3)

_Evening. The sky goes orange, then dark blue._  
_Luna comes up the path with a jar of moths._  
**Hana**: こんばんは (konbanwa, “good evening”), Luna!  
_(Say it: kom-bahn-WAH. Wave to Luna!)_  
**Luna**: Konbanwa! Nova taught me.  

> **Lesson** (science, ELOF: Scientific Reasoning, ask): times of day: morning and evening  
> _The sun is going down. Is it morning or evening?_  
> - ✅ Evening → Evening! The sun goes down in the evening. So we say konbanwa.
> - Morning → In the morning the sun comes up. Now it’s going down. That’s evening!
> _For grown-ups: Noticed that the sun goes down in the evening, when Hana says konbanwa (“good evening”)._

**Hana**: Bedtime word: おやすみ (oyasumi, “good night”).  
_(Say it: oh-yah-SOO-mee. Whisper it, soft and sleepy.)_  
**Hana**: Everybody gets an oyasumi. That’s the rule.  
_Dumpling is asleep already. In the herb bed._  

**Choices**

**Notes:** Sprout (ages 3–6): a grown-up reads aloud. Lines in brackets are prompts for the grown-up, not for the characters. Every Japanese word plays its native-speaker recording (JAPANESE_COURSE.vocab[].audio) when it first appears in a scene; never TTS. The “Say it” sound-alikes are the vetted ones from the course. Fonts: Noto Sans JP (or similar) for every line with kana, marked lang="ja"; for the big tracing shapes use a textbook-style (kyōkasho) font such as Klee One, whose strokes match handwriting. No bidi handling is needed. Tracing: show the kana big, draw its strokes one at a time, numbered, in school stroke order, then let the child trace with a finger. It is a shape to trace, never a reading test. Call-and-response: after a “Say it” prompt, pause so the child can repeat the word to a Nubbin; the Nubbin reacts (bows, waves, wiggles). No fail state. Words (sprout): konnichiwa, hajimemashite (s1); hai, iie (s1-done); ohayō, arigatō, gomen nasai (s2); konbanwa, oyasumi (s3); mata ne (s3-done). Polite forms, sumimasen, sayōnara, ojigi and watashi wa ___ desu / yoroshiku onegaishimasu are left to the reader band. Bowing: lj1-s1 is a do-lesson (edu:bow:konnichiwa). The Bowing Song is the base file’s original song, sung as written; the Nubbins bow on the last beat of each line. Shape to trace: こ (ko), 2 strokes, on the lantern sign.

**Notes:** Sprout (ages 3–6): a grown-up reads aloud. Lines in brackets are prompts for the grown-up, not for the characters. Every Japanese word plays its native-speaker recording (JAPANESE_COURSE.vocab[].audio) when it first appears in a scene; never TTS. The “Say it” sound-alikes are the vetted ones from the course. Fonts: Noto Sans JP (or similar) for every line with kana, marked lang="ja"; for the big tracing shapes use a textbook-style (kyōkasho) font such as Klee One, whose strokes match handwriting. No bidi handling is needed. Tracing: show the kana big, draw its strokes one at a time, numbered, in school stroke order, then let the child trace with a finger. It is a shape to trace, never a reading test. Call-and-response: after a “Say it” prompt, pause so the child can repeat the word to a Nubbin; the Nubbin reacts (bows, waves, wiggles). No fail state. Words (sprout): kawa, hikari (s1); yama, yuki (s1-done); ki, mizu (s2); ame (s3); then the review party (s3-done and complete) brings back every sprout word with its sound-alike. Culture, sprout version: Obon and tōrō nagashi are described in English only (“a special time in summer when families remember the people in their family who have died”; “my family floats a paper lantern down the river for my grandpa”); the names are reader-band words. No religious detail, no spirits. The floating is Hana’s family’s custom, which she invites the child to share: never a game, a count or a reward. The counting lesson is later, at the party, with Nubbins. Grief: Hana’s grandpa died; this is said plainly (“his body stopped working. It won’t start again”), never as sleep or going away, and the lantern is a way to remember, not something that carries him anywhere. No oyasumi or nemui near the lantern scene. No lantern is made for Hazel, and nothing says whether she is alive. Lanterns: Luna lights them; children never handle the flame. Shape to trace: き (ki), 4 strokes in school order.
